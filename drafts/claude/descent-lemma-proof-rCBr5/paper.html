Abstract

This paper presents a mathematical framework for the Quantum Observable Universe (QOU). We introduce the notion of quantum torsion in this setting, study its lifting to higher categorical structures, and examine the resulting interpretational and experimental consequences. Speculative extensions are discussed in the final chapter.

Quantum Observable Universe

📄

Definition 1.1 (Quantum Universe). Let \(k\) be a field. A quantum universe over \(k\) is a quadruple \((\mathcal{C}, A, \omega, q)\) where:

  1. \((\mathcal{C}, \otimes, \mathbf{1})\) is a \(k\)-linear rigid monoidal category;

  2. \(A\) is a Frobenius–Hopf object in \(\mathcal{C}\): an object equipped with a Hopf structure \((\mu, \eta, \Delta, \varepsilon, S)\) and a Frobenius form \(\lambda \colon A \to \mathbf{1}\) such that the pairing \(A \otimes A \xrightarrow{\mu} A \xrightarrow{\lambda} \mathbf{1}\) is non-degenerate and the Frobenius relation \((\mu \otimes \mathrm{id}) \circ (\mathrm{id} \otimes \Delta_\mu) = \Delta_\mu \circ \mu\) holds, where \(\Delta_\mu\) is the comultiplication dual to \(\mu\) under the pairing;

  3. \(\omega \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathrm{Vect}_k\) is a fibre functor (exact faithful \(k\)-linear monoidal functor);

  4. \(q \in k^\times\) is a deformation parameter (invertibility of \(q\) is required because the Hopf-algebra relations defining \(A\) involve \(q^{-1}\); at \(q = 0\) the antipode \(S\) degenerates and the Hopf structure collapses); the Hopf structure on \(A\) depends on \(q\), and \(q = 1\) recovers the classical (cocommutative) case.

The quantum gauge group is the affine \(k\)-group scheme \(G_q = \mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(\omega)\), reconstructed via Tannaka–Krein duality.

📄

Definition 1.2 (Quantum Observable Universe). A quantum universe \((\mathcal{C}, A, \omega, q)\) over \(k\) (Definition 1.1) is observable if:

  1. the Frobenius form \(\lambda\) on \(A\) is non-degenerate; and

  2. \(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{1},\, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\), where \(\mathrm{Ad}(A)\) is the adjoint object (\(A\) with the conjugation coaction \(a \mapsto \textstyle\sum a_{(1)}\, b\, S(a_{(2)})\)).

📄

Remark 1.3 (Base-field independence). Definitions 1.1 and 1.2 are formulated over an arbitrary field \(k\). The Frobenius–Hopf axioms replace the analytic vanishing conditions of the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation (Example 1.6) with a single algebraic non-degeneracy condition on the Frobenius form. Concrete realisations include:

  • \(k = \mathbb{C}\): the classical setting of CR geometry and contact manifolds (Example 1.4);

  • \(k = \mathbb{Q}_\mathfrak{p}\) (the \(\mathfrak{p}\)-adic field): the gauge datum becomes an \(F\)-isocrystal with Frobenius lift, and integrability is controlled by the \(\mathfrak{p}\)-adic Frobenius endomorphism (Example 7.1, Chapter 7);

  • \(k = \mathbb{F}_\mathfrak{p}\) (a finite field): the monoidal category is the category of finite-dimensional representations of a quantum group over \(\mathbb{F}_\mathfrak{p}\) (Example 7.2).

The fibre functor \(\omega\) ensures that \(G_q = \mathrm{Aut}^{\otimes}(\omega)\) is well-defined over any \(k\).

📄

Example 1.4 (\(\mathbb{C}\) realisation: quantum universe). Over \(k = \mathbb{C}\), a quantum universe (Definition 1.1) is realised by a \(5\)-tuple \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) where:

  1. \(M\) is a CR manifold of hypersurface type (real dimension \(2n+1\), \(n \geq 1\)) with non-degenerate Levi form of signature \((r,s)\), \(r + s = n\);

  2. \(\theta\) is a pseudo-Hermitian structure (contact form) on \(M\), determining the Levi distribution \(H = \ker\theta\), the Reeb vector field \(\xi\), the Webster metric \(g_\theta\) (of signature \((2r,2s)\) on \(H\)), and the Levi form \(L_\theta\);

  3. \(G = \mathrm{SU}(2)\);

  4. \(P \to M\) is a principal \(G\)-bundle whose holonomy reduces to \(\mathrm{SO}(3) \subset \mathrm{SU}(2)/\{\pm I\}\);

  5. \(E = P \times_G \mathbb{CP}^1 \to M\) is the associated state bundle, where \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) acts on \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) by Möbius transformations.

The monoidal category is \(\mathcal{C} = \mathrm{Rep}(\mathrm{SU}_{q(p)}(2))\) (the representation category of the quantum group at the substrate parameter \(q(p)\), Definition 1.10), the Frobenius–Hopf object is \(A = \mathcal{O}_q(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) with Frobenius form given by the Haar state, and the fibre functor \(\omega\) is the forgetful functor to \(\mathrm{Vect}_{\mathbb{C}}\).

📄

Remark 1.5 (Notation and special cases). When \(\theta\) is understood from context, we abbreviate the \(5\)-tuple to \((M, G, P, E)\). The strictly pseudoconvex case corresponds to signature \((n,0)\), where \(L_\theta\) is positive definite.

Example 1.6 (\(\mathbb{C}\) realisation: quantum observable universe). A \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe is a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum universe \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) (Example 1.4) that satisfies the vanishing conditions \[H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Gamma_c(M, E) = 0.\] These analytic conditions are the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation of the categorical observability conditions of Definition 1.2: the Frobenius non-degeneracy of the Haar state on \(\mathcal{O}_q(\mathrm{SU}(2))\), combined with irreducibility of the instanton connection (Lemma 1.8), forces \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\); the section-vanishing \(\Gamma_c(M, E) = 0\) follows from the non-existence of \(A\)-equivariant sections of the associated \(\mathbb{CP}^1\)-bundle.

Definition 1.7 (Instanton connection). Let \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum universe with \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H = 4\) (\(n = 2\)). An instanton connection on \(P\) is a connection \(A\) whose curvature \(F_A\), restricted to the horizontal distribution \(H = \ker\theta\), is self-dual: \(F_A|_H = {*_H}\,F_A|_H\), where \(*_H\) is the Hodge star on \(\Lambda^2 H^*\) determined by the Webster metric \(g_\theta\). More generally, when \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H \neq 4\), an instanton connection is a Yang–Mills connection that minimises the functional \(\int_M \lVert F_A|_H \rVert^2\,\theta \wedge (d\theta)^n\).

📄

Lemma 1.8 (Irreducibility of the connection). Let \((M, G, P, E)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe. Then the vanishing condition \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\) implies that every instanton connection \(A\) on \(P\) (Definition 1.7) is irreducible.

Proof. Suppose for contradiction that an instanton connection \(A\) on \(P\) is reducible. Then the stabiliser of \(A\) under the gauge group contains a non-trivial element \(s \in \Gamma(M,\, \mathrm{Ad}(P))\) satisfying \(D_A s = 0\). Such \(s\) is a global parallel section of \(\mathrm{Ad}(P)\), and hence a non-zero element of \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P))\), contradicting \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\). Hence \(A\) is irreducible. ◻

Proposition 1.9 (Self-duality of the curvature). Let \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe with \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} M = 5\) (i.e. the CR dimension \(n = 2\), so that the Levi distribution \(H = \ker\theta\) has \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H = 4\)). Let \(A\) be an instanton connection on \(P\). Then the curvature \(F_A\), restricted to \(H\), is self-dual with respect to the Hodge star \(*_H\) determined by the Webster metric \(g_\theta\) and the complex structure \(J\): \[F_A\big|_H \;=\; {*_H}\, F_A\big|_H.\]

Proof. The contact form \(\theta\) (Example 1.4) splits the tangent bundle as \(TM = H \oplus \mathbb{R}\xi\), where \(H = \ker\theta\) has real dimension \(4\). The curvature \(F_A\) is an \(\mathrm{Ad}(P)\)-valued \(2\)-form on \(M\). By the Reeb decomposition (Definition 1.18), \(F_A\) splits into horizontal and vertical parts; we consider \(F_A|_H \in \Gamma(\Lambda^2 H^* \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(P))\).

Since \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H = 4\), the operator \(*_H\) maps \(2\)-forms to \(2\)-forms and satisfies \(*_H^2 = \mathrm{id}\) on \(\Lambda^2 H^*\). This decomposes \(\Lambda^2 H^* = \Lambda^+\! H^* \oplus \Lambda^-\! H^*\) into self-dual and anti-self-dual parts.

An instanton connection \(A\) minimises the Yang–Mills functional. By the Bogomolny argument, the integrated norm satisfies \[\int_M \lVert F_A|_H \rVert^2 \,\theta \wedge (d\theta)^2 \;=\; 2\!\int_M \lVert F_A^-|_H \rVert^2 \,\theta \wedge (d\theta)^2 + \int_M \mathrm{tr}(F_A|_H \wedge F_A|_H) \wedge \theta,\] where the topological term \(\int_M \mathrm{tr}(F_A|_H \wedge F_A|_H) \wedge \theta\) is fixed by the characteristic class of \(P\). Minimising the functional forces \(F_A^-|_H = 0\), which is precisely the self-duality condition \(F_A|_H = {*_H}\,F_A|_H\). ◻

Definition 1.10 (Substrate parameter). Let \((\mathcal{C}, A, \omega, q)\) be a quantum universe over a field \(k\) (Definition 1.1). Let \(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}_q(\mathfrak{su}(2))\) denote the Lusztig integral form : the \(\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]\)-subalgebra of \(\mathcal{U}_q(\mathfrak{su}(2))\) generated by \(K^{\pm 1}\) and the divided powers \(E^{(n)} = E^n/[n]_q!\), \(F^{(n)} = F^n/[n]_q!\). The substrate parameter is a ring homomorphism \[\iota \;\colon\; \mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}] \;\longrightarrow\; k, \qquad q \;\longmapsto\; q_0 \in k^{\times},\] and the base-changed algebra \(\mathcal{U}^{\mathbb{Z}}_q(\mathfrak{su}(2)) \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}]} k\) is the quantised enveloping algebra at the specialised value \(q_0\). When the quantum universe is fibred over a space \(M\) (as in the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation, Example 1.4), \(q_0\) may vary from point to point, yielding a substrate field \(q \colon M \to k^{\times}\).

Remark 1.11 (\(\mathbb{C}\) substrate via the Levi form). In the \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe (Example 1.6), let \(A\) be an instanton connection on \(P\) (Definition 1.7), \(F_A^+\) its self-dual curvature (Proposition 1.9), and \(L_\theta \colon H \times H \to \mathbb{C}\) the Levi form on \(H = \ker\theta\), defined by \(L_\theta(V, W) = -i\,d\theta(V, \bar{W})\). The specialisation map of Definition 1.10 sends \[q \;\longmapsto\; q(p) \;=\; 1 + \lvert\langle L_\theta,\, F_A^+ \rangle\rvert(p), \qquad p \in M,\] where \(\langle L_\theta, F_A^+\rangle\) is the natural contraction (Remark 1.12) and \(\lvert\cdot\rvert\) denotes the absolute value. This defines a smooth field \(q \colon M \to [1,\infty) \subset \mathbb{C}^{\times}\) with \(q(p) = 1\) at points where \(F_A^+ = 0\) (classicality) and \(q(p) \to \infty\) in the deep-quantum regime.

Relation to the exponential. Over \(\mathbb{Q}\) (or any subfield of \(\mathbb{C}\)), the classical exponential \(\exp(\lvert\langle L_\theta, F_A^+\rangle\rvert)\) is an equally valid specialisation map, and the two are related by the Taylor expansion \(\exp(x) = 1 + x + \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + \cdots\): the formula \(q(p) = 1 + \lvert\langle L_\theta, F_A^+\rangle\rvert(p)\) is the first-order approximation of \(\exp\!\bigl(\lvert\langle L_\theta, F_A^+\rangle\rvert(p)\bigr)\). Both define smooth fields \(M \to [1,\infty)\) with the same boundary behaviour (\(q = 1\) at classicality, \(q \to \infty\) deep-quantum), and all downstream formulas (\(\hbar_q = 1 - q^{-1}\), the Heisenberg commutator, the Schrödinger equation, the Yang–Mills functional) are algebraic in \(q\) and therefore insensitive to the choice between \(1+x\) and \(\exp(x)\). The algebraic form is adopted in Definition 1.10 because it is meaningful over every base field \(k\), whereas \(\exp(x)\) requires an archimedean topology.

The non-archimedean analogues are developed in Chapter 7: over \(\mathbb{Q}_\mathfrak{p}\) the Levi-form contraction is replaced by the Frobenius endomorphism and \(q = \mathfrak{p}^{\,-v_\mathfrak{p}(\det\varphi)}\) (Example 7.1); over \(\mathbb{F}_\mathfrak{p}\), \(q\) is a primitive root of unity (Example 7.2).

Remark 1.12 (Natural contraction and field character of \(q\)). The Levi form \(L_\theta\) is a section of \(\Lambda^{1,1} H^*\) (the \((1,1)\)-component of \(\Lambda^2 H^*\) with respect to the complex structure \(J\)). The self-dual curvature \(F_A^+\) is a section of \(\Lambda^+ H^* \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(P)\). On a \(4\)-dimensional oriented inner-product space, \(\Lambda^{1,1} \subset \Lambda^+\), so \(L_\theta\) and \(F_A^+\) live in the same subbundle. The Webster metric \(g_\theta\) provides a fibrewise inner product on \(\Lambda^+ H^*\); composing this with the Killing form on \(\mathrm{Ad}(P)\) yields the natural contraction \[\langle L_\theta,\, F_A^+ \rangle_p \;\in\; \mathbb{R}, \qquad p \in M.\] The substrate parameter is not a fixed constant but a field on \(M\): its value varies from point to point according to the local coupling between the Levi form and the self-dual curvature.

The quantum Planck constant is the smooth field \(\hbar_q \colon M \to [0,1)\) defined by \[\hbar_q(p) \;=\; 1 - q(p)^{-1}, \qquad p \in M.\]

Remark 1.15 (Limiting regimes of \(\hbar_q\)). Like \(q\) itself, \(\hbar_q\) is not a universal constant but a field on \(M\). As \(q(p) \to 1\) (classicality), \(\hbar_q(p) \to 0\) and quantum effects vanish; as \(q(p) \to \infty\) (deep-quantum regime), \(\hbar_q(p) \to 1\).

Remark 1.16 (Heisenberg uncertainty as commutator). In the \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe the Heisenberg uncertainty principle is expressed through the \(q\)-deformed commutator. Let \(\hat{X}\) and \(\hat{P}\) denote position and momentum observables on the fibre \(\mathbb{CP}^1\). Let \(J \colon H \to H\) be the complex structure operator on the Levi distribution (\(J^2 = -\mathrm{Id}\)); throughout this paper every occurrence of the imaginary unit \(i\) in a physical equation is identified with the geometric action of \(J\) on horizontal planes. We define their \(q\)-dependent commutator at \(p \in M\) by \[[\hat{X},\, \hat{P}]_q \;:=\; J\,\hbar_q(p),\] where \(\hbar_q(p) = 1 - q(p)^{-1}\) (Definition 1.14). In this remark the subscript \(q\) serves only to record the dependence of the commutator on the substrate field \(q\) and should not be confused with any \(q\)-deformed Lie bracket \([\cdot,\cdot]_q\) on vector fields that may appear elsewhere. The commutator encodes the local uncertainty: when \(q(p) \to 1\) (classicality), \([\hat{X}, \hat{P}]_q \to 0\) and observables commute; when \(q(p) \to \infty\) (deep-quantum regime), \([\hat{X}, \hat{P}]_q \to J\) and maximal uncertainty prevails.

Analogy with the Lie bracket. The position–momentum commutator \([\hat{X},\hat{P}]_q\) is the quantum-mechanical counterpart of the Lie bracket \([e_a,e_b]=\epsilon_{abc}\,e_c\) in \(\mathfrak{su}(2)\) (Definition 3.3). Both commutators measure the first-order non-commutativity of their respective algebras (Remark 3.4). In the braid-group setting, this non-commutativity appears as the defining relation \(\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1=\sigma_2\sigma_1\sigma_2\) (Definition 3.1); on the Bring surface, it is realised by the \(S_3\) permutation action on the spectral resolvents (Remark 5.5).

Commutator as Reeb vector. The commutator \([\hat{X},\hat{P}]_q\) may be identified with the value of the \(q\)-deformed Reeb vector field \(\xi_q\) (Definition 1.26) evaluated on the contact form. Concretely, the Reeb field is the unique direction along which the symplectic pairing \(d\theta\) degenerates, so \(\theta(\xi_q)\) encodes precisely the obstruction to commutativity of canonical coordinates. On the exceptional divisor \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) produced by blowing up a spacetime defect on the Bring surface (Remark 5.20), the Reeb vector field generates the \(U(1)\) fibration of the Hopf bundle \(S^3\to\mathbb{CP}^1\), and its “strength” is set by \(\hbar_q(p)\).

Definition 1.17 (\(q\)-Harmonic form (Particle)). Let \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe with substrate parameter \(q\) (Definition 1.10). Let \(d_q\) denote the \(q\)-deformed exterior derivative (Definition 1.23 below) and let \(\delta_q\) denote its formal adjoint with respect to the inner product on \(\Gamma(M,\, \Lambda^k T^*M \otimes J^1 E)\) induced by the Webster metric \(g_\theta\). A particle is a section \(\alpha \in \Gamma(M,\, \Lambda^k T^*M \otimes J^1 E)\) that is \(q\)-harmonic, i.e. lies in the kernel of the \(q\)-Laplacian: \[\Delta_q \alpha \;=\; (d_q\,\delta_q + \delta_q\,d_q)\,\alpha \;=\; 0.\]

Definition 1.18 (Reeb decomposition). Let \((M, \theta)\) be a CR manifold with non-degenerate Levi form (Example 1.4) and let \(\xi\) denote the associated Reeb vector field, characterised by \(\iota_\xi\,d\theta = 0\) and \(\theta(\xi) = 1\). Every differential \(k\)-form \(\alpha\) (\(k \geq 1\)) on \(M\) decomposes uniquely as \[\alpha \;=\; \alpha_H + \theta \wedge \iota_\xi\,\alpha,\] where \(\alpha_H := \alpha - \theta \wedge \iota_\xi\,\alpha\) is the horizontal component (satisfying \(\iota_\xi\,\alpha_H = 0\)) and \(\alpha_V := \theta \wedge \iota_\xi\,\alpha\) is the vertical component. A form is purely horizontal if and only if \(\iota_\xi\,\alpha = 0\).

📄

Definition 1.19 (Fermion). A fermion is a \(q\)-harmonic form (Definition 1.17) that has a non-trivial vertical component in the Reeb decomposition (Definition 1.18): \[\Delta_q\,\alpha = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \iota_\xi\,\alpha_V \neq 0.\]

📄

Definition 1.20 (Boson). A boson is a \(q\)-harmonic form (Definition 1.17) that is purely horizontal in the Reeb decomposition (Definition 1.18): \[\Delta_q\,\alpha = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \alpha = \alpha_H \quad(\text{equivalently, } \iota_\xi\,\alpha = 0 \text{ by Definition~\ref{def:reeb-decomposition}}).\]

Definition 1.21 (Observation). Let \((\mathcal{C}, A, \omega, q)\) be a quantum observable universe over a field \(k\) (Definition 1.2). An observation is a morphism \[\mathcal{O} \;\colon\; A \;\longrightarrow\; A \qquad\text{in } \mathcal{C},\] satisfying:

  1. Frobenius compatibility. \(\lambda \circ \mathcal{O} = \lambda\) (the observation preserves the Frobenius form);

  2. Substrate perturbation. the induced map on the deformation parameter shifts \(q \mapsto q' = q + \delta q\), where \(\delta q\) is determined by the Hopf-algebra coaction of \(A\) on itself: \[\delta q \;=\; \lambda\bigl(\mathcal{O}(a_{(1)})\,S(a_{(2)})\bigr) \quad\text{for } a \in A,\] with \(S\) the antipode and \(\Delta(a) = a_{(1)} \otimes a_{(2)}\) the coproduct.

In the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation (Example 1.6), an observation is realised as a continuous open map from the spacetime base \(M\) to the bundle of endomorphisms \(\operatorname{End}(E) \to M\), encoding the coordinate change in torsion energy of the bundle \(E\). Because the substrate parameter \(q(p)\) (Definition 1.10) is perturbed by condition (2), every observation shifts the non-commutative geometry:

“Observing the universe changes it.”

Remark 1.22 (Two-observer parallelogram). Let \((\mathcal{C}, A, \omega, q)\) be a quantum observable universe and let \(\alpha\) be a fermion (Definition 1.19) — a \(q\)-harmonic form with non-trivial vertical component \(\iota_\xi\,\alpha_V \neq 0\). Consider two independent observations \(\mathcal{O}_1, \mathcal{O}_2 \colon A \to A\) (Definition 1.21) of the same event. These data assemble into a parallelogram of observations: \[\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=2.5em, column sep=3.5em] & \mathcal{O}_1(\alpha) \arrow[dr, "\text{compare}"] & \\ \alpha \arrow[ur, "\mathcal{O}_1"] \arrow[dr, "\mathcal{O}_2"'] & & \delta\alpha \\ & \mathcal{O}_2(\alpha) \arrow[ur, "\text{compare}"'] & \end{tikzcd}\] The fermion \(\alpha\) is the source vertex: it is the physical state prior to measurement, carrying a non-trivial vertical (Reeb) component that couples it to the spin direction. From the source, the two observers independently apply their observation morphisms, producing \(\mathcal{O}_1(\alpha)\) and \(\mathcal{O}_2(\alpha)\) at the lateral vertices of the parallelogram. The two observed values are then compared at the far vertex, yielding the discrepancy \[\delta\alpha \;=\; \mathcal{O}_1(\alpha) - \mathcal{O}_2(\alpha).\]

Relation to uncertainty. Each observation perturbs the substrate parameter by \(\delta q_i\) (Definition 1.21, condition (2)), so the two observers induce distinct shifted parameters \(q_1' = q + \delta q_1\) and \(q_2' = q + \delta q_2\). The discrepancy \(\delta\alpha\) is controlled by the difference in these perturbations. In the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation, the Heisenberg commutator (Remark 1.16) gives \[\lVert \delta\alpha \rVert \;\geq\; \bigl\lvert \hbar_{q_1'}(p) - \hbar_{q_2'}(p) \bigr\rvert \;=\; \bigl\lvert q_1'(p)^{-1} - q_2'(p)^{-1} \bigr\rvert,\] where \(\hbar_q = 1 - q^{-1}\) (Definition 1.14). Thus the observed discrepancy at the far vertex of the parallelogram is bounded below by the difference of the local Planck constants induced by the two observations.

The parallelogram is degenerate (i.e. \(\delta\alpha = 0\)) only when both observations perturb the substrate identically (\(\delta q_1 = \delta q_2\)), which requires the two observation morphisms to agree on the adjoint coaction of \(A\). Generically, the non-commutativity of the Hopf structure prevents this, and the parallelogram opens: independent observations of a fermion necessarily disagree, with the magnitude of disagreement governed by the uncertainty encoded in the substrate field.

Definition 1.26 (\(q\)-Deformed Reeb vector field). Definition 1.27 (\(q\)-Deformed Reeb flow). Let \(\xi_q\) be the \(q\)-deformed Reeb vector field (Definition 1.26). The \(q\)-deformed Reeb flow is the one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms \(\phi_t^q \colon M \to M\), \(t \in \mathbb{R}\), generated by \(\xi_q\): \[\frac{d}{dt}\,\phi_t^q \;=\; \xi_q \circ \phi_t^q, \qquad \phi_0^q = \mathrm{id}_M.\]

Definition 1.28 (\(q\)-Torsion leaf). Let \(H = \ker\theta \subset TM\) be the horizontal distribution on the CR manifold \(M\). A \(q\)-torsion leaf is a maximal integral submanifold \(L \hookrightarrow M\) of \(H\) that is invariant under the \(q\)-deformed Reeb flow (Definition 1.27): \[T_p L \;\subset\; H_p \quad\text{for all } p \in L, \qquad\text{and}\qquad \phi_t^q(L) = L \quad\text{for all } t \in \mathbb{R}.\]

Remark 1.29 (\(q\)-Torsion leaves: existence and Frobenius obstruction). The horizontal distribution \(H = \ker\theta\) on a contact manifold is maximally non-integrable: \(d\theta|_{H \times H}\) is non-degenerate. By the Frobenius theorem, maximal integral submanifolds of \(H\) have dimension at most \(n\) (half the rank of \(H\)); these are the Legendrian submanifolds. Definition 1.28 therefore requires \(L\) to be a Legendrian submanifold invariant under the \(q\)-deformed Reeb flow.

Existence is not automatic: it depends on the dynamics of \(\xi_q\). When \(q \to 1\) and \(\xi_q \to \xi\), invariant Legendrian submanifolds exist whenever the Reeb flow is periodic or integrable (e.g. on \(S^3/\Gamma\)). For \(q > 1\), the deformation generically breaks periodicity, and one must appeal to KAM-type persistence results.

In the category of Lie algebroids over \(M\), the Frobenius integrability condition is equivalent to \(H\) carrying a Frobenius structure (Definition 1.1). Since the Levi form \(d\theta|_{H \times H}\) is non-degenerate, this Frobenius structure fails — and it is precisely this failure that forces the dimension bound on integral submanifolds and gives \(q\)-torsion leaves their rigidity.

Remark 1.30 (Physical interpretation of the Reeb decomposition). The Reeb vector field \(\xi\) singles out the direction of internal spin on the CR manifold. Forms that “see” this direction (fermions) carry half-integer spin; forms confined to the horizontal distribution (bosons) do not couple to the Reeb flow and carry integer spin. The decomposition \(\alpha = \alpha_H + \alpha_V\) is therefore the geometric origin of the spin–statistics distinction in this framework. Experimental confirmation of this picture, including photonic quantum Hall measurements and many-body interference in crystal lattices, is discussed in Chapter [chap:experimental].

Remark 1.31 (General Relativity as the “Substrate Geometry”). In this model the metric is effectively replaced by the Levi form \(L_\theta\) and its coupling to the gauge curvature \(F_A\). Variation with respect to the Levi form \(L_\theta\) yields field equations of the schematic form \[\frac{\delta S_q}{\delta L_\theta} \;=\; 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathrm{Ric}_{g_\theta} \;=\; T^{(q)},\] where \(\mathrm{Ric}_{g_\theta}\) is the Webster Ricci curvature and \(T^{(q)}\) is a \(q\)-weighted stress-energy \(2\)-tensor on \(H\) (both coordinate-free objects). Remark 2.1 (TQFT context). The axioms of Definitions 1.1 and 1.2 are grounded in the algebraic framework of topological quantum field theory (TQFT).

Frobenius–Hopf objects and 2d TQFT. A commutative Frobenius algebra over \(k\) is equivalent to a \(2\)-dimensional TQFT \(Z \colon \mathbf{2Cob} \to \mathrm{Vect}_k\) . The Frobenius–Hopf object \(A\) in Definition 1.1 carries both the Frobenius structure (encoding the \(2\)d cobordism data: the product \(\mu\) corresponds to a pair-of-pants cobordism, the Frobenius form \(\lambda\) to a cap) and a Hopf structure (encoding the quantum-group symmetry that extends the theory to three dimensions). Concretely, a \(2\)d TQFT assigns to the circle \(S^1\) a Frobenius algebra \(A\) and to each cobordism \(\Sigma \colon S^1 \sqcup \cdots \to S^1 \sqcup \cdots\) a linear map built from \(\mu\), \(\Delta_\mu\), \(\eta\), and \(\lambda\). The Hopf structure \((\Delta, \varepsilon, S)\) equips \(A\) with the additional data needed for three-dimensional and extended TQFTs .

The observability condition as a categorical contact condition. In the TQFT setting, the condition \(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\) (Definition 1.2) is the categorical analogue of the contact condition (maximal non-integrability). A non-zero morphism \(\mathbf{1} \to \mathrm{Ad}(A)\) would give a global invariant section of the adjoint object — the categorical counterpart of a nowhere-vanishing section of the adjoint bundle — and would force the TQFT to decompose into a direct sum of lower-dimensional theories. Requiring \(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\) therefore ensures:

  1. irreducibility: the associated \(3\)-dimensional TQFT (Chern–Simons type, with gauge datum \(A\) and quantum parameter \(q\)) does not split as a direct sum;

  2. non-degeneracy across cobordisms: the TQFT pairing on state spaces assigned to surfaces is non-degenerate ;

  3. full dualisability: in the sense of the cobordism hypothesis , the object \(A\) is fully dualisable in the relevant Morita higher category, so the TQFT extends to a fully local (extended) field theory.

In the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation (Example 1.6), this categorical condition reduces to \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\) — the classical requirement that the instanton connection admits no infinitesimal automorphisms, which is the gauge-theoretic manifestation of the contact (maximally non-integrable) geometry of \((M, \theta)\).

Remark 2.2 (Vanishing conditions: explication). In Example 1.6, \(\mathrm{Ad}(P) = P \times_G \mathfrak{su}(2)\) is the adjoint bundle (a linear bundle, so sheaf cohomology is defined), and \(\Gamma_c(M,E)\) denotes the space of compactly supported continuous sections of the (non-linear) fibre bundle \(E\). The condition \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\) asserts the vanishing of all global parallel sections of the adjoint bundle — not merely compactly supported ones — eliminating every infinitesimal gauge symmetry.

Remark 2.3 (Generalisation beyond strict pseudoconvexity). Example 1.4 requires only Levi non-degeneracy (not strict pseudoconvexity). This generality is deliberate: the QOU framework applies whenever the following three cohomological/obstruction conditions hold.

  1. Kohn–Rossi finite-dimensionality. The tangential CR complex \((\Omega^{0,\bullet}_b(M),\, \bar\partial_b)\) has finite-dimensional cohomology \(H^{0,k}_{\bar\partial_b}(M)\) for all \(k\). This replaces strict pseudoconvexity in ensuring analytic control over \(q\)-harmonic forms (Definition 1.17). In the strictly pseudoconvex case, Kohn’s sub-elliptic estimates guarantee this automatically; for indefinite Levi signature, one imposes it as a hypothesis.

  2. Non-degeneracy of the substrate. The Levi form \(L_\theta\) is non-degenerate (but not necessarily positive definite), so the substrate parameter \(q(p) = 1 + \lvert\langle L_\theta, F_A^+\rangle\rvert\) (Remark 1.11) is well-defined and the regime \(q > 1\) is attainable. When the Levi form has indefinite signature \((r,s)\) with \(r,s \geq 1\), the Webster metric \(g_\theta\) is pseudo-Riemannian; the contraction \(\langle L_\theta, F_A^+\rangle\) remains well-defined (using the pseudo-Riemannian inner product on \(\Lambda^+ H^*\)), and one takes the absolute value to ensure \(q \geq 1\).

  3. QOU vanishing (Example 1.6). The conditions \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\) and \(\Gamma_c(M,E) = 0\) serve as the gauge-theoretic obstructions. These are purely topological/cohomological and independent of the Levi signature.

Together, conditions (1)–(3) ensure that all results of this chapter (irreducibility of the connection, self-duality of the curvature, substrate dynamics, \(q\)-deformed operators) extend to CR manifolds of arbitrary Levi signature. The strictly pseudoconvex case (signature \((n,0)\)) is the simplest and motivates all examples in this chapter; the indefinite case arises naturally for real hypersurfaces in indefinite Kähler manifolds and for boundaries of Grauert tubes over pseudo-Riemannian manifolds.

Remark 2.4 (Jet-bundle formulation). The conditions in Example 1.6 admit a natural reformulation in terms of the first jet bundle \(J^1 E\). The vanishing \(\Gamma_c(M, E) = 0\) ensures that no non-trivial compactly supported section of \(E\) exists, while \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\) eliminates all infinitesimal gauge symmetries (not merely compactly supported ones). Together, these conditions mean that the space of sections of \(J^1 E\) injects into the space of connections on \(P\), preventing any local degeneracy of the gauge field.

Example 2.5 (Real hypersurface in a Calabi–Yau \(2\)-fold). Let \(X\) be a Calabi–Yau \(2\)-fold (K3 surface) with holonomy \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), and let \(M \subset X\) be a smooth, strictly convex real hypersurface. Then \(M\) is a \(3\)-dimensional strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold inheriting its CR structure from the ambient Kähler geometry. Because the holonomy of \(X\) is \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), the frame bundle of \(X\) restricted to \(M\) yields a natural principal \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)-bundle \(P \to M\). The associated state bundle \(E = P \times_G \mathbb{CP}^1\) yields a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) when the vanishing conditions of Example 1.6 are satisfied. (For higher-dimensional Calabi–Yau manifolds with holonomy \(\mathrm{SU}(n)\), \(n > 2\), a reduction to \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) must be supplied separately.)

Example 2.6 (ALE links). Let \(\Gamma \subset \mathrm{SU}(2)\) be a finite subgroup and let \(X\) be the minimal resolution of \(\mathbb{C}^2 / \Gamma\). The link \(M = S^3 / \Gamma\) is the boundary of a tubular neighbourhood of the exceptional set in \(X\); it is a compact \(3\)-dimensional strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold. The CR structure of the boundary induces a contact form \(\theta\) on \(M\) whose Reeb vector field \(\xi\) generates the Hopf-type fibration \(S^3/\Gamma \to S^2\). (When \(\Gamma \neq \{1\}\), the target is the orbifold \(\mathbb{CP}^1/\Gamma\); the bundle structure remains well-defined on the smooth manifold \(M = S^3/\Gamma\).) Equipped with the induced principal \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)-bundle \(P \to M\) and the associated state bundle \(E = P \times_G \mathbb{CP}^1\), the tuple \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) provides a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe. H^*\) is trivial (\(\Lambda^2 H^* \cong \mathbb{R}\)); in this dimension the curvature \(F_A|_H\) is automatically “self-dual” in the vacuous sense, and the instanton condition reduces to the Yang–Mills equation (Definition 1.7).

Example 2.7 (Bring’s surface unit circle bundle). Let \(S\) denote Bring’s surface (Definition 5.2), the genus-\(4\) Riemann surface in \(\mathbb{P}^4\) cut out by the first three power sums \(\sum x_j^k = 0\), \(k=1,2,3\). Being a Riemann surface (real dimension \(2\)), \(S\) itself is not a CR manifold. The CR geometry lives on the unit circle bundle \(M_S \subset T^*S\): the Liouville \(1\)-form on \(T^*S\) restricts to a contact form \(\theta\) on \(M_S\), making it a \(3\)-dimensional strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold. The Reeb vector field \(\xi\) generates the \(U(1)\) fibre rotation of \(M_S \to S\). The natural projection \(\pi\colon S \to \mathbb{CP}^1\) (a degree-\(4\) branched covering with \(6\) branch points corresponding to the \(6\) defects of Euler characteristic \(\chi=-6\)) lifts to a principal \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)-bundle \(P \to M_S\). Setting \(E = P \times_G \mathbb{CP}^1\), the \(4\)-tuple \((M_S,\, G,\, P,\, E)\) assembles into a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe whose exceptional divisors support the \(q\)-deformed Reeb flow (Definition 1.27).

Remark 2.8 (\(\mathbb{C}\) examples as instances of the generalised framework). Each of the preceding \(\mathbb{C}\) examples is an instance of the base-field-independent Definition 1.1, realised over \(k = \mathbb{C}\) via the Levi non-degenerate framework of Example 1.4 and Remark 2.3.

  • CY hypersurfaces (Example 2.5). A smooth strictly convex real hypersurface \(M\) in a Kähler manifold inherits a strictly pseudoconvex CR structure — i.e. the Levi form has definite signature. The Kohn–Rossi condition holds automatically by Kohn’s sub-elliptic estimates.

  • ALE links (Example 2.6). The link \(S^3/\Gamma\) is the CR boundary of the minimal resolution of \(\mathbb{C}^2/\Gamma\); its Levi form has signature \((1,0)\). Kohn–Rossi finite-dimensionality follows from compactness of \(M\).

  • Bring’s surface unit circle bundle (Example 2.7). The unit circle bundle \(M_S \subset T^*S\) is a compact \(3\)-dimensional strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold with Levi signature \((1,0)\).

All three satisfy the three cohomological conditions of Remark 2.3: Kohn–Rossi finite-dimensionality (by strict pseudoconvexity or compactness), substrate non-degeneracy (positive-definite Levi form ensures \(q > 1\) wherever \(F_A^+ \neq 0\)), and the QOU vanishing conditions (by the topology of the respective principal bundles). More generally, any real hypersurface in an indefinite Kähler manifold, or the boundary of a Grauert tube over a pseudo-Riemannian manifold, yields a CR manifold with indefinite Levi signature satisfying these conditions — extending the QOU framework beyond the strictly pseudoconvex setting.

Remark 2.9 (Quantum gauge group: category-theoretic perspective). The quantum gauge group (Definition 1.13) is realised pointwise by the Hopf algebra \(\mathcal{O}_{q(p)}(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) of regular functions on \(\mathrm{SU}_{q(p)}(2)\). The categorical view replaces each fibre with its representation category \(\mathrm{Rep}(\mathrm{SU}_{q(p)}(2))\), a braided monoidal category. Tannaka–Krein duality reconstructs \(\mathcal{O}_{q(p)}\) from \(\mathrm{Rep}(\mathrm{SU}_{q(p)}(2))\) via \(\mathrm{End}^{\otimes}(\omega)\), where \(\omega\) is the forgetful fibre functor. For any two points \(p, p' \in M\) connected by a path \(\gamma\), the passage between fibres is captured by the commutative square \[\label{eq:tannaka-krein-functoriality} \begin{CD} \mathcal{O}_{q(p)}(\mathrm{SU}(2)) @>{\mathrm{Rep}}>> \mathrm{Rep}(\mathrm{SU}_{q(p)}(2)) \\ @V{\text{transition along } \gamma}VV @VV{\text{monoidal functor}}V \\ \mathcal{O}_{q(p')}(\mathrm{SU}(2)) @>>{\mathrm{Rep}}> \mathrm{Rep}(\mathrm{SU}_{q(p')}(2)) \end{CD}\] Tannaka–Krein reconstruction inverts the horizontal arrows, so the Hopf-algebra and representation-category viewpoints are equivalent fibrewise.

Remark 2.10 (Hopf-algebra structure and non-cocommutativity). Write \(\mathcal{O}_q = \mathcal{O}_q(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) and let \(\Delta_q\), \(\varepsilon_q\), \(S_q\) denote the comultiplication, counit, and antipode. The Hopf comultiplication satisfies coassociativity: \[\label{eq:coassociativity} \begin{CD} \mathcal{O}_q @>{\Delta_q}>> \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \\ @V{\Delta_q}VV @VV{\mathrm{id} \otimes \Delta_q}V \\ \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q @>>{\Delta_q \otimes \mathrm{id}}> \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \end{CD}\] Let \(\sigma \colon \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \to \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q\) denote the tensor flip \((a \otimes b \mapsto b \otimes a)\). The cocommutativity square \[\label{eq:non-cocommutativity} \begin{CD} \mathcal{O}_q @>{\Delta_q}>> \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \\ @V{\Delta_q}VV @VV{\sigma}V \\ \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q @= \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \end{CD} \quad\text{commutes}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;q = 1.\] For \(q \neq 1\) the failure of [eq:non-cocommutativity] is controlled by the universal \(R\)-matrix of \(U_q(\mathfrak{su}(2))\): \(\sigma \circ \Delta_q = \mathrm{Ad}_{R_q} \circ \Delta_q\), where \(R_1 = 1 \otimes 1\) recovers cocommutativity at \(q = 1\).

Remark 2.11 (Self-duality as a short exact sequence). In the QOU setting with \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H = 4\), the Hodge star \(*_H\) gives the splitting \(\Lambda^2 H^* = \Lambda^+\!H^* \oplus \Lambda^-\!H^*\) and hence the short exact sequence of \(\mathrm{Ad}(P)\)-valued forms \[\label{eq:self-dual-ses} \begin{CD} 0 @>>> \Gamma\!\bigl(\Lambda^-\!H^* \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(P)\bigr) @>{\iota}>> \Gamma\!\bigl(\Lambda^2 H^* \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(P)\bigr) @>{\pi^+}>> \Gamma\!\bigl(\Lambda^+\!H^* \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(P)\bigr) @>>> 0 \end{CD}\] The instanton condition \(F_A|_H = {*_H}\,F_A|_H\) (Proposition 1.9) is exactly the condition \(F_A|_H \in \ker(\mathrm{id} - {*_H})\), i.e. \(F_A^-|_H = 0\). In the language of \(2\)-groups, this has a clean reinterpretation (Remark 2.12).

Remark 2.12 (Self-duality as constrained \(2\)-group gauge theory). A strict \(2\)-group is equivalently a crossed module: a group homomorphism \(\partial \colon H \to G\) together with an action of \(G\) on \(H\) satisfying the Peiffer identity. The associated exact sequence \[\label{eq:crossed-module-ses} \begin{CD} 1 @>>> \ker\partial @>>> H @>{\partial}>> G @>>> \mathrm{coker}\,\partial @>>> 1 \end{CD}\] encodes the \(2\)-group as a homotopy type with \(\pi_0 = \mathrm{coker}\,\partial\) and \(\pi_1 = \ker\partial\).

The inner-automorphism \(2\)-group \(\mathrm{INN}(G) = (G \xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}} G)\) has \(\ker\partial = 1\) and \(\mathrm{coker}\,\partial = 1\), so it is connected and simply connected as a \(2\)-group. A \(2\)-connection for \(\mathrm{INN}(G)\) on a principal \(2\)-bundle over \(M\) consists of a \(\mathfrak{g}\)-valued \(1\)-form \(A\) and a \(\mathfrak{g}\)-valued \(2\)-form \(B\), subject to the fake-flatness condition \[\label{eq:fake-flatness} F_A \;=\; B.\] In the QOU setting, \(G = \mathrm{SU}(2)\) and the Levi distribution \(H = \ker\theta\) has \(\dim_{\mathbb{R}} H = 4\), so the Hodge decomposition \(\Lambda^2 H^* = \Lambda^+\!H^* \oplus \Lambda^-\!H^*\) is available. Imposing the simplicity constraint \[B \;\in\; \Gamma\!\bigl(\Lambda^+\!H^* \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(P)\bigr)\] — that is, requiring the \(2\)-form datum to be self-dual — and combining with fake flatness [eq:fake-flatness] yields the commutative diagram \[\label{eq:2group-instanton} \begin{CD} \Omega^1(M,\,\mathfrak{g}) @>{d_A}>> \Omega^2(M,\,\mathfrak{g}) @>{\pi^+}>> \Omega^{2,+}(M,\,\mathfrak{g}) \\ @. @AA{=\;B}A @AA{\text{simplicity}}A \\ @. F_A @= F_A^+ \end{CD}\] The fake-flatness condition \(F_A = B\) combined with the simplicity constraint \(B = F_A^+\) forces \(F_A = F_A^+\), hence \(F_A^- = 0\). This is precisely the instanton equation \(F_A|_H = {*_H}\,F_A|_H\) of Definition 1.7. Thus, the self-dual formulation used throughout this paper is a special case of \(2\)-group gauge theory for \(\mathrm{INN}(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) subject to a simplicity constraint on the \(2\)-form datum.

This perspective clarifies the relationship between the instanton condition in Chapter 1 and the higher-categorical structures of later chapters: the parallel-transport \(2\)-functor of the \(\mathrm{INN}(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) \(2\)-bundle assigns to paths in \(M\) not merely group elements in \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) but \(2\)-morphisms encoding the self-dual curvature, with the simplicity constraint selecting the physical (instanton) sector.

Remark 2.13 (Frobenius structure of the \(\mathbb{C}\) gauge algebra). The Hopf algebra \(\mathcal{O}_q(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) carries a natural Frobenius structure: the Haar state \(h_q \colon \mathcal{O}_q \to \mathbb{C}\) induces a non-degenerate pairing \(\langle a, b \rangle = h_q(ab)\), making \(\mathcal{O}_q\) into a Frobenius algebra with comultiplication \(\Delta_\mu\) dual to the multiplication. This Frobenius comultiplication is compatible with the Hopf comultiplication \(\Delta_q\) of [eq:coassociativity] and satisfies the Frobenius relation: \[\label{eq:frobenius-relation} \begin{CD} \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q @>{\mu}>> \mathcal{O}_q \\ @V{\mathrm{id} \otimes \Delta_\mu}VV @VV{\Delta_\mu}V \\ \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q @>>{\mu \otimes \mathrm{id}}> \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q \end{CD}\] where \(\mu\) is the algebra multiplication and \(\Delta_\mu\) the Frobenius comultiplication (dual to \(\mu\) under the Haar pairing). The non-degeneracy of \(h_q\) is expressed by requiring the composite \[\label{eq:frobenius-nondegen} \begin{CD} \mathcal{O}_q \otimes \mathcal{O}_q @>{\mu}>> \mathcal{O}_q @>{h_q}>> \mathbb{C} \end{CD}\] to be a perfect pairing, i.e. the induced map \(\mathcal{O}_q \to \mathcal{O}_q^*\) is an isomorphism.

This Frobenius–Hopf structure on \(\mathcal{O}_q(\mathrm{SU}(2))\) is precisely the datum required by the general Definition 1.1: the Haar state \(h_q\) realises the Frobenius form \(\lambda \colon A \to \mathbf{1}\), and the non-degeneracy [eq:frobenius-nondegen] is the \(\mathbb{C}\) instance of condition (1) in Definition 1.2. In the \(\mathbb{C}\) realisation (Example 1.4), the \(5\)-tuple \((M, \theta, G, P, E)\) and the two analytic vanishing conditions of Example 1.6 reduce to a triple:

  1. a CR manifold \((M, \theta)\) of hypersurface type with non-degenerate Levi form;

  2. a non-degenerate Frobenius–Hopf object \(A\) in the monoidal category of vector bundles over \(M\), playing the rôle of the triple \((G, P, E)\); and

  3. an instanton connection on the underlying \(A\)-bundle (Definition 1.7).

The vanishing condition \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(P)) = 0\) then follows from the non-degeneracy of the Frobenius form \(\lambda\) via the diagram \[\label{eq:frobenius-vanishing} \begin{CD} \mathrm{Ad}(A) \otimes \mathrm{Ad}(A) @>{\mu_{\mathrm{Ad}}}>> \mathrm{Ad}(A) @>{\lambda_{\mathrm{Ad}}}>> \mathcal{O}_M \\ @V{\text{global sections}}VV @. @VV{\int_M}V \\ H^0(M,\,\mathrm{Ad}(A)) \otimes H^0(M,\,\mathrm{Ad}(A)) @. @>>{\text{pairing}}> \mathbb{C} \end{CD}\] The adjoint representation \(\mathrm{Ad}(A)\) inherits a non-degenerate Frobenius pairing from \(\lambda\); any global parallel section would pair non-trivially with itself, contradicting the irreducibility of the instanton connection (Lemma 1.8). Hence the Frobenius non-degeneracy, combined with irreducibility, forces \(H^0(M, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\). The section-vanishing condition \(\Gamma_c(M, E) = 0\) follows similarly from the non-existence of \(A\)-equivariant sections of the associated \(\mathbb{CP}^1\)-bundle.

Note that the horizontal distribution \(H = \ker\theta\) itself fails to carry a Frobenius structure (as discussed in Remark 1.29); it is the gauge algebra, not the distribution, that is Frobenius.

Remark 2.21 (\(q\)-Deformed Schrödinger equation). The \(q\)-deformed Schrödinger equation governs the evolution of states in the linearised state bundle. Throughout this paper the symbol \(i\) in the Schrödinger equation is identified with the complex structure operator \(J \colon H \to H\) on the Levi distribution (\(J^2 = -\mathrm{Id}\)): it acts as a geometric twisting operator on horizontal planes rather than as an algebraic imaginary unit.

Linearisation convention. The fibre \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) is a non-linear space, so one cannot write a linear PDE for sections of \(E\) directly. We work instead with the linearised model: identify a neighbourhood of a base-point in \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) with \(\mathbb{C}\) via stereographic projection, and interpret \(\psi\) as a section of the line bundle \(E_{\mathrm{lin}}\) obtained by linearising \(E\) at the chosen section. The covariant derivative \(D_A = d + A\) then acts on sections of this line bundle in the standard way.

For a section \(\psi \in \Gamma(E_{\mathrm{lin}})\) and the \(q\)-deformed Reeb vector field \(\xi_q\) (Definition 1.26), the Schrödinger dynamics take the form \[J\,\hbar_q(p)\,\iota_{\xi_q} D_A \psi \;=\; \hat{H}_q\, \psi,\] where \(\hat{H}_q = -\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar_q\,\Delta_q + V_q\) is the \(q\)-deformed Hamiltonian (here \(\Delta_q\) is the \(q\)-Laplacian of Definition 1.17 acting on sections of \(E_{\mathrm{lin}}\), and \(V_q\) is a potential depending on the curvature \(\Omega_q\)), and:

  • \(\hbar_q(p) = 1 - q(p)^{-1}\): the quantum Planck constant (Definition 1.14) is a local field;

  • \(D_A = d + A\): the instanton connection on \(P\) acts as the potential;

  • \(\iota_{\xi_q} D_A \psi\): the covariant derivative \(D_A\psi\) is an \(E_{\mathrm{lin}}\)-valued \(1\)-form; contracting it with \(\xi_q\) yields a section of \(E_{\mathrm{lin}}\), matching the right-hand side.

As \(q(p) \to 1\) (classicality), the commutator \([\hat{X}, \hat{P}]_q \to 0\) (Remark 1.16), the uncertainty vanishes, and the wave-like nature of the section freezes into a classical trajectory.

Categorical role. Although this remark is stated as a PDE rather than in categorical language, it is essential to the paper’s structure: the Schrödinger equation provides the bottom row of the main commutative diagram (Proposition 1.32). Categorically, the \(q\)-deformed Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}_q\) defines an endomorphism of the module category \(\mathrm{Mod}_{E_{\mathrm{lin}}}\) over the jet algebra \(\Gamma(J^1 E)\), and the Schrödinger equation is the condition that \(\psi\) lies in the kernel of \(\hat{H}_q - J\hbar_q\,\iota_{\xi_q}D_A\). In the base-field-independent setting of Definitions 1.1 and 1.2, this extends to arbitrary base fields \(k\) by replacing \(\Gamma(E_{\mathrm{lin}})\) with the appropriate \(k\)-linear category of sections.

Path Integrals and Braiding

In the QOU framework, traditional path integrals are replaced by finite sums over the discrete fibre structure. Since the base space \(B\) and each fibre are finite, the continuum measure \(\mathcal{D}\phi\) collapses to a combinatorial sum, eliminating the need for renormalisation of divergent integrals.

The braid group acts naturally on the fibre degrees of freedom. What we observe as particle interactions corresponds to the action of braid operators exchanging strands in the fibre bundle. The algebraic content of these interactions is therefore encoded in braid-group representations rather than in Feynman vertices.

The exchange operations carry a \(q\)-deformation parameter inherited from the quantum group structure of the fibres. Concretely, the \(R\)-matrix of \(\mathcal{U}_q(\mathfrak{su}(2))\) furnishes a \(q\)-deformed braid operator whose eigenvalues govern the phase acquired under particle exchange.

Under this correspondence, specific knot types map to particle identities. The trefoil knot, being the simplest non-trivial torus knot, is identified with the quark; the muon arises as a lift of the trefoil to a higher fibre level. The unknot, carrying trivial braiding, corresponds to the neutrino. Experimental evidence supporting this \(q\)-deformed braiding picture is discussed in Chapter [chap:experimental].

Remark 3.4 (First-order approximation). The Lie bracket [eq:su2-commutator] captures the first-order behaviour of the group operation: for elements \(A,B\) near the identity, \[e^A e^B \;=\; e^{A+B+\tfrac{1}{2}[A,B]+\cdots},\] so the commutator \([e_a,e_b]\) is the first-order Taylor polynomial of the non-commutativity. Higher-order corrections (the full Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series) encode the complete nonlinear group law and reappear naturally in the universal enveloping algebra (Section 7.2).

Definition 3.5 (\(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) generators of \(\mathrm{Dic}_3\)). Definition 3.7 (Braid involutions: twist and flip). Let \(\mathbf{w}=(w_1,w_2,w_3,w_4)\) be a spectral resolvent tuple. Remark 3.8 (Holonomy generators and the trefoil). Given a branched cover \(\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{CP}^1\) with singular fibres at the critical values, choose two loops \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\) in \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) encircling distinct branch points. Parallel-transporting the spectral modes \(w_k\) around these loops yields holonomy operators \[\label{eq:holonomy-generators} H_1=\sigma_1,\qquad H_2=\sigma_2,\] where \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2\) are the braid generators [eq:braid-twist], [eq:braid-flip]. In the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) embedding (Definition 3.5) these become \[H_1=\exp(T\theta\,e_1),\qquad H_2=\exp(T\theta\,e_2),\] with \(T\) the geometric torsion scalar and \(\theta\) a branch angle, so the holonomy group is a representation of \(B_3\) inside \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\).

The assignment \(x\mapsto H_1\), \(y\mapsto H_2\) is therefore an isomorphism \(\pi_1(S^3\setminus K_{3,2})\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\; \langle H_1,H_2\rangle\): the monodromy group of the branched cover is the trefoil-knot group.

The composite holonomy \(H_1 H_2\colon\mathbf{w}\mapsto(w_3,w_4,w_1,w_2)\) is the \(3\)-cycle \(\alpha\) [eq:su2-3cycle] and satisfies \((H_1 H_2)^3=\mathrm{id}\) on \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\).

The trefoil knot

Remark 3.9 (Trefoil knot group). The trefoil knot \(K_{3,2}\) is the simplest non-trivial torus knot. Its complement has fundamental group \[\pi_1(S^3\setminus K_{3,2}) \;=\;\langle x,y\mid xyx=yxy\rangle \;\cong\;B_3,\] so the trefoil-knot group is isomorphic to the braid group on three strands (Definition 3.1). Example 3.10 (Trefoil braid relation and operator algebra). In the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) representation (Definition 3.5), the trefoil braid relation \(xyx=yxy\) becomes the operator identity \[\alpha\,\beta\,\alpha \;=\;\beta\,\alpha\,\beta\] for the generators \(\alpha\) [eq:su2-3cycle] and \(\beta\) [eq:su2-2cycle]. Lifting of Quantum Torsion

📄

Proposition 4.1 (Jet Prolongation). Let \((M, G, P, E)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe (Example 1.6) with instanton connection \(A\) on \(P\) and metric \(g\) on \(E\). Write \(\widetilde{M} = E\) for the total space of \(E\), viewed as a manifold, and define \(P'\) as the frame bundle and \(E'\) as the bundle of orthonormal frames of the first jet bundle \(J^1 E\). Then \(A\) and \(g\) uniquely determine a connection \(A'\) on \(P'\) such that \((\widetilde{M}, G, P', E')\) is a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe.

Proof. The proof proceeds in the rigid monoidal category underlying the quantum observable universe. Let \(A\) denote the Frobenius-Hopf object representing the gauge-bundle data of \((M,G,P,E)\).

Step 1: Categorical jet object. The first jet bundle \(J^1 E\) is represented categorically by the object \(A \otimes \Omega^1(A)\), where \(\Omega^1(A) = \ker(m\colon A\otimes A \to A)\) is the kernel of the multiplication (the bimodule of Kähler differentials, i.e. the universal first-order differential calculus over \(A\)). Because \(A\) is Frobenius, the non-degenerate pairing \(\varepsilon \circ m \colon A \otimes A \to \mathbf{1}\) exhibits \(\Omega^1(A)\) as a direct summand of \(A \otimes A\), hence \(J^1(A) := A \otimes \Omega^1(A)\) is again an object of the ambient category.

Step 2: Inherited Frobenius-Hopf structure. The Frobenius relation \((\mathrm{Id} \otimes m) \circ (\Delta \otimes \mathrm{Id}) = \Delta \circ m = (m \otimes \mathrm{Id}) \circ (\mathrm{Id} \otimes \Delta)\) ensures that the comultiplication \(\Delta\colon A \to A \otimes A\) restricts to a well-defined comultiplication \(\Delta'\colon J^1(A) \to J^1(A) \otimes J^1(A)\), and the counit \(\varepsilon\colon A \to \mathbf{1}\) induces a counit \(\varepsilon'\) on \(J^1(A)\). The Hopf antipode \(S\) extends to \(J^1(A)\) by functoriality of the Kähler differential construction. Thus \(J^1(A)\) is a Frobenius-Hopf object.

Step 3: Non-degeneracy and vanishing. The non-degeneracy of the Frobenius form on \(A\) (the pairing \(\varepsilon \circ m\) is a perfect pairing) implies non-degeneracy of the induced form on \(J^1(A)\), since \(\Omega^1(A)\) is a direct summand. For the vanishing condition, \(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\) implies \(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(J^1(A))) = 0\): the adjoint representation of \(J^1(A)\) contains \(\mathrm{Ad}(A)\) as a subobject, and any invariant in \(\mathrm{Ad}(J^1(A))\) would project to a non-zero invariant in \(\mathrm{Ad}(A)\), contradicting the hypothesis.

Step 4: Geometric realisation. Applying the fibre functor \(\omega\colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathrm{Vect}_k\) (where \(\mathcal{C}\) is the rigid monoidal category of the quantum observable universe) recovers the geometric data: \(\omega(J^1(A))\) is the space of sections of the first jet bundle \(J^1 E\), the frame bundle \(P'\) and orthonormal frame bundle \(E'\) are the geometric counterparts of the Frobenius-Hopf structure on \(J^1(A)\), and the connection \(A'\) is the image under \(\omega\) of the comultiplication \(\Delta'\). Its uniqueness follows from the irreducibility of \(A'\), which is guaranteed by the inherited vanishing condition of Step 3. ◻

Remark 4.2 (Prolongation from the Frobenius-Hopf structure). The proof above shows that jet prolongation is an intrinsic consequence of the Frobenius-Hopf structure in a rigid monoidal category: no additional geometric input beyond the categorical data \((\mathcal{C}, A, \omega)\) is required. Concretely:

  1. The Frobenius structure provides the Kähler differentials \(\Omega^1(A)\) as a direct summand, from which the jet object \(J^1(A)\) is built.

  2. The Frobenius relation ensures that the comultiplication and multiplication are compatible, so the prolonged object inherits the full Hopf algebra structure.

  3. The non-degeneracy and vanishing conditions propagate from \(A\) to \(J^1(A)\) by functoriality.

In this sense, the recursive brane tower (Definition 4.3) is generated entirely by iterating the categorical jet construction. The tower, together with its associated descent (Subsection 4.0.1), is a formal consequence of the axioms of a quantum observable universe and does not require any case-by-case verification.

Remark 4.8 (Weight-space decomposition of the harmonic basis). The adjoint bundle \(\mathrm{ad}\, E_n\) has fibre \(\mathfrak{su}(2)\). Via the weight-space decomposition of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), the harmonic basis of \(\mathcal{H}^1(E_n,\, \mathrm{ad}\, E_n)\) splits into two families.

Longitudinal modes. These forms constitute the \(\mathrm{U}(1)\) backbone of the decomposition. Ladder modes. These are the raising and lowering vortices of the decomposition. They describe the entangling flows that twist the \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) fibre as one moves along the brane. In the \(1\)-jet bundle \(J^1 E_n\) (Remark 2.15), each basis element \(\alpha\) is a pair consisting of a value (the direction of the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) flow in the fibre) and a momentum (the gradient of that flow relative to the complex structure of the brane).

Proposition 4.14 (Existence and Uniqueness of the Vacuum State). Let \(\mathcal{A}_\infty\) be the direct limit algebra (Definition 4.11) equipped with the global \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) action and the recursive \(q\)-deformations. Then there exists a unique state \(\omega \colon \mathcal{A}_\infty \to \mathbb{C}\) that is \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)-invariant: \[\omega(g \cdot a) = \omega(a) \quad\text{for all } g \in \mathrm{SU}(2),\; a \in \mathcal{A}_\infty.\]

Proof. The algebra \(\mathcal{A}_\infty\) is the direct limit of the fibre algebras \(\Gamma(J^1 E_n,\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_n}(2))\) (Definition 4.11), each of which is a \(C^*\)-algebra carrying a continuous \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) action by automorphisms.

Existence. At each finite level \(n\) the algebra \(\Gamma(J^1 E_n,\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_n}(2))\) is a type-I \(C^*\)-algebra (it is a section algebra of a locally trivial bundle with compact fibre). By compactness of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), averaging any state \(\psi_n\) over the Haar measure \(\mu\) produces an \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)-invariant state: \[\omega_n(a) \;=\; \int_{\mathrm{SU}(2)} \psi_n(g \cdot a)\; d\mu(g).\] The descent homomorphisms \(\mathfrak{d}_n\) (Lemma 4.20) are equivariant and surjective, so the invariant states \(\{\omega_n\}\) form a compatible system. By the universal property of the direct limit, they determine a state \(\omega\) on \(\mathcal{A}_\infty\) satisfying \(\omega(g \cdot a) = \omega(a)\).

Uniqueness. The vanishing condition \(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\) of the quantum observable universe axioms implies that the trivial representation appears with multiplicity one in each \(\Gamma(J^1 E_n,\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_n}(2))\) (it occurs only in the scalar summand \(\mathbb{C} \cdot \mathbf{1}\)). Any \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)-invariant state must factor through the projection onto this trivial isotypic component, which is one-dimensional. Hence \(\omega\) is the unique such state. ◻

Proposition 4.17 (Stability of the Euler characteristic). Let \((M, G, P, E)\) be a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe and let \(\{E_n\}_{n \geq 1}\) be the recursive brane tower (Definition 4.3). Then the Euler characteristic \(\chi(E)\) is stable under descent and lifting: for each level \(n\), \[\chi(E_{n+1}) \;=\; \chi(E_n).\] In particular, if the base level satisfies \(\chi(E_1) = c\), then \(\chi(E_n) = c\) for all \(n \geq 1\).

Proof. By the construction of the recursive brane tower (Definition 4.3), \(E_{n+1}\) is the bundle of orthonormal frames of the first jet bundle \(J^1 E_n\) (Proposition 4.1). The Euler characteristic of a fibre bundle \(F \hookrightarrow E_{n+1} \to E_n\) satisfies the multiplicativity formula \[\chi(E_{n+1}) \;=\; \chi(E_n) \cdot \chi(F),\] provided the fibre \(F\) has the homotopy type of a finite CW-complex and the base acts trivially on \(H^*(F;\, \mathbb{Q})\). In the brane tower the fibre is \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3\), which has \(\chi(S^3) = 0\) for the odd-dimensional sphere. However, the relevant bundle is not \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) itself but the projectivised frame bundle, whose typical fibre is \(\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1) \cong \mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2\) with \(\chi(S^2) = 2\). Nonetheless, the Euler characteristic is preserved for the following reason.

The Leray–Serre spectral sequence of \(S^2 \hookrightarrow E_{n+1} \to E_n\) has \(E_2^{p,q} = H^p(E_n;\, H^q(S^2;\, \mathbb{Z}))\). The quantum characteristic classes \(\kappa_n\) (Definition 4.5) are precisely the obstructions to trivial monodromy of the fibre cohomology \(H^*(S^2)\). By the vanishing condition of the quantum observable universe axioms—which forces \(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\)—the monodromy representation of \(\pi_1(E_n)\) on \(H^*(S^2;\, \mathbb{Z})\) is trivial (the adjoint bundle has no invariant sections to twist the fibre cohomology). Consequently the spectral sequence collapses at the \(E_2\) page. The preservation of the Euler characteristic follows from the fact that the instanton connection \(A_n\) provides a global section of the associated \(S^2\)-bundle \(E_{n+1}/\mathrm{U}(1) \to E_n\): the instanton itself determines a preferred point in each fibre \(\mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1) \cong S^2\). This global section makes the projection \(E_{n+1} \to E_n\) a homotopy equivalence, whence \(\chi(E_{n+1}) = \chi(E_n)\). ◻

Lemma 4.20 (Descent preserves the quantum observable universe). Let \(\{E_n\}_{n \geq 1}\) be the recursive brane tower (Definition 4.3) and let \(n \geq 2\). The restriction of the connection \(A_n\) and bundle \(E_n\) to the base manifold \(M_{n-1} = E_{n-1}\) yields a tuple \((M_{n-1}, G, P_{n-1}, E_{n-1})\) that is again a \(\mathbb{C}\) quantum observable universe. Moreover, the descent map \[\mathfrak{d}_n \;\colon\; \Gamma\!\bigl(J^1 E_n,\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_n}(2)\bigr) \;\longrightarrow\; \Gamma\!\bigl(J^1 E_{n-1},\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_{n-1}}(2)\bigr)\] is a surjective \(*\)-homomorphism that intertwines the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) actions at levels \(n\) and \(n{-}1\).

Proof. The proof mirrors the ascent (Proposition 4.1) in reverse, working inside the rigid monoidal category \(\mathcal{C}\).

Step 1: Frobenius restriction. The jet object \(J^1(A) = A \otimes \Omega^1(A)\) at level \(n\) contains \(A\) as a direct summand via the unit section \(\eta \otimes \mathrm{id}\colon \Omega^1(A) \hookrightarrow A \otimes \Omega^1(A)\). Composing with the counit \(\varepsilon\colon A \to \mathbf{1}\) on the first tensor factor gives the restriction map \(r_n\colon J^1(A) \to A\). Because \(A\) is Frobenius, \(r_n\) is a coalgebra morphism: the comultiplication \(\Delta\) commutes with restriction by the Frobenius relation, and the counit restricts by construction. Hence the restricted object inherits a Frobenius–Hopf structure.

Step 2: Connection restriction. The connection \(A_n\) on \(E_n\) is determined by the comultiplication \(\Delta'\) on \(J^1(A)\) (Proposition 4.1, Step 2). Restricting \(\Delta'\) along \(r_n\) recovers the original comultiplication \(\Delta\) on \(A\), which by the construction of the brane tower corresponds to the connection \(A_{n-1}\) on \(E_{n-1}\). In particular, \(A_{n-1}\) satisfies the Hermitian Yang–Mills equations on \(E_{n-1}\) because it did so at level \(n{-}1\) before the ascent.

Step 3: Non-degeneracy and vanishing. Non-degeneracy of the Frobenius form on the restricted object follows from the direct-summand inclusion \(A \hookrightarrow J^1(A)\): the restriction of a non-degenerate pairing to a direct summand remains non-degenerate. The vanishing condition \(\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbf{1}, \mathrm{Ad}(A)) = 0\) is unchanged since we recover the original object \(A\).

Step 4: The descent homomorphism. Applying the fibre functor \(\omega\) to \(r_n\) yields \(\mathfrak{d}_n\colon \Gamma(J^1 E_n,\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_n}(2)) \to \Gamma(J^1 E_{n-1},\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_{n-1}}(2))\). This is a \(*\)-homomorphism because \(r_n\) preserves multiplication and the antipode. Surjectivity follows from the fact that the ascent map \(\ell_n\) of Proposition 4.1 is injective (it embeds \(A\) into \(J^1(A)\) as a direct summand), so its categorical adjoint \(r_n\) is surjective.

Step 5: Equivariance. The \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) action on sections at level \(n\) is induced by the adjoint representation of the Hopf algebra. Since \(r_n\) is a Hopf algebra morphism, it intertwines the adjoint actions at levels \(n\) and \(n{-}1\). ◻

Definition 4.21 (Descent involution). Let \(\ell_n\) denote the ascent embedding (Proposition 4.1) and \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) the descent homomorphism (Lemma 4.20). The descent involution at level \(n\) is the endomorphism \[\tau_n \;=\; \ell_n \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \;\colon\; \Gamma\!\bigl(J^1 E_{n+1},\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_{n+1}}(2)\bigr) \;\longrightarrow\; \Gamma\!\bigl(J^1 E_{n+1},\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_{n+1}}(2)\bigr).\] Since \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \circ \ell_n = \mathrm{id}\) on \(\Gamma(J^1 E_n,\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_n}(2))\) (the descent of the ascent recovers the original section, because \(\ell_n\) embeds \(A\) as a direct summand of \(J^1(A)\) and \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) is the retraction), we have \[\tau_n^2 \;=\; \ell_n \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \circ \ell_n \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \;=\; \ell_n \circ (\mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \circ \ell_n) \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \;=\; \ell_n \circ \mathrm{id} \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \;=\; \tau_n,\] so \(\tau_n\) is idempotent. Its image is the descended subalgebra \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{desc}} \subseteq \Gamma(J^1 E_{n+1}, \mathrm{SU}_{q_{n+1}}(2))\), consisting of sections at level \(n{+}1\) that factor through the level-\(n\) algebra via the ascent embedding.

Proposition 4.22 (Eigenspaces of the descent involution). The descent involution \(\tau_n\) (Definition 4.21) decomposes the level-\((n{+}1)\) section algebra into a direct sum \[\Gamma\!\bigl(J^1 E_{n+1},\, \mathrm{SU}_{q_{n+1}}(2)\bigr) \;=\; \mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{desc}} \;\oplus\; \mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{trans}},\] where \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{desc}} = \mathrm{im}\,\tau_n\) is the descended subalgebra (sections that factor through level \(n\)) and \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{trans}} = \ker\,\tau_n\) is the transient complement (genuinely new modes at level \(n{+}1\)). The algebraic filter (Remark 4.9) refines this decomposition:

  1. The bosonic (longitudinal) modes lifted from level \(n\) lie entirely in \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{desc}}\): they commute with the curvature and survive every round-trip through the tower.

  2. The fermionic (ladder) modes at level \(n{+}1\) split between the two summands; those in \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{trans}}\) are the modes created by the jet prolongation that have no pre-image at level \(n\).

Proof. Since \(\tau_n\) is an idempotent \(*\)-endomorphism of a \(C^*\)-algebra, the splitting \(\mathrm{im}\,\tau_n \oplus \ker\,\tau_n\) is an orthogonal direct sum.

For item (1), let \(\alpha \in \Gamma(J^1 E_{n+1})\) be a bosonic mode lifted from level \(n\): \([F_A, \alpha] = 0\) (Remark 4.9). Bosonic modes take values in the Cartan subalgebra \(\mathrm{U}(1) \subset \mathfrak{su}(2)\) (Remark 4.8). Since \(\alpha\) lies in the image of the ascent \(\ell_n\), we have \(\alpha = \ell_n(\beta)\) for some \(\beta \in \Gamma(J^1 E_n)\). Then \(\tau_n(\alpha) = \ell_n(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}(\ell_n(\beta))) = \ell_n(\beta) = \alpha\), using \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1} \circ \ell_n = \mathrm{id}\). Hence \(\alpha \in \mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{desc}}\).

For item (2), the ladder modes \(\omega^\pm \otimes E_\pm\) at level \(n{+}1\) couple to the curvature \(F_A\) through the covariant derivative \(d_A\). Those that arise as lifts of level-\(n\) modes lie in \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{desc}}\) by the same argument as item (1). The remaining modes—genuinely new harmonic directions in \(J^1 E_{n+1}\) with no counterpart at level \(n\)—lie in \(\mathcal{A}_{n+1}^{\mathrm{trans}}\) and are annihilated by \(\tau_n\): the descent \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) maps them to zero in the level-\(n\) algebra, so \(\tau_n = \ell_n \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) sends them to zero. ◻

Definition 4.23 (Defining boundary of the brane). Let \(\{E_n\}_{n \geq 1}\) be the recursive brane tower (Definition 4.3) and let \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) be the descent homomorphism (Lemma 4.20). The defining boundary of the \(n\)-th brane is the subset \[\partial E_n \;=\; \bigl\{\, p \in E_n \;\bigm|\; \ker\bigl(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\big|_p\bigr) \neq 0 \,\bigr\} \;\subseteq\; E_n,\] i.e. the locus of points where the fibrewise descent map has a non-trivial kernel. Equivalently, \(\partial E_n\) is the degeneracy locus of the restriction \(r_n\colon J^1(A) \to A\) evaluated fibre by fibre.

Proposition 4.24 (Structure of the defining boundary). The defining boundary \(\partial E_n\) (Definition 4.23) has the following properties:

  1. Codimension. \(\partial E_n\) is a closed subset of real codimension at least \(2\) in \(E_n\).

  2. Exceptional divisor. At each blow-up centre \(p \in \partial E_n\) the fibre \(\ker(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}|_p)\) is isomorphic to \(\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2\), which is the exceptional divisor of the topological blow-up replacing the singular point.

  3. Involution symmetry. The descent involution \(\tau_n\) (Definition 4.21) restricts to an involution on \(\partial E_n\), whose fixed-point set coincides with the monodromy fixed points of the quantum characteristic classes (Definition 4.5).

  4. Chirality interface. When \(n = 1\), \(\partial E_1\) separates the macroscopic spacetime coordinates from the internal chiral modes: it is the geometric boundary between the base manifold \(M\) and the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) fibre, across which the Reeb involution \(\xi \mapsto -\xi\) (Remark 6.8) reverses orientation.

Proof. (1). The descent map \(\mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) is a surjective \(*\)-homomorphism of fibre algebras (Lemma 4.20). Its kernel at a generic point is zero because the ascent \(\ell_n\) embeds \(A\) as a direct summand of \(J^1(A)\) and the restriction \(r_n\) is the left-inverse of this embedding. The kernel becomes non-trivial only where the direct-summand splitting degenerates, which is a closed algebraic condition on the Frobenius pairing \(\varepsilon \circ m\). Since \(A\) is a Frobenius–Hopf object in a rigid monoidal category, this degeneracy locus has real codimension at least \(2\) by the standard codimension bound for degeneracy loci of morphisms between vector bundles of equal rank.

(2). At a blow-up centre \(p \in \partial E_n\) the kernel of \(r_n|_p\) is the fibre of \(\Omega^1(A)\) over \(p\) modulo the image of \(\eta\colon \mathbf{1} \to A\). Since \(\Omega^1(A) \hookrightarrow A \otimes A\) and \(A\) has fibre \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), the quotient is isomorphic to the projectivisation of the \(2\)-dimensional representation, i.e. \(\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2\). This is the exceptional divisor introduced by the topological blow-up that resolves the singularity at \(p\).

(3). The descent involution \(\tau_n = \ell_n \circ \mathfrak{d}_{n+1}\) (Definition 4.21), acting on sections of \(J^1 E_{n+1}\), restricts to the boundary locus \(\partial E_n\) as follows.
