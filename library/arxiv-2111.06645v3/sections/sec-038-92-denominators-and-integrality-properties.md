[]{#sub.denominators label="sub.denominators"}

The universal denominator statement given in formula [\[Dn\]](#Dn){reference-type="eqref" reference="Dn"} above was found empirically on the basis of the extensive numerical data for the $4_1$, $5_2$ and the $(-2,3,7)$ pretzel knots presented in the appendix to this paper. In this section, we prove it for the denominators of the power series defined in terms of Gaussian-type integrals in [@DG2]. This proof only applies to $\sigma\in\mathcal P^{\text{red}}_K$, since there is no such integral representation for the trivial representation, but the corresponding denominator statement is true here also and can in fact be strengthened because the power series in that case come from the Habiro ring, as explained at the end of this section.

::: Theorem
[]{#thm.denom label="thm.denom"} For each knot $K$, representation $\sigma\in\mathcal P_K$, and number $\alpha\in \mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$, we have $$D_n  \widetilde A^{(K,\sigma)}_{\alpha,n} \in \mathcal O_S\big[\zeta_\alpha,c^{-1}\big],$$ where is as in [@DG2], $D_n$ is as in [\[Dn\]](#Dn){reference-type="eqref" reference="Dn"}, is as in [\[Phiprec2\]](#Phiprec2){reference-type="eqref" reference="Phiprec2"}, $c$ is the denominator of $\alpha$, $\mathcal O$ is the ring of integers of $F_\sigma$ and $S$ is a finite set of primes of $F_\sigma$ that depends on $K$ but not on $n$ or on $\alpha$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* We give the proof only for the case $\alpha=0$, $c=1$, using the formulas in [@DG]. The general case can be proved along the same lines using the more complicated formulas in [@DG2], in which the Bernoulli numbers are replaced by Bernoulli polynomials, but we do not give the details here. We will also ignore the prime 2 in our proof since it behaves somewhat differently and in any case can be added to the finite set of excluded primes $S$ in the statement of the theorem.

The power series attached to a triangulation $\mathcal T$ of $\mathbb S^3\smallsetminus K$ were defined in [@DG] as formal Gaussian integrals $\langle f_{\mathcal T}\rangle$ of the formal power series $$\label{psixz}
f_{\mathcal T}(x;z) = \exp\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^N \sum_{\substack{r, k\ge0 \\ 2r+k-2 > 0}}
\frac{B_r}{r!} \frac{(-x_j)^k}{k!} \mathrm{Li}_{2-r-k}(z_j)  h^{r+\tfrac k2-1} \Bigg)$$ in a multi-variable $x=(x_1,\dots,x_N)$, where $z_1,\dots,z_N$ are the shape parameters of $\mathcal T$ and where ${\langle f\rangle=\langle f(x)\rangle_Q}$ is the mean value defined by Gaussian integration with respect to a certain quadratic form $Q$ with coefficients in the field $F_\sigma$. This form is essentially the one given by the symmetric matrix $A+B \,\operatorname{\mathbf{diag}}(z_j/(1-z_j))$ that occurred in the discussion of [\[Nahm3\]](#Nahm3){reference-type="eqref" reference="Nahm3"} in Section [\[sub.PhiPerturbative\]](#sub.PhiPerturbative){reference-type="ref" reference="sub.PhiPerturbative"}, and the function [\[psixz\]](#psixz){reference-type="eqref" reference="psixz"} is essentially the product of the functions occurring in [\[newpsixz\]](#newpsixz){reference-type="eqref" reference="newpsixz"}, except that the terms with $r+k=m$ fixed were combined there into a single Bernoulli polynomial $B_m(x)$ for $m\ge3$ or $B_2(x)-x^2$ for $m=2$, and that the normalizations used in [@DG] were slightly different from the ones used in Section [\[sub.PhiPerturbative\]](#sub.PhiPerturbative){reference-type="ref" reference="sub.PhiPerturbative"}.

We now expand the exponential in [\[psixz\]](#psixz){reference-type="eqref" reference="psixz"} as the product of the exponentials of the monomials in the sum, and recall that $\mathrm{Li}_{2-m}(z)\in\mathbb Z[1/(1-z)]$ for every $m\ge2$, to deduce that is an $R$-linear combination of Gaussian averages $\langle T\rangle$ of terms $T$ of the form $$\label{term}
T = \prod_{j=1}^N\prod_{\substack{r, k\ge0 \\ 2r+k\ge3\substack}}
\frac1{\lambda_j(r,k)!} \biggl(\frac{B_r}{r!}
\frac{x_j^k}{k!} h^{r+\tfrac k2-1}\biggr)^{\lambda_j(r,k)}$$ with non-negative multiplicities $\lambda_j(r,k)$, where $R=R_{\mathcal T,\sigma}$ denotes the ring generated over $\mathbb Z$ by the numbers $(1-z_j)^{-1}$. We write the monomial $T$ as $c(T) h^n \mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K!$ with $$\label{nAndK}
n = \sum_{j,r,k}\lambda_j(r,k)\left(r+\frac k2-1\right),\qquad K_j=\sum_{r,k}\lambda_j(r,k)k$$ and where for notational convenience have written $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf K$ for the $N$-tuples $(x_1,\dots,x_N)$ and ${(K_1,\dots,K_N)}$ (thus deviating from the convention in the rest of the paper where boldface denotes matrices) and $\mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K!$ for the divided power . To prove the theorem, we have to bound both the denominators of the numerical coefficient $c(T)$ and the further denominators coming from the Gaussian averaging $\mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K! \mapsto\big\langle\mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K! \big\rangle$.

We begin with the latter question. For this, we recall first that the Gaussian average $\langle f\rangle_Q$ is given by for any power series $f$, where $\Delta_Q$ is the Laplacian associated to $Q$, and hence is equal to $\Delta_Q^\ell(f)/\ell!$ if $f$ is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2\ell$. (For polynomials of odd degree it of course vanishes trivially.) The Laplacian $\Delta_Q$ is a quadratic polynomial in the derivatives $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x_i$, and we can enlarge the ring $R$ by adjoining to it the coefficients of this polynomial, so since the image of any divided factorial under any product $\partial_1^{\ell_1}\cdots\partial_N^{\ell_N}$ is an integer, we then certainly have that the Gaussian integral $\big\langle\mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K! \big\rangle$ is $1/\ell!$ times an element of $R$. Unfortunately, it turns out that this estimate is not good enough for our purposes, and we have to work a little harder.

Writing $\Delta_Q$ as an $R$-linear combination of binomials $\partial_i\partial_j$ with $1\le i\le j\le N$, and applying the multinomial theorem, we see that $\Delta_Q^\ell/\ell!$ is an $R$-linear combination of terms with $m_{ij}\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$. Define an even symmetric $N\times N$ matrix $M$ by setting $M_{ij}=M_{ji}=m_{ij}$ for $i<j$ and $M_{ii}=2 m_{ii}$. Then with $\mathbf K=M\mathbf 1$, where $\mathbf 1$ is the vector consisting of $N$ 1's, and this sends $\mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K!$ to 1 and all other monomials to 0. It follows that a universal denominator of $\big\langle\mathbf x^\mathbf K/\mathbf K! \big\rangle$ is the number $$\begin{aligned}
\Delta(\mathbf K) := \text{l.c.m.}\Biggl\{ \prod_{1\le i\le N}(M_{ii}/2)!\prod_{1\le i<j\le N}M_{ij}!
 \,\Big|\, \text{$M=M^t\in M_{N,N}(\mathbb Z_{\ge0})$ even, $M\mathbf 1=\mathbf K$}\Biggr\} .
\end{aligned}$$ Notice that this does divide $\ell!$, because $\ell$ is the sum of the diagonal $M_{ii}/2$ and of the $M_{ij}$ with ${i<j}$, so that this statement refines the bound given above, but $\Delta(\mathbf K)$ is in general much smaller than $\ell!$ and this improvement will be needed for the proof. A usually sharper multiplicative upper bound for $\Delta(\mathbf K)$ is the largest integer $S$ whose square divides the product of the $K_j!$ (the proof of this also uses only the integrality of multinomial coefficients), and then of course $\Delta(\mathbf K)$ also divides the g.c.d. of $\ell!$ and $S$, which in general is smaller than either one. (For instance, for $\mathbf K=(6,9,9,10)$ we have $\ell!=355687428096000$, $S=3135283200$, and ${(\ell!,S)=S/3}$.) Either of these two latter upper bounds would be sufficient for our proof, but in fact there is an easy upper bound that is stronger than either one of them and is extremely sharp (in particular, it is equal to $\Delta(\mathbf K)$ for all $\mathbf K$ with $N\le4$ and $\max(K_j)\le 30$), namely $$\Delta^*(\mathbf K) := \prod_{\text{$p$ prime}} p^{\delta_p(\mathbf K)} \quad \text{with} \quad
\delta_p(K_1,\dots,K_N) := \sum_{s\ge1}
\left[\frac12\sum_{j=1}^N\left[\frac{K_j}{p^s}\right]\right] .$$ To show that $\Delta(\mathbf K)$ divides $\Delta^*(\mathbf K)$, we need $\sum_iV_p(M_{ii}/2)+\sum_{i<j}V_p(M_{ij})\le\delta_p(\mathbf K)$ for every prime $p$ and every even symmetric matrix $M$ with non-negative entries and row sums $\mathbf K$, where ${V_p(m):=v_p(m!)}$ for any $m\ge0$ denotes the largest power of $p$ dividing $m!$. In view of the standard formula $V_p(m)=\sum_{s\ge1}[m/p^s]$, it suffices for this to show that $$\sum_i[M_{ii}/2q]+\sum_{i<j}[M_{ij}/q]\le\frac12\sum_j[K_j/q]$$ for every prime power $q$, and this follows immediately from the obvious facts $[x/2q]=[[x/2]/q]$ and $[x]+[y]+\cdots \le[x+y+\cdots]$ valid for arbitrary real numbers $x, y,\dots$.

Now going back to our main problem, we now see that it suffices to show that the product $\Delta^*(\mathbf K) c(T)$ has denominator dividing $D_n$ for all terms $T$ as above, with $\mathbf K=(K_1,\dots,K_N)$ and $n$ defined by [\[nAndK\]](#nAndK){reference-type="eqref" reference="nAndK"}. We will prove this one prime at a time (ignoring the prime 2), which is natural in view of the fact that the upper bound $\Delta^*(\mathbf K)$ is defined by its prime power decomposition. To do this, we will split both the term $T$ and the corresponding numerical coefficient $c(T)$, and also each of the $N$ factors $T_j$ and $c(T_j)$ of which they are comprised, as the product of four factors in a way depending on the prime $p$ being studied, labelled "$s$" (terms with $r=0$ and $k$ smaller than $p$), "$b$" (terms with $r=0$ and $k$ bigger than or equal to $p$), "1" (terms with ${r=1}$), and "$\ge2$" (terms with $r\ge2$), with a similar splitting of the individual weights $K_j$ into the sum of four pieces , , , and . We also decompose the number $n$ (the exponent of $h$ in $T$) in [\[nAndK\]](#nAndK){reference-type="eqref" reference="nAndK"} as $\ell+n'-t$ with $$\ell := \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^NK_j , \qquad
n' :=\sum_{\substack{1\le j\le N \\ r\ge2, k\ge0\substack}}\lambda_j(r,k) (r-1) , \qquad

::: Lemma
[]{#LemmaB label="LemmaB"} For any integers $r\ge2$ and $\lambda\ge0$, we have $$\label{BernEstimate}
\frac1{\lambda!} \left(\frac{B_r}{r!}\right)^\lambda\in \frac1{D_{\lambda(r-1)}} \mathbb Z.$$
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* We prove this one prime at time. By well-known results of von Staudt and Clausen, the Bernoulli number $B_r$ ($r>0$) has $p$-adic valuation $-1$ if ${(p-1)|r}$ and $B_r/r$ is $p$-integral if ${p-1}$ does not divide $r$. From this we deduce that the $p$-adic valuation of the denominator of $B_r/r!$ is bounded above by $\bigl[\frac r{p-1}\bigr]$, which is $\le\bigl[\frac {r-1}{p-2}\bigr]$ since $\frac{r-1}{p-2}\ge\frac r{p-1}$ if $r\ge p-1$ and both expressions vanish otherwise. The $p$-adic valuation of the denominator of is therefore bounded above by . On the other hand, from the definition of $D_n$ we have $v_p(D_n)=m+V_p(m)=V_p(pm)$, where $m=\bigl[\frac n{p-2}\bigr]$. We must therefore show that $$V_p(\lambda)+\lambda\biggl[\frac{r-1}{p-2}\biggr] \le
V_p\biggl(p \biggr[\frac{\lambda(r-1)}{p-2}\biggr]\biggl)$$ for all $\lambda\ge0$ and $r\ge2$. For this, we make a case distinction: if $2\le r<p-1$, then $$\text{l.h.s.} = V_p(\lambda) = V_p\biggl(p \biggl[\frac\lambda p\biggr]\biggr) \le
V_p\biggl(p \biggl[\frac\lambda{p-2}\biggr]\biggr) \le \text{r.h.s.} ,$$ while if $r\ge p-1$, then we set $h=\bigl[\frac{r-1}{p-2}\bigr]\ge1$ and have instead $$\text{l.h.s.} = V_p(\lambda)+\lambda h \le \lambda h+V_p(\lambda h) = V_p(p\lambda h) \le \text{r.h.s.}$$ because $[\lambda x]\ge \lambda[x]$ for any positive real number $x$. ◻
:::

We end this subsection with several further observations concerning the denominators and integrality properties of the coefficients of our divergent power series. The first is that the bound in Theorem [\[thm.denom\]](#thm.denom){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.denom"} is not only sharp in the strong sense that it is best possible for *every* integer $n\ge0$ and not merely that it is attained for some $n$, but that this optimality is reached in two very different extreme ways: in the above lemma if $r=p-1$ and $\lambda\ge0$ is arbitrary (in which case both sides of [\[BernEstimate\]](#BernEstimate){reference-type="eqref" reference="BernEstimate"} have the same value $V_p(p\lambda)$) and again in the calculation [\[Final\]](#Final){reference-type="eqref" reference="Final"} in the case when only occurs and all $k_i$ are equal to $p$, so that is exactly (in other words whenever the dominating contribution in [\[term\]](#term){reference-type="eqref" reference="term"} comes from the terms with $(r,k)=(p-1,0)$ or $(0,p)$). The fact that two completely different mechanisms lead to the same function $n\mapsto D_n$ suggests that this function may be a more fundamental one than appears at first sight and may have a broader domain of applicability.

The second observation is that the universal denominator statement given by Theorem [\[thm.denom\]](#thm.denom){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.denom"} can be sharpened by considering the series at the logarithmic level, or equivalently, by studying the denominators of the contributions from connected rather than from all Feynman diagram. This was motivated by the observation of Peter Scholze that the logarithm of the series in [\[as41\]](#as41){reference-type="eqref" reference="as41"}, which we had calculated up to order $O\big(h^{150}\big)$, had coefficients with smaller denominators than those of the series itself. Specifically, he found experimentally that the first occurrence of $p^k$ for small primes $p$ ($\ne2, 3$) and $k\ge1$ in occurred for the coefficient of $h^n$ with ${n=k(p-1)-1}$ rather than $n=k(p-2)$ as for the power series itself. At first sight this statement seems to contradict the intuition mentioned at the beginning of the section that the arithmetic of the series $\Phi_\alpha(h)$ is much simpler if one does not take their logarithms. But in fact both statements are true! The reason is that in general $\Phi_\alpha(h)$ is a linear combination of finitely many power series corresponding to the stationary points of the function being integrated (specifically, there are $c^M$ such series, where $c$ is the denominator of $\alpha$ and $M$ can be taken to be the number of tetrahedra in a triangulation of the knot complement), and it is not reasonable to take logarithms of sums. But for $\alpha=0$ there is only one summand, so here it is reasonable to take the logarithm, and for general $\alpha$ the logarithm of each of the finitely many summands of $\Phi_a(h)$ coming from the contribution to the state integral near an individual stationary point is indeed simpler than this summand itself, because it corresponds to a sum over only connected rather than over all Feynman diagrams. The final statement is given in the following theorem.

::: Theorem
[]{#thm.denomc label="thm.denomc"} For each integer $n\ge1$, define $$D_n^\mathrm{conn}= \prod_{\text{$p$ {\rm prime}}} p^{[(n+1)/(p-1)]}  .$$ Then the coefficient of $h^n$ in for any knot $K$ and any $\sigma\in\mathcal P_K$ has denominator dividing $D_n^\mathrm{conn}$ apart from a finite set of primes depending only on $K$ and $\sigma$. More generally, for any $\alpha\in\mathbb Q$ we have .
:::

\label{Dnconnvalues}

958003200, \\ &   2615348736000, \ 5230697472000, \ \dots.
\end{aligned}$$ This sequence too appears in the online-encyclopedia of integer sequences [@OEIS] under the name `A091137` and with the formula given above, and coincides with the denominator of the Todd polynomials given in Hirzebruch's book [@Hirzebruch Lemmas 1.5.2 and 1.7.3] without proof and quoted from the paper [@Atiyah-Hirzebruch].

The proof of the above theorem (which actually implies Theorem [\[thm.denom\]](#thm.denom){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.denom"}) is similar to the proof of Theorem [\[thm.denom\]](#thm.denom){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.denom"} and is omitted.

Note that the "connected denominators" $D_n^\mathrm{conn}$ are considerably smaller than the "additive denominators" $D_n$: $D_n/n!$ is an integer growing exponentially like $(44.621{\dots}+o(1))^n$, as already mentioned, while $D_n^\mathrm{conn}/(n+2)!$ is an integer of subexponential growth.

The final remarks concern the relation of the above results with the known integrality properties of elements of the Habiro ring. However, as we know, this remaining case is actually simpler because it belongs to the Habiro ring and therefore satisfies , and more generally .
