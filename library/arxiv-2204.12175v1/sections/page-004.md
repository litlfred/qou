3. Explanations
Since the anomalies ﬁt into the coherent patterns of LFUV beyond the SM, it is natural to ask
how they could be explained in terms of new particles and new interactions. For a consistent
renormalizable extension of the SM, only scalars bosons (spin 0), fermions (spin 1/2) and vectors
bosons4 (spin 1) are at disposal. Here, we will consider four classes of SM extensions (focusing
on heavy NP realized above the EW breaking scale):
• Leptoquarks (LQs): Scalar or vector particles that carry color and couple directly to a
quark and a lepton [82, 83]. They were ﬁrst proposed in the context of the Pati-Salam
model [84], Grand Uniﬁed Theories (GUTs) [85–89] and in the R-parity violating MSSM
(see e.g. Ref. [90] for a review).
• W ′ bosons: Singly charged, QCD neutral vector particles. They appear as Kaluza Klein
excitations of the SM W in composite [91, 92] or extra-dimensional models [93] as well as
in models with additional SU(2) factors, including left-right-symmetric models [94].
• Z′ bosons: Neutral (color and electric charge) vector bosons. They can be singlets under
SU(2)L but also neutral components of an SU(2)L multiplet. Again, they can be resonances
of the SM Z or originate from an abelian symmetry like B −L [84] or gauged ﬂavour
symmetries [95,96].
• New scalars and fermions (S/F): In this category we pigeonhole all vector-like fermions as
well as all scalar particles that are not LQs. Vector-like fermions appear in GUTs [97–99],
composite models or models with extra dimensions [100, 101] and vector-like leptons are
involved in the type I [102,103] and type III [104] seesaw mechanisms. New fermions and
scalars could be supersymmetric partners of SM particles [105] and the MSSM also includes
additional Higgses like in the 2HDMs [106,107].
b →sℓ+ℓ−: As these processes are suppressed in the SM, the required O(20%) NP eﬀect
(w.r.t. the SM) is small and we have three diﬀerent classes of solutions: 1) A Z′ boson with
a ﬂavour violating couplings to bottom and strange quarks can account for the anomaly at
tree-level [108–113]. Even though one in general expects an eﬀect in Bs −¯Bs mixing [114], and
the Z′ can be produced resonantly at the LHC (see e.g. [115]), such a solution is viable if the
couplings to ﬁrst generations quarks are suppressed and the models possesses an approximate
global U(2) ﬂavour symmetry to protect it from K0−¯K0 and D0−¯D0 mixing [116]. 2) Three LQ
representations, the scalar triplet (S1), vector singlet (U1) and vector triplet (S3), can already
at tree-level give a good ﬁt to b →sℓ+ℓ−data [117, 118]. The couplings to electrons should
be small because of µ →eγ [119] and µ →e conversion. As eﬀects in b →sγ and Bs −¯Bs
mixing are only generated at the loop-level, these processes are not constraining and also the
LHC bounds are weak [120] as no coupling to ﬁrst generation quarks are needed. 3) Loop-eﬀect
involving box diagrams with new heavy scalars and fermions [121–124] or top quarks [125] (either
in combinations with LQs [126] or a Z′ [127]) can account for the anomaly if NP is at or below
the TeV scale.
b →cτν: As this transition is tree-level mediated in the SM, also a tree-level NP contribution
is necessary to obtain the desired eﬀect of 10% w.r.t the SM (for heavy NP with perturbative
couplings). As it is a charged current process, the only possibilities are charged Higgses [128–130],
W ′ bosons [131–137] (with or without right-handed neutrinos) or LQs [138–141]. The ﬁrst two
options are disfavoured by the Bc lifetime [142,143] and/or LHC searches [131,132], leaving LQ
as the best option for a full explanation. However, also in this case, a solution is not trivial, as
constraints from Bs −¯Bs mixing, B →K∗νν and LHC searches must be avoided. Therefore,
either the SU(2)L singlet vector LQ [144–150] or the singlet-triplet model [151–153] that can
avoid these constraints are particularly interesting.
4 Here a consistent extension requires some spontaneous symmetry breaking via a Higgs mechanism or some
composite or extra-dimensional dynamics.
