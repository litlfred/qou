In the paper [@steiner] we gave the following description of the angles of ideal polyhedra in $\mathbf{H}^3$: let $P$ be a combinatorial polyhedron, and let $A: E(P) \rightarrow [0, \pi)$ be a function. Then, there exists an ideal polyhedron combinatorially equivalent to $P$, such that the exterior angle at every edge $e$ is given by $A(e)$ if and only if the sum of $A(e)$ over all edges adjacent to a vertex of $P$ is equal to $2\pi,$ while the sum of $A(e)$ over any *nontrivial cutset* of edges of $P$ (that is, a collection of edges which separates the $1$-skeleton of $P$, but which are not all adjacent to the same vertex) is strictly greater than $2\pi.$ Furthermore, it was shown in [@rvol] that the dihedral angles determine the ideal polyhedron up to congruence.

It was observed in [@rivid; @rvol] that this was a special case of the problem of characterizing the *Delaunay tessellations* of singular Euclidean surfaces -- there is a canonical way to associate ideal polyhedra to Delaunay triangulations of a convex flat disk with convex polygonal boundary. The general situation is described in detail below, but one of the goals of this paper is to extend the characterization above to the completely general case of singular Euclidean surfaces with boundary. This is duly done, see, eg, Theorems [\[mainthm\]](#mainthm){reference-type="ref" reference="mainthm"}, [\[versimple\]](#versimple){reference-type="ref" reference="versimple"}, [\[andreevthm\]](#andreevthm){reference-type="ref" reference="andreevthm"}:

Consider a surface $S$, equipped with a Euclidean (or, more generally, a similarity) structure $E$, possibly with cone singularities. Assume that there is a discrete collection $P=\{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$ of distinguished points on $S$, and assume that $P$ contains the cone points of $S$. There is a canonical tessellation attached to the triple $(S, E, P)$ -- the so-called Delaunay tessellation (see, *eg*, [@be; @rvol]). The moduli space $\mathcal{M}$ of such triples is then naturally decomposed into disjoint subsets $\mathcal{M}_T$, corresponding to the different combinatorial types $T$ of the Delaunay tessellation. This is a canonical decomposition of $\mathcal{M}$. In the paper [@rvol] I studied the subsets $\mathcal{M}_T$, and showed that the *dihedral angles* of the Delaunay triangulation are natural coordinates (*moduli*) for $\mathcal{M}_T$, which induce on $\mathcal{M}_T$ the structure of a convex polytope.

The aformentioned decompositon of moduli space then becomes a polyhedral complex, the top-dimensional cells of which corresponds to Delaunay tessellations which are triangulations, while pairs of adjacent top-dimensional cells differ combinatorially by a diagonal flip. This decomposition is closely related to the well-known Harer complex (see, eg, [@harer]). As mentioned above, the top-dimensional cells of this complex are identified along some of their lower-dimensional faces, while other lower dimensional faces correspond to degenerations of the Euclidean structures of $(S, E, P)$. It is then clear that the polyhedral structure of the cells $\mathcal{M}_T$ is of considerable interest. However, in [@rvol] only an indirect description was given -- $\mathcal{M}_T$ was shown to be a convex polytope by virtue of being an image of another convex polytope under a fairly complicated linear map. The methods of [@steiner] come from hyperbolic geometry and are based on the study of dihedral angles of *compact* hyperbolic polyhedra in [@rh], so do not easily generalize to the case of general singular Euclidean and similarity structures alluded to above. In the current paper, methods of mathematical programming and the results of [@rvol] are used to give a completely general extension of the result of [@steiner] (described in the beginning of this Introduction) to Delaunay triangulations of arbitrary singular surfaces (Theorems [\[mainthm\]](#mainthm){reference-type="ref" reference="mainthm"}, [\[versimple\]](#versimple){reference-type="ref" reference="versimple"}, [\[andreevthm\]](#andreevthm){reference-type="ref" reference="andreevthm"}). Since the arguments do not depend on the results of [@steiner], we have a different, essentially combinatorial, proof of a principal result (Theorem 0.1) of that paper (Theorem [\[andreevthm\]](#andreevthm){reference-type="ref" reference="andreevthm"} here). The other result of [@steiner] -- the characterization of finite-volume polyhedra -- is, seemingly, not accessible by current methods. The above-mentioned result permit us to get good understanding of the boundary structure of the cells $\mathcal{M}_T$, and, consequently, of $\mathcal{M}$ itself. Since $\mathcal{M}_T$ fibers (in multiple ways) over the moduli space of finite area hyperbolic structures on $S$, some information is obtained about the latter moduli space.

In addition, the methods, combined with a geometric estimate, allow us to give a description of dihedral angles of Delaunay tessellations of $(S, E, P)$, where $(S, P)$ is not necessarily of finite topological type (Theorem [\[infmain\]](#infmain){reference-type="ref" reference="infmain"}). This stops well short of solving the moduli problem, unlike in the finite case, but a conjectural picture seems fairly clear.

The methods are also brought to bear onto some questions in combinatorial geometry, and to provide efficient algorithms for solving the "inverse problem" of determining when a combinatorial complex, or a combinatorial complex equipped with dihedral angle data, can be realized as the Delaunay tessellation of a singular Euclidean surface.

In addition, we use our methods to prove some observations of Casson on ideally triangulated $3$-manifolds. That subject is not so far removed from the geometry of similarity and Euclidean structures on surfaces. Indeed, the basic idea of [@rvol] is to study the similarity and Euclidean structures by means of constructing a canonical hyperbolic polyhedral complex as a "cone" over the surface being studied.

The plan of the paper is as follows: In section [\[oldstuff\]](#oldstuff){reference-type="ref" reference="oldstuff"} the relevant definitions and results of [@rvol] are recalled. In section [\[necessary\]](#necessary){reference-type="ref" reference="necessary"} we describe a set of constraints which must be satisfied by the dihedral angles of any (not necessarily Delaunay) triangulation. In section [\[suff\]](#suff){reference-type="ref" reference="suff"} we show that these constraints are actually sufficient under the assumption that the triangulation is Delaunay, and refine them to a minimal set of constraints. In section [\[ideal\]](#ideal){reference-type="ref" reference="ideal"} we show how the results apply to ideal polyhedra, and in particular to characterize infinite ideal polyhedra. In section [\[moduli\]](#moduli){reference-type="ref" reference="moduli"} we comment on the boundary structure of the moduli space of singular Euclidean structures, and describe a correspondence between the Euclidean and hyperbolic structures, which hopefully clarifies the picture. In section [\[lhyp\]](#lhyp){reference-type="ref" reference="lhyp"} we apply the methods of section [\[suff\]](#suff){reference-type="ref" reference="suff"} to the study of ideal triangulations of $3$-manifolds. In section [\[nflow\]](#nflow){reference-type="ref" reference="nflow"} we give a network flow interpretation of the results of section [\[dihsec\]](#dihsec){reference-type="ref" reference="dihsec"}. In addition to the intrinsic interest, this allows us to give efficient algorithms for deciding whether a weighed graph is the 1-skeleton of a Delaunay triangulation (with weights being the dihedral angles). These computational issues are discussed in section [\[complexsec\]](#complexsec){reference-type="ref" reference="complexsec"}. In section [\[someapps\]](#someapps){reference-type="ref" reference="someapps"} we discuss some combinatorial-geometric applications of the results of section [\[dihsec\]](#dihsec){reference-type="ref" reference="dihsec"}.
