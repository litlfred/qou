::: proposition
[]{#prop:CumulantsGrowSlow label="prop:CumulantsGrowSlow"} We use the notations and assumptions of [\[theo:lln\]](#theo:lln){reference-type="ref" reference="theo:lln"}. For the random variable $\mathcal{R}_l=\mathcal{R}_l(\lambda_n)$ we define $$\label{eq:free-cumulants-exist}
    r_l := \lim_{n\to\infty} {n^{- \frac{l}{2}}}\  \mathbb{E}_{\chi_n} \mathcal{R}_l.$$

Then there exists some constant $C$ such that $$\label{eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow}
    |r_l| \leq C^{l}\ l^{ml}$$ holds true for each integer $l\geq 2$, where $m$ is given by [\[eq:what-is-m\]](#eq:what-is-m){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:what-is-m"}.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* For a given value of $n$ we will investigate the collection of random variables $$\label{eq:my-collection}
    \left( {n^{- \frac{l}{2}}} \mathcal{R}_l \right)_{l\geq 2}.$$ Approximate factorization property, by [\[def:approx-factorization-charactersB\]](#def:approx-factorization-charactersB){reference-type="ref" reference="def:approx-factorization-charactersB"}[\[item:afp2\]](#item:afp2){reference-type="ref" reference="item:afp2"}[\[item:cumulants-classic-polynomial\]](#item:cumulants-classic-polynomial){reference-type="ref" reference="item:cumulants-classic-polynomial"}, implies (for $\ell=1$) that the limit [\[eq:free-cumulants-exist\]](#eq:free-cumulants-exist){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:free-cumulants-exist"} exists and is finite for each integer $l \geq 2$. Furthermore, it implies that each cumulant $\kappa_{\ell}$ for $\ell\geq 2$ of the random variables [\[eq:my-collection\]](#eq:my-collection){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:my-collection"} converges to zero as $n\to\infty$.

Each moment (i.e. the mean value of a product of some random variables) can be expressed as a polynomial in the cumulants of the individual random variables via *"moment-cumulant formula"*. Thus by vanishing of the higher cumulants which correspond to $\ell\geq 2$, the expected value of a product of free cumulants approximately factorizes: $$\label{eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice}
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n^{\frac{k_1+\cdots+k_\ell}{2}}} 
    \ \mathbb{E}_{\chi_n}\left[ \mathcal{R}_{k_1} \cdots \mathcal{R}_{k_\ell} \right]  =
    r_{k_1} \cdots r_{k_\ell}.$$

We consider first the case $g=0$, $m=1$. Let us divide both sides of [\[eq:kerov-polynomial-subtle\]](#eq:kerov-polynomial-subtle){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:kerov-polynomial-subtle"} by $n^{\frac{l+1}{2}}$ and take the mean value $\mathbb{E}_{\chi_n}$. By taking the limit $n\to\infty$ and using [\[eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice\]](#eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice"} we obtain in this way $$r_{l+1}=a_{l+1}$$ and the claim follows immediately.

From the following on we consider the generic case $g\neq 0$ and $m=2$. Analogously as above, let us divide both sides of [\[eq:Sniady-proved-this\]](#eq:Sniady-proved-this){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:Sniady-proved-this"} by $n^{\frac{l+1}{2}}$ and take the mean value $\mathbb{E}_{\chi_n}$. By taking the limit $n\to\infty$ and using [\[eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice\]](#eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:free-cumulants-behave-nice"} we obtain in this way $$\label{eq:totoro}
    a_{l+1} = \sum_{[(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)]\in\mathcal{X}_l/\sim} 
    g^{l+1-|C(\sigma_1)|-|C(\sigma_2)|} 
    \sum_{\substack{q\colon C(\sigma_2)\to\{2,3,\dots\} \\
            \text{$(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,q)$ is an expander}}}
    \prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} r_{q(c)}.$$ We shall cluster the summands according to the parameters $u$ and $v$ given by $$u:= \|\sigma_1\| \qquad \text{and} \qquad 
    v:=l+1-|C(\sigma_1)|-|C(\sigma_2)|.$$

In the following we will show that from the transitivity requirement in the definition of $\mathcal{X}_l$ it follows that $$v\geq 0.$$ Indeed, let us construct a bipartite graph $G = (V_\circ \sqcup
    V_\bullet,E)$ with the vertices corresponding to the cycles of $\sigma_1$ and the cycles of $\sigma_2$: $V_\circ =
    C(\sigma_1), V_\bullet = C(\sigma_2)$; we connect two vertices by an edge if the corresponding cycles are not disjoint, that is $e = (c_1,c_2)\in E$ if $c_1 \cap c_2 \neq \emptyset$. Then, the transitivity of the action of the group generated by $\sigma_1,\sigma_2\in\mathfrak{S}(l)$ means precisely that the graph $G$ is connected. Since the number of edges of $G$ is bounded from above by $l$ it follows that the number of vertices of $G$, which is equal to $|C(\sigma_1)|+|C(\sigma_2)|$, cannot exceed $l+1$, which gives the required inequality.

Furthermore, the contribution of the terms for which the equality $v=0$ holds true corresponds to the specialization $g=0$; by revisiting [\[eq:kerov-polynomial-subtle\]](#eq:kerov-polynomial-subtle){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:kerov-polynomial-subtle"} it follows that this contribution is equal to $r_{l+1}$ which corresponds to the unique equivalence class $$\big\{ (\operatorname{id},\sigma_2) : 
    \sigma_2\in\mathfrak{S}(l) \text{ is such that $|C(\sigma_2)|=1$} \big\}$$ for which $u=0$ and $v=0$. It is easy to check that it is the unique summand for which $u=0$ (since the latter condition is equivalent to $\sigma_1=\operatorname{id}$). By singling out this particular summand, [\[eq:totoro\]](#eq:totoro){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:totoro"} can be transformed to $$\label{eq:induction-step}
    r_{l+1} = a_{l+1} -
    \sum_{\substack{u\geq 1 \\ v\geq 1}}
    \sum_{\substack{[(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)]\in\mathcal{X}_l/\sim \\ \|\sigma_1\|=u\\ 
            l+1-|C(\sigma_1)|-|C(\sigma_2)|=v}} 
    g^{v} 
    \sum_{\substack{q\colon C(\sigma_2)\to\{2,3,\dots\} \\
            \text{$(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,q)$ is an expander}}}
    \prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} r_{q(c)}.$$

With the notations used in [\[eq:induction-step\]](#eq:induction-step){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:induction-step"} $$\label{eq:sum-of-qs}
    v+ \sum_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} q(c) = v+ |C(\sigma_1)|+|C(\sigma_2)|=l+1$$ which implies that for each $C>0$ we have that $$\begin{gathered}
    \label{eq:induction-step-2}
    \left| \frac{ r_{l+1}}{C^{l+1}\ (l+1)^{2(l+1)}} \right|  \leq 
    \left| \frac{a_{l+1}}{C^{l+1}\ (l+1)^{2(l+1)}} \right| + \\
    \sum_{\substack{u\geq 1 \\ v\geq 1}} 
    \sum_{\substack{[(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)]\in\mathcal{X}_l/\sim \\ \|\sigma_1\|=u\\ l+1-|C(\sigma_1)|-|C(\sigma_2)|=v}} 
    \left(\frac{|g|}{C\ (l+1)^2}\right)^{v} \times \\ \times
    \sum_{\substack{q\colon C(\sigma_2) \to\{2,3,\dots\} \\
            \text{$(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,q)$ is an expander}}}
    \prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} \left| \frac{r_{q(c)}}{C^{q(c)}\ (l+1)^{2 q(c)}} \right|.
    
\end{gathered}$$

By [\[lem:not-many-permutations\]](#lem:not-many-permutations){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:not-many-permutations"} and [\[eq:takietam\]](#eq:takietam){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:takietam"}, for each pair of integers $u,v\geq 1$ the number of equivalence classes $[(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)]$ which could possibly contribute to the above sum is bounded from above by $$\frac{l^{2u}}{u!}  (l+1)^{|C(\sigma_2)|-1} = \frac{l^{2u}}{u!}  (l+1)^{u-v} \leq \frac{(l+1)^{3u-v}}{u!}.$$

For each representative $(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$ of an equivalence class the number of functions $q\colon C(\sigma_2)\to\{2,3,\dots\}$ which fulfill [\[eq:sum-of-qs\]](#eq:sum-of-qs){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:sum-of-qs"} is bounded from above by $$(l+1)^{|C(\sigma_2)|-1}= (l+1)^{u-v}.$$

Our strategy is to prove [\[eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow\]](#eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow"} for $m=2$ by induction over $l\geq 2$. It is trivial to check that $r_2 = 1$ always holds true and thus the induction base $l=2$ is valid if $C\geq 1$. We shall assume that that [\[eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow\]](#eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:free-cumulants-grow-slow"} holds true for $2\leq k\leq l$. The value of the constant $C$ will be specified at the end of the proof in such a way that each induction step can be justified. The induction hypothesis implies that $$\prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} \left| r_{q(c)} \right| \leq C^{l+1-v}  \prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} q(c)^{2q(c)}.$$

We claim that the following inequality holds true: $$\label{eq:convex}
    \prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} q(c)^{2q(c)} \leq 2^{4(u-v)}\  (l+1)^{2\big( l+1-v-2(u-v)\big)}.$$ Indeed, the logarithm of the left-hand side is a convex function $$\mathbb{R}_+^{|C(\sigma_2)|} \ni \big( q(c) : c\in C(\sigma_2) \big) \mapsto 2 \sum_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} {q(c)}\log q(c);$$ its supremum over the simplex given by inequalities $q(c)\geq 2$ and the equality [\[eq:sum-of-qs\]](#eq:sum-of-qs){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:sum-of-qs"} is attained in one of the simplex vertices which corresponds to $$\big( q(c) \big)_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} = \big(l+1-v-2(u-v),\underbrace{2,\dots,2}_{\text{$|C(\sigma_2)|-1=u-v$ times}} \big);$$ this concludes the proof of [\[eq:convex\]](#eq:convex){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:convex"}.

In this way we proved that $$\prod_{c\in C(\sigma_2)} \left| \frac{r_{q(c)}}{C^{q(c)} (l+1)^{2 q(c)}} \right| \leq 
    
    
    \left( \frac{2}{l+1} \right)^{4(u-v)}.$$

It follows that the right-hand side of [\[eq:induction-step-2\]](#eq:induction-step-2){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:induction-step-2"} is bounded from above by $$\begin{gathered}
    \left| \frac{a_{l+1}}{C^{l+1}\ (l+1)^{2(l+1)}} \right| + \\
    \shoveright{\sum_{\substack{u\geq 1 \\ v\geq 1}} 
        \frac{(l+1)^{3u-v}}{u!} (l+1)^{u-v} \left(\frac{|g|}{C\ (l+1)^2}\right)^{v}
        \left( \frac{2}{l+1} \right)^{4(u-v)} \leq} \\
    \shoveleft{\left| \frac{a_{l+1}}{C^{l+1}\ (l+1)^{2(l+1)}} \right| + 
        \sum_{u\geq 1} 
        \frac{2^{4u}}{u!} \sum_{v\geq 1} \left(\frac{|g|}{2^4 C}\right)^{v} = }\\
    \left| \frac{a_{l+1}}{C^{l+1}\ (l+1)^{2(l+1)}} \right| + 
    (e^{16}-1) \frac{ \frac{|g|}{2^4 C} }{1 - \frac{|g|}{2^4 C}}
    
\end{gathered}$$ for $\frac{|g|}{2^4 C}<1$. The right-hand side tends to zero uniformly over $l$ as $C\to\infty$; there exists therefore some $C$ such that the right-hand side is smaller than $1$. Such a choice of $C$ assures that each inductive step is justified. This concludes the proof. ◻
