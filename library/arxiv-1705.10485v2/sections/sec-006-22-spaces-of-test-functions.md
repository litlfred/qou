[]{#subsec:distributions label="subsec:distributions"} Until the end of Section [\[sec:testfunctions\]](#sec:testfunctions){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:testfunctions"}, all the spaces of functions considered will be spaces of complex valued functions on the real line. If $f \in \mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$, we denote its Fourier transform $$\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}} \E^{\I x\xi}\,f(x)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}.$$ Recall that the Schwartz space $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ is by definition the space of infinitely differentiable functions whose derivatives tend to $0$ at infinity faster than any power of $x$. Restricted to $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$, the Fourier transform is an automorphism, and it satisfies the Parseval formula $$\forall f,g \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}),\\ \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x) \,\overline{g(x)}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathbb{R}} \widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi}.$$ We refer to [@Lang93 Chapter VIII] and [@Rud91 Part II] for a proof of this formula, and for the theory of Fourier transforms. The Parseval formula allows to extend by duality and/or density the Fourier transform to other spaces of functions or distributions. In particular, if $f \in \mathrm{L}^2(\mathbb{R})$, then its Fourier transform $\widehat{f}$ is well defined in $\mathrm{L}^2(\mathbb{R})$, although in general the integral $\int_{\mathbb{R}} \E^{\I x\xi}\,f(x)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}$ does not converge; and we have again the Parseval formula $$\forall f,g \in \mathrm{L}^2(\mathbb{R}),\\ \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x) \,\overline{g(x)}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\mathbb{R}} \widehat{f}(\xi)\,\overline{\widehat{g}(\xi)}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi},$$ which amounts to the fact that $f \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\widehat{f}$ is an isometry of $\mathrm{L}^2(\mathbb{R})$ (see [@Rud91 §7.9]).

We denote $\mathscr{M}^1(\mathbb{R})$ the set of probability measures on Borel subsets of $\mathbb{R}$. In the sequel, we will need to apply a variant of Parseval's formula, where $\overline{g(x)}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}$ is replaced by $\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$, with $\mu$ in $\mathscr{M}^1(\mathbb{R})$. This is given in the following lemma (see [@Stroock Lemma 2.3.3], or [@Mal95 p. 134]).

::: lemma
[]{#lem:parsevalmeasures label="lem:parsevalmeasures"} For any function $f \in \mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$ with $\widehat{f} \in \mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$, and any Borel probability measure $\mu \in \mathscr{M}^{1}(\mathbb{R})$, the pairing $\left\langle \mu\vphantom{f}\,\right |\left.f \vphantom{\mu}\right\rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x)\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$ is well defined, and the Parseval formula holds: $$\int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x)\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}} \widehat{f}(\xi)\,\widehat{\mu}(-\xi)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi},$$ where $\widehat{\mu}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \E^{\I \xi x}\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$. The formula also holds for finite signed measures.
:::

Let us now introduce two adequate spaces of test functions, for which we shall be able to prove speed of convergence estimates. We first consider functions $f \in \mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$ with compactly supported Fourier transforms:

::: definition
[]{#def:T0 label="def:T0"} We call *smooth test function of order $0$*, or simply *smooth test function* an element $f \in \mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$ whose Fourier transform is compactly supported. We denote $\mathscr{T}_0(\mathbb{R})$ the subspace of $\mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$ that consists in smooth test functions; it is an ideal for the convolution product.
:::

::: example
If $$\mathrm{sinc}(x):=\frac{\sin x}{x}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^1 \E^{\I x\xi}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi},$$ then by Fourier inversion $\widehat{\mathrm{sinc}}(\xi) = \pi\,1_{|\xi| \leq 1}$ is compactly supported on $[-1,1]$. Therefore, $f(x) = (\mathrm{sinc}(x))^2$ is an element of $\mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$ whose Fourier transform is compactly supported on $[-1,1]+[-1,1]=[-2,2]$, and $f \in \mathscr{T}_0(\mathbb{R})$.
:::

Let us comment a bit Definition [\[def:T0\]](#def:T0){reference-type="ref" reference="def:T0"}. If $f$ is in $\mathscr{T}_0(\mathbb{R})$, then its Fourier transform $\widehat{f}$ is bounded by $\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^1}$ and vanishes outside an interval $[-C,C]$, so $\widehat{f} \in \mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R})$. Since $f$ and $\widehat{f}$ are integrable, we can apply Lemma [\[lem:parsevalmeasures\]](#lem:parsevalmeasures){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:parsevalmeasures"} with $f$. Moreover, $f$ is then known to satisfy the Fourier inversion formula (see [@Rud91 §7.7]): $$f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}} \widehat{f}(\xi)\,\E^{-\I \xi x}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi}.$$ As the integral above is in fact on a compact interval $[-C,C]$, the standard convergence theorems ensure that $f$ is infinitely differentiable in $x$, hence the term \"smooth\". Also, by applying the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma to the continuous compactly supported functions $\xi \mapsto (-\I \xi)^k\,\widehat{f}(\xi)$, one sees that $f(x)$ and all its derivatives $f^{(k)}(x)$ go to $0$ as $x$ goes to infinity. To conclude, $\mathscr{T}_0(\mathbb{R})$ is included in the space $\mathscr{C}_0^\infty(\mathbb{R})$ of smooth functions whose derivatives all vanish at infinity.

Actually, we will need to work with more general test functions, defined by using the theory of tempered distributions. We endow the Schwartz space $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ of smooth rapidly decreasing functions with its usual topology of metrizable locally convex topological vector space, defined by the family of semi-norms $$\|f\|_{k,l} = \sum_{a \leq k}\sum_{b \leq l}\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |x^a \,(\partial^b f)(x)| .$$ We recall that a tempered distribution $\psi$ is a continuous linear form $\psi : \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{C}$. The value of a tempered distribution $\psi$ on a smooth function $f$ will be denoted $\psi(f)$ or $\left\langle \psi\vphantom{f}\,\right |\left.f \vphantom{\psi}\right\rangle$. The space of all tempered distributions is classically denoted $\mathscr{S}'(\mathbb{R})$, and it is endowed with the $*$-weak topology. The spaces of integrable functions, of square integrable functions and of probability measures can all be embedded in the space $\mathscr{S}'(\mathbb{R})$ as follows: if $f$ is a function in $\mathrm{L}^1(\mathbb{R}) \cup \mathrm{L}^2(\mathbb{R})$, or if $\mu$ is in $\mathscr{M}^{1}(\mathbb{R})$, then we associate to them the distributions $$\left\langle f\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{f}\right\rangle=\int_\mathbb{R}f(x) g(x) \,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}\qquad;\qquad \left\langle \mu\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\mu}\right\rangle = \int_\mathbb{R}g(x)\, \mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}).$$ We then say that these distributions are represented by the function $f$ and by the measure $\mu$.

The Fourier transform of a tempered distribution $\psi$ is defined by duality: it is the unique tempered distribution $\widehat{\psi}$ such that $$\left\langle \widehat{\psi}\vphantom{f}\,\right |\left.f \vphantom{\widehat{\psi}}\right\rangle = \left\langle \psi\vphantom{\widehat{f}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{f} \vphantom{\psi}\right\rangle$$ for any $f \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$. This definition agrees with the previous definitions of Fourier transforms for integrable functions, square integrable functions, or probability measures (all these elements can be paired with Schwartz functions). Similarly, if $\psi$ is a tempered distribution, then one can also define by duality its derivative: thus, $\partial\psi$ is the unique tempered distribution such that $$\left\langle \partial\psi\vphantom{f}\,\right |\left.f \vphantom{\partial\psi}\right\rangle = -\left\langle \psi\vphantom{\partial f}\,\right |\left.\partial f \vphantom{\psi}\right\rangle$$ for any $f \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$. The definition agrees with the usual one when $\psi$ comes from a derivable function, by the integration by parts formula. On the other hand, Fourier transform and derivation define linear endomorphisms of $\mathscr{S}'(\mathbb{R})$; also note that the Fourier transform is bijective.

::: definition
A *smooth test function of order $1$*, or *smooth test distribution* is a distribution $f \in \mathscr{S}'(\mathbb{R})$, such that $\partial f$ is in $\mathscr{T}_0(\mathbb{R})$, that is to say that the distribution $\partial f$ can be represented by an integrable function with compactly supported Fourier transform. We denote $\mathscr{T}_1(\mathbb{R})$ the space of smooth test distributions.
:::

We now discuss Parseval's formula for functions in $\mathscr{T}_1(\mathbb{R})$.

::: proposition
[]{#prop:parsevaldistribution label="prop:parsevaldistribution"} Any smooth test distribution $f \in \mathscr{T}_1(\mathbb{R})$ can be represented by a bounded function in $\mathscr{C}^\infty(\mathbb{R})$. Moreover, for any smooth test distribution in $\mathscr{T}_1(\mathbb{R})$:

1.  []{#item:distribution1 label="item:distribution1"} If $\mu$ is a Borel probability measure, then the pairing $\left\langle \mu\vphantom{f}\,\right |\left.f \vphantom{\mu}\right\rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x)\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$ is well defined.

2.  []{#item:distribution2 label="item:distribution2"} The tempered distribution $\widehat{f}$ can be paired with the Fourier transform $\widehat{\mu}$ of a probability measure with finite first moment, in a way that extends the pairing between $\mathscr{S}'(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ when $\mu$ (and therefore $\widehat{\mu}$) is given by a Schwartz density.

3.  []{#item:distribution3 label="item:distribution3"} The Parseval formula holds: if $f \in \mathscr{T}_1(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mu$ has finite expectation, then $$\left\langle \mu\vphantom{f}\,\right |\left.f \vphantom{\mu}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\left\langle \widehat{f}\vphantom{\overline{\widehat{\mu}}}\,\right |\left.\overline{\widehat{\mu}} \vphantom{\widehat{f}}\right\rangle .$$
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* We start by giving a better description of the tempered distributions $f$ and $\widehat{f}$. Denote $\phi  = \partial f$; by assumption, this tempered distribution can be represented by a function $\phi \in \mathscr{T}_0(\mathbb{R})$, which in particular is of class $\mathscr{C}^\infty$ and integrable. Set $$\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{f}(x) &= \int_{y=0}^x \phi(y)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y}.
\end{aligned}$$ This is a function of class $\mathscr{C}^\infty$, whose derivative is $\phi$, and which is bounded since $\phi$ is integrable. Therefore, it is a tempered distribution, and for any $g\in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$, $$\left\langle \partial f\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\partial f}\right\rangle = \left\langle \phi\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\phi}\right\rangle = \left\langle \partial\widetilde{f}\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\partial\widetilde{f}}\right\rangle.$$ We conclude that $\partial(f-\widetilde{f})$ is the zero distribution. It is then a standard result that, given a tempered distribution $\psi$, one has $\partial \psi = 0$ if and only if $\psi$ can be represented a constant. So, $$f(x) = \int_{y=0}^x \phi(y)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y} + f(0).$$ This shows in particular that $f$ is a smooth bounded function.

A similar description can be provided for $\widehat{f}$. Recall that the principal value distribution, denoted $\mathrm{pv}(\frac{1}{x})$, is the tempered distribution defined for any $g \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ by $$\left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)}\right\rangle = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0} \left(\int_{|x|\geq \varepsilon} \frac{g(x)}{x}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}\right).$$ The existence of the limit is easily proved by making a Taylor expansion of $g$ around $0$. Denote $$\begin{aligned}
\mathscr{S}^{[1]} &= \{g \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\,|\,g(x) = x\,h(x) \text{ with }h \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\} ;\\
\mathscr{S}_{[1]} &= \{g \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\,|\,g = \partial h \text{ with }h \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})\} ;
\end{aligned}$$ the Fourier transform establishes an homeomorphism between $\mathscr{S}^{[1]}$ and $\mathscr{S}_{[1]}$, and the restriction of $\mathrm{pv}(\frac{1}{x})$ to $\mathscr{S}^{[1]}$ is $$\left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)}\right\rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \frac{g(x)}{x}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}.$$ Let $\widehat{g}(\xi)$ be an element of $\mathscr{S}^{[1]}$, which we write as $\widehat{g}(\xi) = (-\I\xi)\,\widehat{h}(\xi)$ for some $h \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$. This is equivalent to $g(x) = (\partial h)(x)$. Let us denote $g_-(x) = g(-x)$, $h_-(x) = h(-x)$, and $\mathrm{pv}(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi})$ the tempered distribution defined by $$\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right) = \I\, \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)\circ m_{\widehat{\phi}},$$ with $m_{\widehat{\phi}}:\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ equal to the multiplication by $\widehat{\phi}$. Then we can make the following computation: $$\begin{aligned}
\left\langle \widehat{f}\vphantom{\widehat{g}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{g} \vphantom{\widehat{f}}\right\rangle &= \left\langle f\vphantom{\widehat{\widehat{g}}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{\widehat{g}} \vphantom{f}\right\rangle = 2\pi\,\left\langle f\vphantom{g_-}\,\right |\left.g_- \vphantom{f}\right\rangle = - 2\pi\, \left\langle f\vphantom{\partial h_-}\,\right |\left.\partial h_- \vphantom{f}\right\rangle = 2\pi\, \left\langle \phi\vphantom{h_-}\,\right |\left.h_- \vphantom{\phi}\right\rangle = \left\langle \phi\vphantom{\widehat{\widehat{h}}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{\widehat{h}} \vphantom{\phi}\right\rangle\\
&= \left\langle \widehat{\phi}\vphantom{\widehat{h}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{h} \vphantom{\widehat{\phi}}\right\rangle = \left\langle \widehat{\phi}\vphantom{\frac{\I \widehat{g}(\xi)}{\xi}}\,\right |\left.\frac{\I \widehat{g}(\xi)}{\xi} \vphantom{\widehat{\phi}}\right\rangle = \left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{\widehat{g}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{g} \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle.
\end{aligned}$$ Thus, the tempered distributions $\widehat{f}$ and $\mathrm{pv}(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi})$ agree on the codimension $1$ subspace $\mathscr{S}^{[1]}$ of $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$. However, $\mathscr{S}^{[1]}$ is also the space of functions in $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ that vanish at $0$, so, if $g_0$ is any function in $\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$ such that $g_0(0)=1$, then for $g \in \mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})$, $$\begin{aligned}
\left\langle \widehat{f}\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\widehat{f}}\right\rangle &= \left\langle \widehat{f}\vphantom{g-g(0)g_0}\,\right |\left.g-g(0)g_0 \vphantom{\widehat{f}}\right\rangle + g(0)\left\langle \widehat{f}\vphantom{g_0}\,\right |\left.g_0 \vphantom{\widehat{f}}\right\rangle \\
&= \left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{g-g(0)g_0}\,\right |\left.g-g(0)g_0 \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle + \left\langle \widehat{f}\vphantom{g_0}\,\right |\left.g_0 \vphantom{\widehat{f}}\right\rangle\,\left\langle \delta_0\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\delta_0}\right\rangle \\
&= \left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle + \left(\left\langle \widehat{f}-\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{g_0}\,\right |\left.g_0 \vphantom{\widehat{f}-\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle\right)\,\left\langle \delta_0\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\delta_0}\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)+ L\,\delta_0\vphantom{g}\,\right |\left.g \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)+ L\,\delta_0}\right\rangle 
\end{aligned}$$ where $L$ is some constant. Thus, $$\widehat{f}(\xi) = \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)}{\xi}\right)+ L\,\delta_0$$ and a computation against test functions shows that $$L = 2\pi f(0) - \I\,\left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{\widehat{\phi}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{\phi} \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle.$$ The three parts of the proposition are now easily proven. For [\[item:distribution1\]](#item:distribution1){reference-type="ref" reference="item:distribution1"}, since $f(x)$ is smooth and bounded, we can indeed consider the convergent integral $\int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x)\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$. For [\[item:distribution2\]](#item:distribution2){reference-type="ref" reference="item:distribution2"}, assuming that $\mu$ has a finite first moment, $\widehat{\mu}$ is a function of class $\mathscr{C}^1$ and with bounded derivative. The same holds for $\widehat{\phi}\widehat{\mu}$, and therefore, one can define $$\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(\xi) \widehat{\mu}(-\xi) \,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi} &= \I \,\left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{\widehat{\phi}\,\overline{\widehat{\mu}}}\,\right |\left.\widehat{\phi}\,\overline{\widehat{\mu}} \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle + L \\
&= \left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to \infty}\int_{|x|\geq \varepsilon} \frac{\I\,\widehat{\phi}(\xi)\,\widehat{\mu}(-\xi)}{\xi}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi} \right)+L.
\end{aligned}$$ Indeed, if $f \in \mathscr{C}^1(\mathbb{R})$, then $\lim_{\varepsilon\to \infty} \int_{1\geq |x|\geq \varepsilon} \frac{f(x)}{x}\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}$ always exists, as can be seen by replacing $f$ by its Taylor approximation at $0$. Finally, let us prove the Parseval formula [\[item:distribution3\]](#item:distribution3){reference-type="ref" reference="item:distribution3"}. The previous calculations show that $$\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}} \widehat{f}(\xi)\,\widehat{\mu}(-\xi)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi} &=  \frac{\I}{2\pi} \,\left\langle \mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)\vphantom{\widehat{\phi}\,\overline{\widehat{\mu}}-\widehat{\phi} }\,\right |\left.\widehat{\phi}\,\overline{\widehat{\mu}}-\widehat{\phi}  \vphantom{\mathrm{pv}\!\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)}\right\rangle + f(0) \\
&=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0} \left(\frac{\I}{2\pi}\int_{|\xi|\geq \varepsilon} \widehat{\phi}(\xi)\left(\frac{\widehat{\mu}(-\xi)-1}{\xi}\right)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi}\right) + f(0) \\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}} \widehat{\phi}(\xi)\left(\frac{\widehat{\mu}(-\xi)-1}{-\I\xi}\right)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{\xi} + f(0). 
\end{aligned}$$ Indeed, the function $\xi \mapsto \frac{\widehat{\mu}(-\xi)-1}{-\I\xi}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ and bounded, with value $\frac{\widehat{\mu}'(0)}{\I} = \int_{\mathbb{R}} x\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$ at $\xi=0$; it can therefore be integrated against the function $\widehat{\phi}$ which is integrable (and even with compact support). On the other hand, $$\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x}) &= \int_{x \in \mathbb{R}} \int_{y=0}^x \phi(y)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y}\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})+f(0) \\
&=\int_{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2} (1_{x>y>0} - 1_{x<y<0})\, \phi(y)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y}\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})+f(0) \\
&= \int_{y \in \mathbb{R}} \phi(y)\,F(y)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y}+f(0),\quad\text{with }F(y) = \mu((y,\infty)) - 1_{y\leq 0}.
\end{aligned}$$ One has $\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(y)|\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y} = \int_{y=0}^\infty\mu((y,\infty)) + \int_{y=-\infty}^0 \mu((-\infty,y)) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} |x|\,\mu(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})$, which is finite. In the integral above, we can therefore consider $F(y)\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{y}$ as a finite signed measure, and the Parseval formula applies by Lemma [\[lem:parsevalmeasures\]](#lem:parsevalmeasures){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:parsevalmeasures"}. One computes readily $$\widehat{F}(\xi) = \frac{\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-1}{\I\xi},$$ which ends the proof.0◻ ◻
:::

::: remark
[]{#rmk:parsevaldistribution label="rmk:parsevaldistribution"} The Parseval formula of Proposition [\[prop:parsevaldistribution\]](#prop:parsevaldistribution){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:parsevaldistribution"} extends readily to finite signed measures $\mu$ such that $\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|\,|\mu|(\!\,d\hspace*{-0.3mm}{x})<+\infty$. Actually, it is sufficient to have a finite signed measure $\mu$ such that $$\frac{\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-\widehat{\mu}(0)}{\xi^v}$$ is bounded in a vicinity of $0$, for some $v>0$. Then, $(\widehat{\mu}(\xi)-\widehat{\mu}(0))/\xi$ is integrable in a neighborhood of $0$. This ensures that the distribution $f(x)$ (respectively, the distribution $\widehat{f}(\xi)$) can be evaluated against the measure $\mu(x)$ (respectively, against $\widehat{\mu}(\xi)$), and then, the proof of Parseval's formula is analogous to the previous arguments.
:::
