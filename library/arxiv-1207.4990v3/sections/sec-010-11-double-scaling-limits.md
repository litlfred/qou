[]{#sec-ds label="sec-ds"} Of all the challenges in Toeplitz theory that arise from the Ising model, the deepest is the issue of the double-scaling limit (or in physicists' parlance, simply the scaling limit), $T\to T_c$ and $n\to\infty$. We have already mentioned the importance of this problem in physics. At the purely mathematical level, the issue is the following: Suppose one has a Toeplitz determinant $D_n(\varphi)$ with a symbol that depends on some external parameter, say $t$, $\varphi\!\left(e^{i\theta}\right) = \varphi_t \!\left(e^{i\theta}\right)$. For $t>0$, $\varphi_t$ is regular, but at $t=0$, $\varphi_t$ has a (Fisher-Hartwig) singularity, and hence, as we know, the nature of the asymptotics of $D_n(\varphi_t)$ as $n\to\infty$ is different for $t>0$ and $t=0$. This raises the general question: How does one describe the transition from the one kind of asymptotics to the other as $n\to\infty$ and $t\downarrow 0$?

In [@WMTB], Wu, McCoy, Tracy, and Barouch discovered a remarkable scaling relation for the Ising model. They defined the scaling functions $$\label{eq70}
G_\pm (r) = \lim_{\ell, m\to \infty, t\to\mp 0} \; \left|1 - e^{-2t}\right|^{-\frac{1}{4}} \left\langle \sigma_{1,1}\,
\sigma_{1+\ell, 1+m}\right\rangle, \quad t = \log \left(\sinh\frac{2J_1}{k_BT}\sinh\frac{2J_2}{k_BT}\right)$$ in the double-scaling limit $$\label{eq71}





\left(\frac{\sinh \left(2 \,J_1/k_B\,T_c\right)\ell^2 + \sinh \left(2\,J_2/k_B\,T_c\right)m^2} 
{\sinh\left(2 \,J_1/k_B\,T_c\right)+\sinh\left(2 \,J_2/k_B\,T_c\right)}
\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} |t|
\equiv r, \text{ fixed }.$$

Here $\pm$ refer to $T> T_c$ and $T< T_c$ respectively. They then showed that $G_\pm (r)$ could be expressed in terms of a solution of the Painlevé III equation $$\label{eq72} 
\frac{d^2\eta}{d\theta^2} = \frac{1}{\eta} \left(\frac{d\eta}{d\theta}\right)^2 - \frac{1}{\theta} \frac{d\eta}{d\theta}
+ \eta^3-\eta^{-1}$$ as $$\label{eq73}
G_\pm (r) = \frac{1 \mp \eta\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}{2\eta \left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{1/2}}
\exp \left[ \frac{1}{4} \int^\infty_{r/2} \theta\frac{\left(1-\eta^2\right)^2 -\left(\eta'\right)^2}{\eta^2}\;d\theta
\right]$$ with the boundary condition $\eta(\theta) \sim 1-\frac{2}{\pi}\;K_0 (2\theta)$ as $\theta\to\infty$, where $K_0$ denotes the modified Bessel function.

The above calculation was the first derivation of an explicit scaling law for a two-spin correlation function for any model in statistical physics. As noted before, the scaling functions $G_\pm (r)$ are believed to be universal for a wide class of two-dimensional models with short range interactions.

A crucial issue for [@WMTB] scaling theory was to show that formula ([\[eq70\]](#eq70){reference-type="ref" reference="eq70"}) matches the critical $1/4$ behavior ([\[eq60\]](#eq60){reference-type="ref" reference="eq60"}) as $r \to 0$. In order to do this, the authors needed to know the $\infty \leftrightarrow 0$ *connection formulae* for the Painlevé functions $\eta(\theta)$. In [@WMTB] they extracted this information from the unpublished thesis of Myers [@My] (see below). In a later paper in 1977, McCoy, Tracy, and Wu [@MTW] derived $\infty \leftrightarrow 0$ connection formulae for a two-parameter class of bounded solutions $\eta(\theta;\nu,\lambda)$ of the general Painlevé III equation $$\frac{d^2\eta}{d\theta^2} = \frac{1}{\eta} \left(\frac{d\eta}{d\theta}\right)^2 - \frac{1}{\theta} \frac{d\eta}{d\theta}
+ \frac{2\nu}{\theta}\left(\eta^2-1\right)+\eta^3-\eta^{-1}.$$ We now describe in more detail what was done in [@MTW], restricting our discussion to the case $\nu=0$ which is relevant to [@WMTB].

Consider the one parameter family $\eta(\theta)=\eta(\theta;0,\lambda)$ of the solutions of equation ([\[eq72\]](#eq72){reference-type="ref" reference="eq72"}) defined by the asymptotic condition $$\label{mccoy1}
\eta(\theta) \sim  1 - 2\lambda K_0(2\theta), \quad \theta \to \infty.$$ In [@MTW], it was shown that, for $0\le\lambda<1/\pi$, as $r \to 0$, $$\label{mccoy2}
\eta(r/2) = Br^{\sigma}\left(1 - \frac{1}{16}B^{-2}(1-\sigma)^{-2}r^{2-2\sigma} + O(r^2)\right)$$ where the constants $B$ and $\sigma$ are functions of $\lambda$ given by the explicit formulae: $$\label{mccoy3}
\sigma=\sigma(\lambda) = \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\pi\lambda), \quad 
B = B(\sigma) = 2^{-3\sigma}\frac{\Gamma((1-\sigma)/2)}{\Gamma((1+\sigma)/2)}.$$ In [@My], Myers derived the asymptotics for $\eta(r/2;0,1/\pi)$ as $r\downarrow 0$, $$\label{mccoy4}
\eta\left(\frac{r}{2};0,\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\sim -\frac{r}{2}\left(\log\frac{r}{8}+\gamma_E\right)$$ where $\gamma_E$ is Euler's constant. In [@MTW] the authors recovered [\[mccoy4\]](#mccoy4){reference-type="eqref" reference="mccoy4"} by analyzing formally the limit $\lambda\to 1/\pi$ in [\[mccoy2\]](#mccoy2){reference-type="eqref" reference="mccoy2"}. Proofs of [\[mccoy4\]](#mccoy4){reference-type="eqref" reference="mccoy4"} were given later in [@Wid8] and, using the Riemann-Hilbert method, in [@Niles]. In turn, the asymptotic formula ([\[mccoy4\]](#mccoy4){reference-type="ref" reference="mccoy4"}) yields the estimate: $$\label{mccoy5}
G_{\pm} \sim\mbox{const}\,r^{-1/4}\left[1 \pm \frac{r}{2}\left(\ln\frac{r}{8} + \gamma_E\right)
\right].$$ In order to see that this estimate matches equation ([\[eq60\]](#eq60){reference-type="ref" reference="eq60"}), it is enough to notice that ([\[eq70\]](#eq70){reference-type="ref" reference="eq70"}) and ([\[eq71\]](#eq71){reference-type="ref" reference="eq71"}) imply that $$\label{mccoy6}
\left\langle\sigma_{1,1}\,\sigma_{1+n,1+n}\right\rangle \sim 2^{1/4}G_{\pm}(r)|t|^{1/4} = 
2^{1/4}G_{\pm}(r)r^{1/4}n^{-1/4}.$$

The main term in equation ([\[eq60\]](#eq60){reference-type="ref" reference="eq60"}), up to the multiplicative constant, follows now directly from ([\[mccoy5\]](#mccoy5){reference-type="ref" reference="mccoy5"}). The evaluation of the constant in ([\[mccoy5\]](#mccoy5){reference-type="ref" reference="mccoy5"}) that matches the transcendental constant $A$ in ([\[eq60\]](#eq60){reference-type="ref" reference="eq60"}) was done by Tracy [@T] in 1991.

The Painlevé functions, PI, ..., PVI, (see [@In]) were discovered and analyzed intensively at the beginning of the 20$^{th}$ century until the First World War, and then fell into a period of latency. They reappeared in theoretical physics quite unexpectedly in 1965 in the work of Myers [@My] mentioned above, who showed that the scattering of electromagnetic radiation from a strip in the plane could be expressed in terms of a solution of the Painlevé III equation. The paper [@WMTB] was a seminal event in mathematical physics, followed soon after by the landmark paper of Jimbo et al [@JMMS] on the impenetrable Bose gas, and the Painlevé equations have emerged over the years as the core of modern special function theory, with applications across the board in mathematics, physics and engineering (see, e.g., [@FIKN]). In PDE's, in particular, the Painlevé equations (specifically, Painlevé II) appeared for the first time in the analysis of Ablowitz and Segur [@AS] of self-similar solutions of the Korteweg-de Vries equation. This pioneering development opened the door to understanding the crucial role that the Painlevé equations play in the theory of integrable dynamical systems (see [@AC], [@FIKN]). The work [@MTW] demonstrated for the first time the possibility to derive explicit connection formulae for families of solutions to a Painlevé equation, a property which had been previously known only for the linear ODEs of hypergeometric type. Similar connection formulae were obtained for the second Painlevé equation in [@AS] (only formulae for the amplitude) and in [@SA] (complete formulae for the amplitude and the phase). The papers [@MTW], [@AS], and [@SA] generated a surge of activity in the global asymptotic analysis of the Painlevé equations (see again [@FIKN] for more history and the state of the art in the area). We view these developments as one more example of the way in which questions arising in the analysis of the Ising model gave rise to developments in Toeplitz theory, and in this case, through Toeplitz theory, to the global theory of the Painlevé equations.

An alternative to the representation ([\[eq73\]](#eq73){reference-type="ref" reference="eq73"}) of $G_{\pm}(r)$ was obtained in 1980 by Jimbo and Miwa [@JM]. Their formula for $G_-(r)$ reads: $$\label{p5G}
G_-(r) = \exp\left[-\int_{2r}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma(x)}{x}dx\right],$$ where $\sigma(x)$ is the unique solution of the Painlevé V equation $$\label{p5}
\left(x\frac{d^2\sigma}{dx^2}\right)^2 = \left(\sigma
-x\frac{d\sigma}{dx} +2\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx}\right)^2 
\right)^2 
-4\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx}\right)^2\left(\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx}\right)^2 -\frac{1}{4}\right)$$ satisfying the boundary conditions $$\label{westintro0}
\sigma(x)=\begin{cases}
-1/4+O(x\log x),& x\to
0,\cr 
\frac{1}{2\pi}x^{-1}e^{-x}
 \left(1 +
O\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\right),& x\to +\infty.
\end{cases}$$ The equivalence of ([\[p5G\]](#p5G){reference-type="ref" reference="p5G"}) to the original PIII-formula ([\[eq73\]](#eq73){reference-type="ref" reference="eq73"}) was established in [@MP].

Recently in [@CIK] the following generalization of the Jimbo-Miwa result was obtained. The authors in [@CIK] considered the double-scaling limit for the Toeplitz determinant $D_n(t)$ with the symbol: $$\label{ciksymb}
\varphi(z) = (z-e^{t})^{\alpha + \beta} (z-e^{-t})^{\alpha - \beta}z^{-\alpha +\beta}e^{-i\pi(\alpha +\beta)}e^{V(z)},$$ where $t\geq 0$, $\alpha \pm \beta \neq -1, -2, ....$, $\Re\alpha > -1/2$, and $V(z)$ is analytic in an annulus containing the unit circle. The powers in ([\[ciksymb\]](#ciksymb){reference-type="ref" reference="ciksymb"}) are defined by requiring the arguments to lie between zero and $2\pi$. The symbol $\varphi(z)$ is analytic in ${\mathbb C}\setminus \left([0, e^{-t}]\cup[e^{t}, +\infty)\right)$. It is regular for $t>0$ and has a Fisher-Hartwig singularity for $t=0$ at $z_0=1$, $\varphi(z) = |z-1|^{2\alpha}z^{\beta}e^{-i\pi \beta}e^{V(z)}$. The main result in [@CIK] is the following: There exists a finite (perhaps empty) set $\Delta\subset (0,\infty)$ and a (small) number $t_0>0$, such that for $n\to\infty$ and for all $0< t < t_0$, $\log D_n(t)$ has an expansion of the form (see [@CIK] Theorem 1.4) $$\begin{gathered}
\label{expansionDn}
\log D_n(t)=n V_0+(\alpha+\beta)nt+
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\left[V_k-(\alpha+\beta)\frac{e^{-tk}}{k}\right]
\left[V_{-k}-(\alpha-\beta)\frac{e^{-tk}}{k}\right]\\
+\int_0^{2nt}\frac{\sigma(x)-\alpha^2+\beta^2}{x}dx+(\alpha^2-\beta^2)\log
2nt
+\log\frac{G(1+\alpha+\beta)G(1+\alpha-\beta)}{G(1+2\alpha)}+o(1),
\end{gathered}$$ with the error term being uniform for $0\leq t < t_0$ provided $\text{dist}\left(2nt, \Delta\right) \ge \delta>0$ for some $\delta>0$, and the path of integration in [\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="eqref" reference="expansionDn"} does not intersect with $\Delta$. (In fact, the asymptotics [\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="eqref" reference="expansionDn"} hold uniformly in a sector of the complex plane $-\pi/2+\epsilon<\arg x<\pi/2-\epsilon$, $0<\epsilon<\pi/2$, away from a finite number of points. These points, which include the points of $\Delta$, are possible poles of the function $\sigma(x)$. A choice of the integration contour corresponds to a branch of the logarithm.)

The function $\sigma(x)$ in ([\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="ref" reference="expansionDn"}) is the unique solution of the Painlevé V equation $$\begin{gathered}
\label{p5ab}
\left(x\frac{d^2\sigma}{dx^2}\right)^2 = \left(\sigma
-x\frac{d\sigma}{dx} +2\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx}\right)^2 + 2\alpha\frac{d\sigma}{dx}
\right)^2 \\
-4\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx}\right)^2\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx} +\alpha + \beta\right)\left(\frac{d\sigma}{dx} +\alpha - \beta\right)
\end{gathered}$$ which is real analytic in $(0, +\infty)$ and satisfies the boundary conditions $$\label{westintro1}
\sigma(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha^2-\beta^2+
\frac{\alpha^2-\beta^2}{2\alpha}\{x-x^{1+2\alpha}C(\alpha,
\beta)\}(1+O(x)),& x\to 0,\quad 2\alpha\notin {\mathbb Z}\cr
\alpha^2-\beta^2+O(x)+O(x^{1+2\alpha})+O(x^{1+2\alpha}\log x),& x\to
0,\quad 2\alpha\in {\mathbb Z}\cr x^{-1+2\alpha}e^{-x}
\frac{-1}{\Gamma(\alpha-\beta)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)} \left(1 +
O\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\right),& x\to +\infty,
\end{cases}$$ with $$\label{Cab}
C(\alpha,\beta)=
\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha+\beta)\Gamma(1+\alpha-\beta)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha+\beta)\Gamma(1-\alpha-\beta)}
\frac{\Gamma(1-2\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+2\alpha)^2}\frac{1}{1+2\alpha}.$$ For fixed $t >0$, equation ([\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="ref" reference="expansionDn"}) yields the standard Szegő large $n$ asymptotics for the regular symbol ([\[ciksymb\]](#ciksymb){reference-type="ref" reference="ciksymb"}). It is interesting to note that this implies the following integral identity for the Painlevé function $\sigma(x)$ (cf. (1.31) in [@CIK]): for any $x_0>0$, $$\begin{gathered}
\label{identitycik}
\int_0^{x_0}\frac{\sigma(x)-\alpha^2+\beta^2}{x}dx 
+\int_{x_0}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma(x)}{x}dx +(\alpha^2 - \beta^2)\log x_0\\
= -\log\frac{G(1+\alpha+\beta)G(1+\alpha-\beta)}{G(1+2\alpha)}.
\end{gathered}$$ In the case $t=0$, the equation ([\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="ref" reference="expansionDn"}) transforms to a single-point Fisher-Hartwig formulae: To see this, one utilizes the identity $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-2kt}/k = -\ln\left(1 - e^{-2t}\right)$.

In the special case $\alpha =0$, $\beta = -\frac{1}{2}$, $V(z) \equiv 0$, and with the identification, $e^{-t}=k_{\text{ons}}$, the symbol ([\[ciksymb\]](#ciksymb){reference-type="ref" reference="ciksymb"}) becomes $e^{-t/2}\varphi_\text{diag}(z)$. Therefore, in this case, $D_n(t)= e^{-nt/2}\left\langle \sigma_{1,1}\, \sigma_{1+n, 1+n}\right\rangle$ with $t\downarrow 0$ corresponding to $T\uparrow
T_c$. Set $2nt \equiv 2r$. Then formula ([\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="ref" reference="expansionDn"}), together with the identity ([\[identitycik\]](#identitycik){reference-type="ref" reference="identitycik"}) and the definition ([\[eq70\]](#eq70){reference-type="ref" reference="eq70"}) of the scaling functions $G_{\pm}(r)$, yields the Jimbo-Miwa relation ([\[p5G\]](#p5G){reference-type="ref" reference="p5G"}). With the equivalence of ([\[p5G\]](#p5G){reference-type="ref" reference="p5G"}) and [\[eq73\]](#eq73){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq73"}, this means that the *uniform* asymptotics ([\[expansionDn\]](#expansionDn){reference-type="ref" reference="expansionDn"}) include the double-scaling limit in [@WMTB] as a special case corresponding to $2nt \equiv 2r$ fixed (away from $\Delta$), $n\to\infty$, and $\alpha =0$, $\beta = -\frac{1}{2}$, $V(z) \equiv 0$.

::: remark
[]{#remarkJM label="remarkJM"} In [@JM], the authors used a remarkable relation (which they themselves discovered) between the diagonal Ising correlations and isomonodromy deformations of a certain $2\times2$ Fuchsian system. This discovery laid the foundation for the Riemann-Hilbert method in the theory of correlation functions, random matrices, and Toeplitz and Hankel determinants (see [@DIZ] for more on the history of these developments). Using this link to Fuchsian systems, it was shown in [@JM] that the diagonal correlation function $\left\langle \sigma_{1,1}\, \sigma_{1+n, 1+n}\right\rangle$, as a function of the variable $$s = e^{-2t} \equiv \left(\sinh\frac{2J_1}{k_BT}\sinh\frac{2J_2}{k_BT}\right)^{-2}$$ is expressed in terms of a solution to the Painlevé VI equation. The derivation of ([\[p5G\]](#p5G){reference-type="ref" reference="p5G"}) in [@JM] is done by formally performing a scaling transformation taking the inverse monodromy problem associated with this Painlevé VI to the inverse monodromy (Riemann-Hilbert) problem associated with the fifth Painlevé function $\sigma(x)$. In the rigorous setting of [@CIK], the latter problem appears as a parametrix during the implementation of the nonlinear steepest-descent method.
:::

::: remark
[]{#remarkJM3 label="remarkJM3"} The Painlevé VI description of the diagonal correlations in [@JM] was the first example of the appearance of the Painlevé functions in the theory of Toeplitz determinants before the large $n$ limit is taken. One realizes now that nonlinear differential equations of Painlevé type are always present in situations where the symbol $\varphi(z)$ satisfies the condition: $$\label{quasiclassymb}
\frac{d\log\varphi(z)}{dz} = \mbox{rational function}.$$ This fact is quite easy to see within the Riemann-Hilbert formalism (see, e.g., discussion in [@ITW]). These "finite $n$" Painlevé representations are not universal: the type of the nonlinear ODE depends strongly on the structure of the rational right hand side of ([\[quasiclassymb\]](#quasiclassymb){reference-type="ref" reference="quasiclassymb"}), i.e. on the symbol.
:::

Double-scaling problems for Toeplitz determinants occur in many different areas. For example, in combinatorics, let $\pi = \left(\pi(1), \pi(2), \dots, \pi(N)\right)$ be a permutation of the numbers $1, 2, \dots, N$. If $1\le i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k \le N$ and $\pi(i_1) < \pi(i_2) < \dots < \pi(i_k)$, we say that $\pi(i_1), \dots, \pi(i_k)$ is an *increasing subsequence* in $\pi$ of length $k$. Let $\ell_N(\pi)$ denote the maximum length of all increasing subsequences in $\pi$. With uniform distribution on the permutations, one is interested (see [@BDJ]) in the distribution function $$p_N(n)= \text{Prob } \left\{ \ell_N(\pi) \le n\right\} = \frac{\#\{ \pi:\ell_N(\pi)\le n\}}{N!}$$ as $N, \, n\to\infty$. It turns out [@Ges] that the Poissonized version $\phi_n(\lambda)$ of the process $p_N(n)$ $$\label{eq129}
\phi_n(\lambda) = \sum^\infty_{N=0} e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^N}{N!} \; p_N(n), \qquad \lambda>0$$ can be expressed in terms of a Toeplitz determinant $$\label{eq130}
\phi_n(\lambda) = e^{-\lambda} \, D_n(\varphi_\lambda)$$ where $\phi_\lambda(\theta)=e^{2\sqrt{\lambda} \cos \theta}$. In order to recover $p_N(n)$ as $N,\, n\to\infty$ from [\[eq129\]](#eq129){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq129"} [\[eq130\]](#eq130){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq130"} one must analyze the double-scaling limit for the determinant as $\lambda$, $n\to\infty$. The main result in [@BDJ] is the following: $$\label{eq131}
\lim_{N\to\infty} \text{Prob } \left(\frac{\ell_N-2\sqrt{N}}{N^{1/6}} \le t\right) = F_2(t)$$ where $F_2(t)$ is the Tracy-Widom distribution [@TW] for the largest eigenvalue of a random matrix in the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble. The distribution $F_2(t)$ can be expressed in terms of Painlevé functions as follows: $$F_2(t) =e^{-\int^\infty_t (s-t)\, u^2(s)\,ds}$$ where $u(s)$ is the unique solution of $PII$, $$u'' (s) =2 u^3(s) +s u(s)$$ with $$u(s) \sim -\mathrm{Ai}\,(s)\quad \text{ as }\quad s\to +\infty.$$ Here $\mathrm{Ai}\,(s)$ is the standard Airy function. On the other hand, $F_2(t)$ can be expressed as a Fredholm determinant $$\label{TWairydet}
F_2(t)=\det(I-K^{(s)}_{\mathrm{Airy}})$$ where $K^{(s)}_{\mathrm{Airy}}$ is the trace-class operator with kernel $$K^{(s)}_{\mathrm{Airy}}(x,y)=\frac{\mathrm{Ai}\,(x){d\over dy}\mathrm{Ai}\,(y)-
\mathrm{Ai}\,(y){d\over dx}\mathrm{Ai}\,(x)}{x-y}$$ acting on $L^2(s,\infty)$. In random matrix theory, this determinant is the probability that the interval $(s,\infty)$ contains no eigenvalues in the edge scaling limit in the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble.

The result in [@BDJ] was the first of many results linking problems in pure and applied mathematics and in physics to the Tracy-Widom distribution $F_2(t)$ and its symplectic and orthogonal analogs (see, for example [@Dei2]).

As already noted on several occasions, in his work on impenetrable bosons [@Len1], Lenard was led to consider Toeplitz determinants with symbols of type $\left|e^{i\theta}- e^{i\theta_1}\right| \left|e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta_2}\right|$ (see [\[eq81\]](#eq81){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq81"} above) that vanished on $S^1$. In [@Len2] he then considered symbols with any finite number of such $\alpha$-type FH singularities. Such symbols vanish at only a finite number of points, and in [@Wid6] Widom began considering symbols which vanished on a full interval. Let $\varphi_\mu\!\left(e^{i\theta}\right)$ denote the characteristic function of the interval $\left(\mu, 2\pi-\mu\right)$, $0<\mu <\pi$. In a virtuoso calculation Widom showed that, for $\mu$ fixed, as $n\to\infty$ $$\label{eq132}
\log D_n(\varphi_\mu) = n^2 \log\cos \frac{\mu}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \log \left(n\sin \frac{\mu}{2}\right)
+ c_0 + o(1)$$ where $c_0 = \frac{1}{12} \log 2 + 3 \zeta'(-1)$ and $\zeta(z)$ is the Riemann zeta-function. A few years later in 1976, Dyson [@Dy4] returned to the problem (cf. discussion following [\[6.1\]](#6.1){reference-type="eqref" reference="6.1"} above) of computing the probability $P_s$ that there are no eigenvalues for a random matrix in the interval $(0,2s/\pi)$, in the bulk scaling limit for the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble. He showed that as $s\to\infty$, $\log P_s$ has a full asymptotic expansion $$\label{eq133}
\log P_s =  -\frac{s^2}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \log s + a_0 + \frac{a_1}{s} + \frac{a_2}{s^2}
+ \dots \ ,$$ Dyson identified all the constants $a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots ,$ and it turns out that $a_0$, which is of particular interest, is precisely the constant $c_0$ arising in Widom's expansion [\[eq132\]](#eq132){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq132"} above. Dyson arrived at the identification $a_0=c_0$ by noting first that the probability $P_s$ is given by (see, e.g., [@Meh], [@Dei3]) $$\label{12.1}
P_s=\det(I-K_s)$$ where $K_s$ is the trace-class operator with kernel $$\label{sinekernel}
K_s(x,y)=\frac{\sin(x-y)}{\pi(x-y)}$$ acting on $L^2(-s,s)$. A simple calculation shows that $$D_n(\varphi_\mu)=\det\left(\delta_{jk}-\frac{\sin\mu(j-k)}{\pi(j-k)}\right)_{0\le j,k\le n-1}$$ and so for fixed $s>0$, $$\label{eq134}
\lim_{n\to\infty} D_n \!\left(\varphi_{\frac{2s}{n}}\right) =P_s$$ and hence, if the error term $o(1)$ in [\[eq132\]](#eq132){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq132"} was uniform in the double scaling limit, $n\to\infty$, $\mu\to 0$ such that $\mu n=2s$, one could conclude from [\[eq132\]](#eq132){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq132"} [\[eq134\]](#eq134){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq134"} that $a_0$ is indeed equal to $c_0$. The uniformity of the error term $o(1)$, however, remained open. There are now three proofs that $a_0=c_0$. The first two were given simultaneously and independently by Ehrhardt and Krasovsky, and the third was given a little later in [@DIKZ]. The proofs by Krasovsky and by Deift, Its, Krasovsky, and Zhou proceed by following Dyson's observation [\[eq134\]](#eq134){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq134"} and verifying that the $o(1)$ error term is indeed uniform. We refer the reader to [@DIKZ] for some history of the problem $P_s$, $s\to\infty$, and, in particular, for the relevant references to the work of Krasovsky and Ehrhardt. A similar asymptotic problem arises when instead of the sine kernel determinant [\[12.1\]](#12.1){reference-type="eqref" reference="12.1"} one considers the general so-called confluent hypergeometric kernel determinant which depends on two extra parameters (and is related to the so-called Bessel kernel determinant and also to the determinant [\[12.1\]](#12.1){reference-type="eqref" reference="12.1"} for particular choices of the parameters); another related problem is the asymptotics for the Tracy-Widom distribution [\[TWairydet\]](#TWairydet){reference-type="eqref" reference="TWairydet"} as $s\to-\infty$. These problems can also be represented as double scaling problems for Toeplitz (and also Hankel) determinants, and such representations were crucial for finding complete solutions for the asymptotic problems at hand. We refer the reader to [@Kr] for a discussion of the results and for the references.

Dyson's identification of $a_1,a_2,\dots$ in [@Dy4] involves an ingenious application of inverse scattering theory for Schrödinger operators on the half-line $0\le x<\infty$. He begins by noting that $P_s=\det(I-K_s)$ can be factored $$\label{13.1}
\det(I-K_s)=D_+(s)D_-(s)$$ where $D_\pm(s)=\det(I-f_\pm)_{L^2(0,s)}$ are Toeplitz+Hankel type determinants on $L^2(0,s)$ with kernels $$\label{13.2}
f_\pm(x,y)={1\over\pi}\left[\frac{\sin(x-y)}{x-y}\pm \frac{\sin(x+y)}{x+y}\right].$$ He then considers the functions $$\label{13.3}
W_\pm(s)=-2{d^2\over ds^2}\log D_\pm(s)-1$$ as potentials for Schrödinger operators $H_\pm=-{d^2\over ds^2}+W_\pm(s)$ on $L^2(0,\infty)$ with appropriate Robin boundary conditions at $s=0$. Using the Gelfand-Levitan formalism (see, e.g., [@Fad]), Dyson is able to identify the spectral measures $\rho_\pm(\lambda)d\lambda$ for the operators $H_\pm$. He then obtains the scattering phase functions $e^{i\eta_\pm}$. In the Marchenko formalism (see, e.g., [@Fad]) one utilizes the functions $e^{i\eta_\pm}$ to express the potentials $W_\pm(s)$ in terms of Fredholm determinants of the form $$\label{14.1}
W_\pm(s)=-{1\over 4}\left(s\pm\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-2}\!\!-2{d^2\over ds^2}\log \Delta_\pm(s),\qquad s>\frac{1}{2}$$ where $\Delta_\pm(s)=\det(I-F_\pm)_{L^2(s,\infty)}$ and $F_\pm(x,y)$ are certain explicit kernels which decay as $s\to\infty$. Equating [\[13.3\]](#13.3){reference-type="eqref" reference="13.3"} and [\[14.1\]](#14.1){reference-type="eqref" reference="14.1"}, and integrating twice, one obtains the following identity for $s>\frac{1}{2}$: $$\label{14.2}
\log P_s=\log (I-K_s)=\log D_+D_-=-\frac{1}{2}s^2-{1\over 8}\log\left( s^2-{1\over 4}\right)+a_0+\log\left(\Delta_+(s)\Delta_-(s)\right)$$ (the terms linear in $s$ drop out). As $F_\pm(x,y)$ decay as $x,y\to\infty$, it is clear that [\[14.2\]](#14.2){reference-type="eqref" reference="14.2"} gives rise to a full expansion of the form [\[eq133\]](#eq133){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq133"}, where the coefficients can be computed in terms of the trace powers $\,{\mathrm{tr}}\,F_\pm^m$.

Dyson is able to compute $e^{i\eta_\pm}$, and hence $F_\pm$, explicitly, because of the following fundamental fact from inverse scattering theory. The spectral weights have the form $\rho_\pm(\lambda)=\sqrt{\lambda}/(\pi |\varphi_\pm(\sqrt{\lambda})|^2)$, where $\varphi_\pm(k)$, $k=\sqrt{\lambda}$, are certain functions constructed from the Jost solutions $J_\pm(s,k)$ for $H_\pm$, $H_\pm J_\pm(s,k)=k^2J_\pm(s,k)$, $J_\pm(s,k)\sim e^{iks}$ as $s\to\infty$. The functions $\varphi_\pm(k)$ are analytic in $\{\Im k>0\}$ with prescribed asymptotics as $k\to\infty$, and in the cases at hand, have no zeros in $\{\Im k>0\}$. Hence, by standard arguments, $\varphi_\pm(k)$ are determined by their absolute value $|\varphi(k)|$ for $\Im k=0$. Thus $\rho_\pm(\lambda)$ determine $\varphi_\pm(k)$. However, $e^{i\eta_\pm}$ can be expressed in terms of $\varphi_\pm$ as $e^{i\eta_\pm(k)}=i\overline{\varphi_\pm(k)}/|\varphi_\pm(k)|$, and hence $\rho_\pm(\lambda)$ determine the scattering phase functions, and hence, eventually, $F_\pm$.

These considerations bring to mind the Borodin-Okounkov-Geronimo-Case formula [\[eq38\]](#eq38){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq38"} in which the Szegő function ${\cal D}(z) = \exp \left(\frac{1}{4\pi} \int^\pi_{-\pi}  \frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z}
\;\log \varphi\!\left(e^{i\theta}\right)d\theta\right)$, which is analytic in $|z|<1$, plays the role of the functions $\varphi_\pm(k)$. Indeed, $|{\cal D}(e^{i\theta})|^2=\varphi(e^{i\theta})$, which is the spectral measure for the associated Cantero-Moral-Velasquez (CMV) unitary operator acting on $\ell_+^2$ (see [@Sim2]). Thus we may think of the Toeplitz determinant $D_n(\varphi)$ as an expression in the "Gelfand-Levitan formalism". On the other hand, we may think of the Fredholm determinant on the RHS of [\[eq38\]](#eq38){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq38"} as an expression in the "Marchenko formalism". Indeed, from [\[eq39\]](#eq39){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq39"}, $b=\overline{\cal D}^2/|{\cal D}|^2$, which we may view as a scattering phase function $b=e^{2i\eta}$ for the CMV operator, and $c$ is just $\overline{b}=e^{-2i\eta}$. These considerations are very suggestive and invite further investigation. One notes that the original proof of [\[eq38\]](#eq38){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq38"} by Geronimo and Case in [@GerCase] was developed in the context of scattering theory.

Another example of a double-scaling limit is the following. Recall that the density matrix for the impenetrable bosons is given by $\rho_{N,L}(\xi)=\frac{1}{L}R_N(2\pi\xi/L)$, where $R_N$ is the Toeplitz determinant [\[34p3\]](#34p3){reference-type="eqref" reference="34p3"} with symbol [\[Lensym\]](#Lensym){reference-type="eqref" reference="Lensym"}. For definiteness, fix the length scale so that $L=N$. As we discussed in Remark [\[Lenard\]](#Lenard){reference-type="ref" reference="Lenard"} in Section [\[sectionFH\]](#sectionFH){reference-type="ref" reference="sectionFH"}, of particular interest is the double-scaling limit $N\to\infty$, $\xi/ N\to 0$. In [@Len1] Lenard considered this limit $\rho(\xi)=\lim_{N\to\infty}{1\over N}R_N(2\pi\xi/N)$ with $\xi$ fixed. He obtained an expression for $\rho(\xi)$ in terms of a Fredholm determinant with explicit kernel related to the sine kernel [\[sinekernel\]](#sinekernel){reference-type="eqref" reference="sinekernel"}. He also obtained another representation for $\rho(\xi)$ in terms of a Fredholm minor for the kernel $2\frac{\sin\pi |\xi|(x-y)}{\pi(x-y)}$.

In order to complete his analysis of the limiting momentum distribution in the gas, Lenard needed to know the large $\xi$ behavior of $\rho(\xi)$, but he did not derive this behavior in [@Len1]. The derivation of this limiting behavior was carried out by Vaidya and Tracy [@VaiTr] who found that $$\label{22.1}
\rho(\xi)=\frac{\rho_\infty}{(\pi\xi)^{1/2}}
\left(1+{1\over 8(\pi\xi)^2}\left(\cos2\pi\xi -{1\over 4}\right)+\cdots\right),\qquad \rho_\infty=\pi e^{1/2} 2^{-1/3}A^{-6},$$ as $\xi\to\infty$, where $A$ is again Glaisher's constant [\[Glai\]](#Glai){reference-type="eqref" reference="Glai"}. Note that if one evaluates $\rho_{N,N}(\xi)=\frac{1}{N}R_N(2\pi\xi/N)$, $N, \xi=\frac{tN}{2\pi}\to\infty$ using [\[eq84\]](#eq84){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq84"} [\[eq85\]](#eq85){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq85"}, and assumes that the formulae remain valid as $t=2\pi\xi/N\to 0$, then one obtains the leading term in [\[22.1\]](#22.1){reference-type="eqref" reference="22.1"}.

The double-scaling limit $N\to\infty$, $\xi/ N\to 0$ corresponds to the Toeplitz determinant with two FH singularities merging (at $z=1$) with $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=\frac{1}{2}$, $\beta_1=\beta_2=0$. A general analysis of the double-scaling asymptotics of a Toeplitz determinant with two merging FH singularities (in particular, of Lenard's symbol [\[34p3\]](#34p3){reference-type="eqref" reference="34p3"}) is given in [@CK].

Dyson also considered [@Dy5] the following scaling problem associated with Toeplitz determinants. Let $$\Delta(z,t)=\det(I-z\widehat K_t)$$ where $0<z<1$ and $\widehat K_t$ acts on $L^2(-t,t)$ with kernel $\widehat K_t(x,y)=\frac{\sin\pi(x-y)}{\pi(x-y)}$. With the extra $\pi$ in the sine function (cf. $K_s$ in [\[sinekernel\]](#sinekernel){reference-type="eqref" reference="sinekernel"}), $\Delta(1,t)$ is now the probability that there are no eigenvalues in the interval $(0,2t)$. Of interest here is the double scaling limit for $\Delta(z,t)$ as $t\to\infty$ and $z\uparrow 1$. Dyson analyses the problem by interpreting $\Delta(z,t)$ in terms of a Coulomb gas at inverse temperature $\beta=2$. In this interpretation, $z$ corresponds to an external potential $v$ via the formula $1-z=e^{-\beta v}$. Then $$\Delta(z,t)=\frac{Z_2(v,2t)}{Z_2(0,2t)}$$ where $$\label{23.2}
Z_\beta=\sum e^{-\beta (W+vn)}$$ is the partition function of the gas with external potential $v$ applied to charges in a fixed interval of length $2t$. The sum in [\[23.2\]](#23.2){reference-type="eqref" reference="23.2"} is to be interpreted as an infinite-dimensional integral over all configurations of the gas, $W$ represents the sum of all Coulomb interactions $W_{jk}=-\log|x_j-x_k|$, and for any configuration, $n$ is the number of charges in a fixed interval of length $2t$. Dyson does not define the sum rigorously, but he uses it as a heuristic guide in his calculations. The main result in the paper is the calculation of oscillatory factors in $\Delta(z,t)$ as $t\to\infty$: These factors turn out to be so-called "genuinely non-linear oscillations" and are described by Jacobi elliptic functions. For $0<z<1$, $0<\lambda<1$, the symbol $$f_{z,\lambda}(e^{i\theta})=\chi_{[\pi\lambda,\pi(2-\lambda)]}+(1-z)\chi_{[-\pi\lambda,\pi\lambda]}$$ gives rise to the Toeplitz determinant $$D_n(f_{z,\lambda})=\det\left(\delta_{jk}-z\frac{\sin\lambda\pi(j-k)}{\pi(j-k)}\right)_{0\le j,k\le n-1}$$ and so for fixed $z$, $t$ $$\lim_{n\to\infty}D_n\left(f_{z,{2t\over n}}\right)=\Delta(z,t).$$ Thus Dyson's double scaling limit $z\uparrow 1$, $t\to\infty$ can be viewed as a triple scaling limit for the Toeplitz determinant $D_n(f_{z,2t/n})$ with $z\uparrow 1$, $t\to\infty$, and $n\to\infty$. Note finally that $f_{z,\lambda}$ is precisely the kind of symbol that arises in the Toeplitz eigenvalue problem [\[eq120\]](#eq120){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq120"}.

Lenard ends his 1972 paper [@Len2] with a hope and a prophecy: "It is the author's hope that a rigorous analysis will someday carry the results to the point where the true role of the zeros of the generating function will be understood. When that day comes a capstone will have been put on a beautiful edifice to whose construction many contributed and whose foundations lie in the studies of Gabor Szegő half a century ago".

Almost 100 years have passed since Szegő published his first paper on the asymptotics of Toeplitz determinants. We certainly know more now than in 1972, but many new problems continue to arise, and it is still too early, alas, to set the capstone.
