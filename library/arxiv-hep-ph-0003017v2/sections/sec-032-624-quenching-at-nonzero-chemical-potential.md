[]{#failure label="failure"}

The failure of the quenched approximation at nonzero chemical potential was first understood analytically in terms of chRMT by Stephanov [@Misha]. He showed that the quenched limit is the limit $N_f \to 0$ of a partition function with the absolute value of the fermion determinant, $$|\det({\cal D}(\mu) +m)|^{N_f}\:,
  \label{absdet}$$ rather than $$^{N_f}\:.$$ The absolute value of the fermion determinant can be written as $$\det({\cal D}+m) \det({\cal D}^\dagger+m^*)= 
  \det \left(\matrix{iW+\mu & m \cr m & \!\!\!iW^\dagger+\mu}\right)
  \det\left(\matrix{iW^\dagger-\mu& m^*\cr m^*&\!\!\!iW-\mu}\right)\:.
  \label{dets}$$ Writing the fermion determinant as a Grassmann integral, we observe that the quenched partition function can be interpreted as a partition function of quarks and conjugate antiquarks. Therefore, in addition to the usual Goldstone modes, we have Goldstone modes consisting of a quark and a conjugate antiquark [@Goksch; @Misha]. Such modes, with the same mass as the usual Goldstone bosons, have a nonzero baryon number. The critical chemical potential given by the mass of the lightest particle with nonzero baryon number is thus $m_\pi/2$. The failure of the quenched approximation thus has an important benefit: It allows us to write down the exact low-energy effective partition function, which is discussed in the next section. The product of the two determinants in Eq. ([\[dets\]](#dets){reference-type="ref" reference="dets"}) can be written as the determinant of a single Hermitian matrix. This procedure is known as Hermitization [@Girko; @Fein; @Efetov].
