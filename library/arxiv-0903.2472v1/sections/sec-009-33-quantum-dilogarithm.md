Since quantum dilogarithms play a key role here, we take a little time to discuss some of their most important properties.

Somewhat confusingly, there are at least three distinct---though related---functions which have occurred in the literature under the name "quantum dilogarithm":

$i)$ the function $\text{Li}_2(x;q)$ defined for $x,\,q\in{\mathbb{C}}$ with $|x|,\,|q|<1$ by $$\label{Li2} \text{Li}_2(x;q)\;=\;\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n\,(1-q^n)}\,,$$ whose relation to the classical dilogarithm function $\text{Li}_2(x)=\sum\limits_{n=1}^\infty\dfrac{x^n}{n^2}$ is that $$\label{asymp1} \text{Li}_2\bigl(x;\,e^{2\hbar}\bigr)\;\sim\;-\frac1{2\hbar}\,\text{Li}_2(x)\qquad\text{as $\hbar\to0\,$;}$$

$ii)$ the function $(x;q)_\infty$ defined for $|q|<1$ and all $x\in\mathbb C$ by $$\label{qPoch} (x;q)_\infty\;=\;\prod_{r=0}^\infty\bigl(1-q^rx)\,,$$ which is related to $\text{Li}_2(x;q)$ for $|x|<1$ by $$\label{expLi2}(x;q)_\infty\;=\;\exp\bigl(-\text{Li}_2(x;q)\bigr)\,;$$ and finally

$iii)$ the function $\Phi(z;\tau)$ defined for $\mbox{Re }(\tau)>0$ and $2|\mbox{Re }(z)|<1+\mbox{Re }(\tau)$ by $$\label{qdl_integral} \Phi(z;\tau)\;=\;\exp\biggl(\frac14\int_{\mathbb R^{(+)}}
   \frac{e^{2xz}}{\sinh x\,\sinh\tau x}\,\frac{dx}x\biggr)$$ (here $\mathbb R^{(+)}$ denotes a path from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ along the real line but deformed to pass over the singularity at zero), which is related to $(x;q)_\infty$ by $$\label{qdl_quot} \Phi(z;\tau)\;=\;\begin{cases}
  \phantom x\dfrac{\big(-\bold e(z+\tau/2);\,\bold e(\tau)\big)_\infty}{\big(-\bold e((z-1/2)/\tau);\,\bold e(-1/\tau)\big)_\infty\vphantom{\int_{R_R}}}
  &\text{if $\mbox{Im }(\tau)>0$,} \\ \phantom{xx}
  \dfrac{\vphantom{\int^{\mathbb R^{(+)}}}\big(-\bold e((z+1/2)/\tau);\,\bold e(1/\tau)\big)_\infty}{\big(-\bold e(z-\tau/2);\,\bold e(-\tau)\big)_\infty}
  & \text{if $\mbox{Im }(\tau)<0$.}    \end{cases}$$ (Here and in future we use the abbreviation $\bold e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}$.)

It is the third of these functions, in the normalization $$\Phi_\hbar(z)\;=\;\Phi\bigl(\frac z{2\pi i};\,\frac{\hbar}{i\pi}\bigr)\,, \label{qdl-hbar}$$ which occurs in our "state integral" and which we will take as our basic "quantum dilogarithm," but all three functions play a role in the analysis, so we will describe the main properties of all three here. We give complete proofs, but only sketchily since none of this material is new. For further discussion and proofs, see, *e.g.*, [@faddeev-1994; @faddeev-1999; @faddeev-2001-219; @volkov-2003; @goncharov-2007], and [@zagDilog] (subsection II.1.D).

The asymptotic formula [\[asymp1\]](#asymp1){reference-type="eqref" reference="asymp1"} can be refined to the asymptotic expansion $$\label{asymp2} \text{Li}_2\bigl(x;e^{2\hbar}\bigr)\,\sim\,-\frac1{2\hbar}\,\text{Li}_2(x)-\frac12\log(1-x)
    -\frac x{1-x}\,\frac\hbar{6}+0\hbar^2+\frac{x+x^2}{(1-x)^3}\frac{\hbar^3}{90}+\cdots$$ as $\hbar\to0$ with $x$ fixed, in which the coefficient of $\hbar^{n-1}$ for $n\ge2$ is the product of $-2^{n-1}B_n/n!$ (here $B_n$ is the $n$th Bernoulli number) with the negative-index polylogarithm $\text{Li}_{2-n}(x)\in\mathbb Q\bigl[\frac1{1-x}\bigr]\,$. More generally, one has the asymptotic formula $$\label{asymp3} \text{Li}_2\bigl(xe^{2\lambda\hbar};\,e^{2\hbar}\bigr)\;\,\sim\;\,
  -\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^{n-1}B_n(\lambda)}{n!}\,\text{Li}_{2-n}(x)\,\hbar^{n-1}$$ as $\hbar\to0$ with $\lambda$ fixed, where $B_n(t)$ denotes the $n$th Bernoulli polynomial. [^1] Both formulas are easy consequences of the Euler-Maclaurin summation formula. By combining [\[qdl_quot\]](#qdl_quot){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_quot"}, [\[expLi2\]](#expLi2){reference-type="eqref" reference="expLi2"}, and [\[asymp3\]](#asymp3){reference-type="eqref" reference="asymp3"}, one also obtains an asymptotic expansion

$$\begin{aligned}
 \Phi_\hbar(z+2\lambda\hbar) &= \exp\left(\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{2^{n-1}B_{n}(1/2+\lambda)}{n!}\hbar^{n-1}
\text{Li}_{2-n}(-e^z)\right)\,. \label{qdl-hbarexpansion} 
\end{aligned}$$

(To derive this, note that in [\[qdl_quot\]](#qdl_quot){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_quot"} $(-\bold e(z\pm1/2)/\tau;\bold e(\pm1/\tau))_\infty \sim 1$ to all orders in $\hbar$ as $\hbar\rightarrow 0$.)

The function $(x;q)_\infty$ and its reciprocal have the Taylor expansions $$\label{Taylor} (x;q)_\infty\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{(q)_n}\,q^{\frac{n(n-1)}2}\,x^n\,,
  \qquad\frac1{(x;q)_\infty}\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac1{(q)_n}\,x^n$$ around $x=0$, where $$\label{qPochn} (q)_n\;=\;\frac{\bigl(q;q\bigr)_\infty}{\bigl(q^{n+1};q\bigr)_\infty}
  \;=\;(1-q)(1-q^2)\cdots(1-q^n)$$ is the $n$th $q$-Pochhammer symbol. These, as well as formula [\[expLi2\]](#expLi2){reference-type="eqref" reference="expLi2"}, can be proved easily from the recursion formula $(x;q)_\infty=(1-x)(qx;q)_\infty$, which together with the initial value $(0;q)_\infty=1$ determines the power series $(x;q)_\infty$ uniquely. (Of course, [\[expLi2\]](#expLi2){reference-type="eqref" reference="expLi2"} can also be proved directly by expanding each term in $\sum_r\log(1-q^rx)$ as a power series in $x$.) Another famous result, easily deduced from [\[Taylor\]](#Taylor){reference-type="eqref" reference="Taylor"} using the identity $\sum\limits_{m-n=k}\dfrac{q^{mn}}{(q)_m(q)_n}=\dfrac1{(q)_\infty}$ for all $k\in{\mathbb{Z}}$, is the Jacobi triple product formula $$\label{triple} (q;q)_\infty\,(x;q)_\infty\,(qx^{-1};q)_\infty
  \;=\;\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}(-1)^kq^{\frac{k(k-1)}2}\,x^k\,,$$ relating the function $(x;q)_\infty$ to the classical Jacobi theta function.

The function $\Phi(z;\tau)$ defined (initially for ${\rm Re}\,(\tau)>0$ and $|{\rm Re}\,(z)|<\frac12+\frac12{\rm Re}\,(\tau)$) by [\[qdl_integral\]](#qdl_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_integral"} has several functional equations. Denote by $I(z;\tau)$ the integral appearing in this formula. Choosing for $\mathbb R^{(+)}$ the path $(-\infty,-\varepsilon]\cup\,\varepsilon\exp([i\pi,0])\,\cup[\varepsilon,\infty)$ and letting $\varepsilon\to0$, we find $$\label{split} I(z;\tau)\;=\;\frac{2\pi i}\tau\,\biggl(\frac{1+\tau^2}{12}-z^2\biggr)
    \,+\,2\,\int_0^\infty\biggl(\frac{\sinh 2xz}{\sinh x\;\sinh\tau x}\;-\;\frac{2z}{\tau x}\biggr)\,\frac{dx}x\;.$$ Since the second term is an even function of $z$, this gives $$\label{parity} \Phi(z;\tau)\;\Phi(-z;\tau)\;=\;\bold e\bigl(\frac{\tau^2-12z^2+1}{24\tau}\bigr)\;.$$ From [\[split\]](#split){reference-type="eqref" reference="split"} we also get $$I(z+1/2;\tau)\,-\,I(z-1/2;\tau)\;=\;-\frac{4\pi iz}\tau\,+\,4\int_0^\infty
    \biggl(\frac{\cosh 2xz}{\sinh\tau x}\;-\;\frac1{\tau x}\biggr)\,\frac{dx}x\;.$$ The integral equals $-\log(2\cos(\pi z/\tau))$ (proof left as an exercise). Dividing by 4 and exponentiating we get the first of the two functional equations $$\label{lattice}\frac{\Phi(z-1/2;\tau)}{\Phi(z+1/2;\tau)}\;=\;1\,+\,\bold e\bigl(z/\tau\bigr)\,,
  \qquad\frac{\Phi(z-\tau/2;\tau)}{\Phi(z+\tau/2;\tau)}\;=\;1\,+\,\bold e\bigl(z\bigr)\,,$$ and the second can be proved in the same way or deduced from the first using the obvious symmetry property The complex $z$-plane, showing poles ($X$'s) and zeroes ($O$'s) of $\Phi_\hbar(z)$ at $\hbar=\frac{3}{4}e^{i\pi/3}$.\
[]{#fig:minefield label="fig:minefield"} $$\label{scaling} \Phi(z;\tau)\;=\;\Phi(z/\tau;1/\tau)\,,$$ of the function $\Phi$. (Replace $x$ by $x/\tau$ in [\[qdl_integral\]](#qdl_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_integral"}.)

The functional equations [\[lattice\]](#lattice){reference-type="eqref" reference="lattice"} show that $\Phi(z;\tau)$, which in its initial domain of definition clearly has no zeros or poles, extends (for fixed $\tau$ with $\mbox{Re }(\tau)>0)$ to a meromorphic function of $z$ with simple poles at $z\in\Xi(\tau)$ and simple zeros at $z\in-\Xi(\tau)$, where

$$\label{poles} \Xi(\tau)\;=\;\bigl({\mathbb{Z}}_{\ge0}+\tfrac12\bigr)\,\tau\;+\;\bigl({\mathbb{Z}}_{\ge0}+\tfrac12\bigr)\;\subset\;{\mathbb{C}}\,.$$

In terms of the normalization [\[qdl-hbar\]](#qdl-hbar){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl-hbar"}, this says that $\Phi_\hbar(z)$ has simple poles at $z\in\tilde{\Xi}(\hbar)$ and simple zeroes at $z\in-\tilde{\Xi}(\hbar)$, where

$$\label{polesh} \tilde{\Xi}(\hbar) = (2{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ge0}+1)i\pi+(2{\mathbb{Z}}_{\ge0}+1)\hbar\,.$$

This is illustrated in Figure [\[fig:minefield\]](#fig:minefield){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:minefield"}. Equation [\[qdl_quot\]](#qdl_quot){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_quot"} expressing $\Phi$ in terms of the function $(x;q)_\infty$ also follows, because the quotient of its left- and right-hand sides is a doubly periodic function of $z$ with no zeros or poles and hence constant, and the constant can only be $\pm1$ (and can then be checked to be $+1$ in several ways, e.g., by evaluating numerically at one point) because the right-hand side of [\[qdl_quot\]](#qdl_quot){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_quot"} satisfies the same functional equation [\[parity\]](#parity){reference-type="eqref" reference="parity"} as $\Phi(z;\tau)$ by virtue of the Jacobi triple product formula [\[triple\]](#triple){reference-type="eqref" reference="triple"} and the well-known modular transformation properties of the Jacobi theta function.

From [\[split\]](#split){reference-type="eqref" reference="split"} we also find the Taylor expansion of $I(z;\tau)$ at $z=0$, $$\label{TaylorI}I(z;\tau)\;=\; 4\,\sum_{k=0}^\infty C_k(\tau)\,z^k\,,$$ with coefficients $C_k(\tau)=\tau^{-k}C_k(1/\tau)$ given by $$\begin{aligned}
&&C_0(\tau)\,=\,\frac{\pi i}{24}\bigl(\tau+\tau^{-1}\bigr)\,, \qquad
       C_1(\tau)\,=\,\int_0^\infty\biggl(\frac1{\sinh x\;\sinh\tau x}\,-\,\frac1{\tau x^2}\biggr)\,dx\,, \\
  &&C_2(\tau)\,=\,-\frac{\pi i}{2\tau}\,,\qquad C_k(\tau)\;=\;0\quad\text{for $k\ge4$ even},\\
  &&C_k(\tau)\;=\;\frac{2^{k-1}}{k!}\,\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{k-1}\;dx}{\sinh x\;\sinh\tau x} \quad\text{for $k\ge3$ odd.} 
\end{aligned}$$ By expanding $1/\sinh(x)$ and $1/\sinh(\tau x)$ as power series in $e^{-x}$ and $e^{-\tau x}$ we can evaluate the last of these expressions to get $$C_k(\tau)\;=\;\frac{2^{k+1}}{k!}\sum_{m,n>0,\text{ odd}}\int_0^\infty e^{-mx-n\tau x}\,x^{k-1}\,dx
     \;=\;\frac2k\sum_{s\in\Xi(\tau)}s^{-k}\quad(\text{$k\ge3$ odd})$$ with $\Xi(\tau)$ as in [\[poles\]](#poles){reference-type="eqref" reference="poles"}. Dividing by 4 and exponentiating gives the Weierstrass product expansion $$\label{Weierstrass} \Phi(z;\tau)\;=\;\exp\biggl(\frac{\pi i}{24}\bigl(\tau+\tau^{-1}\bigr)\,+\,C_1(\tau)\,z
    \,-\,\frac{\pi iz^2}{2\tau}\biggr)\prod_{s\in\Xi(\tau)}\biggl(\frac{s+z}{s-z}\,e^{-2z/s}\biggr)$$ of $\Phi(z;\tau)$. From this expansion, one finds that $\Phi(z;\tau)$ extends meromorphically to ${\mathbb{C}}\times\bigl({\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus(-\infty,0]\bigr)$ with simple poles and simple zeros for $z\in\Xi(\tau)$ and $z\in-\Xi(\tau)$ and no other zeros or poles. (This analytic continuation can also be deduced by rotating the path of integration in [\[qdl_integral\]](#qdl_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_integral"}, e.g. by replacing $\int_{\mathbb R^{(+)}}$ by $\int_{\mathbb R^{(+)}/\sqrt\tau}$ for $z$ sufficiently small.)

The quantum dilogarithm is related via the Jacobi triple product formula to the Jacobi theta function, which is a Jacobi form, i.e., it has transformation properties not only with respect to the lattice translations $z\mapsto z+1$ and $z\mapsto z+\tau$ but also with respect to the modular transformations $\tau\mapsto\tau+1$ and $\tau\mapsto-1/\tau$. The function $\Phi(z;\tau)$ has the lattice transformation properties [\[lattice\]](#lattice){reference-type="eqref" reference="lattice"} and maps to its inverse under $(z,\tau)\mapsto(z/\tau,\,-1/\tau)$, but it does not transform in a simple way with respect to $\tau\mapsto\tau+1$. Nevertheless, it has an interesting modularity property of a different kind (cocycle property) which is worth mentioning here even though no use of it will be made in the remainder of this paper. Write [\[qdl_quot\]](#qdl_quot){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_quot"} as $$\label{StoPhi} \Phi(z;\tau)\;=\;\frac{S(z;\tau)}{S(z/\tau;-1/\tau)}\,,\qquad S(z;\tau)\;=\;\begin{cases}
    \prod_{\text{$n>0$ odd}}\bigl(1+q^{n/2}\bold e(z)\bigr)&\text{ if $\mbox{Im }(\tau)>0$}, \\
    \prod_{\text{$n<0$ odd}}\bigl(1+q^{n/2}\bold e(z)\bigr)^{-1}&\text{ if $\mbox{Im }(\tau)<0$}, \end{cases}$$ where $q=\bold e(\tau)$. The function $S(z;\tau)$ has the transformation properties $$S(z;\tau)=S(z+1;\tau)=(1+q^{1/2}\bold e(z))\,S(z+\tau;\tau)=S(z+\tfrac12;\tau+1)=S(z;\tau+2)$$ amd from these we deduce by a short calculation the two three-term functional equations $$\Phi(z;\tau)\;=\;\Phi\bigl(z\pm\frac12,\tau+1\bigr)\,\Phi\bigl(\frac{z\mp\tau/2}{\tau+1},\,\frac\tau{\tau+1}\bigr)$$ of $\Phi$. This is highly reminiscent of the fact (cf. [@LewisZag]) that the holomorphic function $$\psi(\tau)\;=\;f(\tau)-\tau^{-2s}f(-1/\tau)\,,\qquad f(\tau)\;=\;\begin{cases}\phantom{-}
   \sum_{n>0}a_nq^n&\text{ if $\mbox{Im }(\tau)>0$,} \\-\sum_{n<0}a_nq^n&\text{ if $\mbox{Im }(\tau)<0$} \end{cases}$$ associated to a Maass cusp form $u(\tau)$ on $SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})$ with spectral parameter $s$, where $a_n$ are the normalized coefficients in the Fourier-Bessel expansion of $u$, satisfies the Lewis functional equation $\psi(\tau)=\psi(\tau+1)+(\tau+1)^{-2s}\psi\bigl(\frac\tau{\tau+1}\bigr)$ and extends holomorphically from its initial domain of definition ${\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus{\mathbb{R}}$ to ${\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus(-\infty,0]\,$.

Finally, the quantum dilogarithm functions satisfies various five-term relations, of which the classical five-term functional equation of $\text{Li}_2(x)$ is a limiting case, when the arguments are non-commuting variables. The simplest and oldest is the identity $$(Y;q)_\infty\,(X;q)_\infty\;=\;(X;q)_\infty\,(-YX;q)_\infty\,(Y;q)_\infty \label{pre-pentagon}$$ for operators $X$ and $Y$ satisfying $XY=qYX$. From this one deduces the "quantum pentagon relation" $$\label{qdl_pentagon} \Phi_\hbar(\hat{p})\,\Phi_\hbar(\hat q)
  \;=\; \Phi_\hbar(\hat q)\,\Phi_\hbar(\hat p+\hat q)\,\Phi_\hbar(\hat p)$$ for operators $\hat{p}$ and $\hat{q}$ satisfying $[\hat{q},\hat{p}]=2\hbar$. Letting ${\bf S}_{ij}$ be a copy, acting on the $i^{{\rm th}}$ and $j^{{\rm th}}$ factors of $V\otimes V\otimes V$, of the ${\bf S}$-matrix introduced in [\[S-matrix\]](#S-matrix){reference-type="eqref" reference="S-matrix"}, we deduce from [\[qdl_pentagon\]](#qdl_pentagon){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_pentagon"} the operator identity $$\label{S_pentagon}
  {\bf S}_{23} {\bf S}_{12} \;=\; {\bf S}_{12} {\bf S}_{13} {\bf S}_{23}\,.$$ It is this very special property which guarantees that the gluing procedure used in the definition of [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"} is invariant under 2-3 Pachner moves on the underlying triangulations and produces a true hyperbolic invariant [@hikami-2001-16; @hikami-2006; @kashaev-2000]. This identity is also related to the fact (not used in this paper) that the fifth power of the operator which maps a nicely behaved function to the Fourier transform of its product with $\Phi_\hbar(z)$ (suitably normalized) is a multiple of the identity [@goncharov-2007].

## A state integral model for $Z^{(\rho)} (M;\hbar)$ {#sec:statesumdef}

Now, let us return to the analysis of the integral [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"} and compare it with the perturbative $SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})$ invariant $Z^{(\alpha)} (M;\hbar,u)$. Both invariants compute quantum (*i.e.* $\hbar$ - deformed) topological invariants of hyperbolic 3-manifolds and, thus, are expected to be closely related. However, in order to establish a precise relation, we need to face two problems mentioned in the beginning of this section:

$i)$ the integration contour is not specified in [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"}, and

$ii)$ the integral [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"} does not depend on the choice of the classical solution $\alpha$.

These two problems are related, and can be addressed by studying the integral [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"} in various saddle point approximations. Using the leading term in [\[qdl-hbarexpansion\]](#qdl-hbarexpansion){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl-hbarexpansion"}, we can approximate it to leading order as $$H (M;\hbar, u) \; \underset{\hbar \to 0}{\sim} \;
 \int\,e^{\frac{1}{\hbar} V(p_1, \ldots, p_{N-1}, u)}\; dp_1 \ldots dp_{N-1}\,,
\label{Hik_V_limit}$$ with the "potential" $$V({\bf p},u) \;=\; \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \epsilon_i \text{Li}_2(-\exp(g_i({\bf p},2u) + i\pi \epsilon_i))+\frac{1}{2} f({\bf p},2u,\hbar=0)\,.
\label{Hik_gen_V}$$ As explained below [\[qdl-hbarexpansion\]](#qdl-hbarexpansion){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl-hbarexpansion"}, the branches of $\text{Li}_2$ must be chosen appropriately to coincide with the half-lines of poles and zeroes of the quantum dilogarithms in [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"}. The leading contribution to $H (M;\hbar, u)$ will then come from the highest-lying critical point through which a given contour can be deformed.

It was the observation of [@hikami-2006] that the potential $V$ always has one critical point that reproduces the classical Chern-Simons action on the "geometric" branch, that is a critical point ${\bf p}^{({\rm geom})} (u)$ such that $$l_{{\rm geom}} (u) = \exp \left[ \textstyle \frac{d}{du} V({\bf p}^{({\rm geom})}(u), u) \right]
\label{lVgeom}$$ and $$S_0^{({\rm geom})} (u) = V({\bf p}^{({\rm geom})} (u), u)\,.
\label{SVgeom}$$ This identification follows from the fact that both $S_0^{({\rm geom})} (u)$ and matrix elements [\[S_element1\]](#S_element1){reference-type="eqref" reference="S_element1"}-[\[S_element2\]](#S_element2){reference-type="eqref" reference="S_element2"} in the limit $\hbar \to 0$ are related to the complexified volume function $i({\mbox{Vol\,}}(M;u) + i {\rm CS} (M;u))$. We want to argue presently that in fact *every* critical point of $V$ corresponds to a classical solution in Chern-Simons theory (that is, to a branch of $A(l,m)=0$) in this manner, with similar relations $$l_{\alpha} (u) =  e^{\frac{d}{du} V({\bf p}^{(\alpha)} (u), u)}
\label{lValpha}$$ and $$S_0^{(\alpha)} (u) = V({\bf p}^{(\alpha)} (u), u)
\label{SValpha}$$ for some $\alpha$. In particular, $l_{\alpha} (u)$ as given by [\[lValpha\]](#lValpha){reference-type="eqref" reference="lValpha"} and $m=e^u$ obey [\[abranches\]](#abranches){reference-type="eqref" reference="abranches"}.

To analyze generic critical points of $V$, observe that the critical point equations take the form $$2 \frac{\partial}{\partial p_j} V({\bf p},u) =
-\sum_{i=1}^{N} \epsilon_i G_{ji} \log(1+\exp(g_i({\bf p},2u)+ i\pi \epsilon_i))
+ \frac{\partial}{\partial p_j} {f} ({\bf p},2u,0) = 0 \quad\;\; \forall\; j\,,
\label{p_saddles}$$ where $G_{ji} = \frac{\partial}{\partial p_j} g_i({\bf p},2u)$ are some constants and the functions $\frac{\partial}{\partial p_j} {f} ({\bf p},2u,0)$ are linear. Again, the cuts of the logarithm must match the singularities of the quantum dilogarithm. Exponentiating [\[p_saddles\]](#p_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="p_saddles"}, we obtain another set of conditions $$r_j ({\bf x},m) = 1 \quad \forall\; j\,,
\label{x_saddles}$$ where $$r_j({\bf x},m) = \exp \Big( {2 \frac{\partial}{\partial p_j} V({\bf p},u)}\Big)
= \prod_i (1-e^{g_i})^{-\epsilon_i G_{ji}}\exp\Big({\frac{\partial}{\partial p_j}f}\Big)$$

are all *rational* functions of the variables $x_j = e^{p_j}$ and $m = e^u$. Note that an entire family of points $\{{\bf p} + 2\pi i {\bf n} \,|\, {\bf n} \in {\mathbb{Z}}^{N-1} \}$ maps to a single $\bf x$, and that all branch cut ambiguities disappear in the simpler equations [\[x_saddles\]](#x_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="x_saddles"}. Depending on $\arg(\hbar)$ and the precise form of $f$, solutions to [\[x_saddles\]](#x_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="x_saddles"} either "lift" uniquely to critical points of the potential $V$, or they lift to a family of critical points at which $V$ differs only by integer multiples of $2\pi i u$.

Now, the system [\[x_saddles\]](#x_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="x_saddles"} is algebraic, so its set of solutions defines a complex affine variety $${\cal R} = \{ (x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1},m) \in {\mathbb{C}}^N \,|\, r_j(x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1},m) = 0 \quad \forall\, j\}\,,$$ which is closely related to the representation variety ${\cal L}$ given by $A(l,m)=0$. Both generically have complex dimension one. Noting that $s(x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1}, m) = \exp \big( \frac{\partial}{\partial u} V \big)$ is also a rational function, we can define a rational map $\phi:{\mathbb{C}}^N \to {\mathbb{C}}^2$ by $$\phi (x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1}, m) = (s(x_1, \ldots, x_{N-1},m),m)\,.$$ The claim in [@hikami-2006] that one critical point of $V$ always corresponds to the geometric branch of ${\cal L}$ means that $\overline{\phi(\cal R)}$ (taking an algebraic closure) always intersects ${\cal L}$ nontrivially, along a subvariety of dimension 1. Thus, some irreducible component of $\overline{\phi(\cal R)}$, coming from an irreducible component of $\cal R$, must coincide with the entire irreducible component of ${\cal L}$ containing the geometric branch. Every solution ${\bf x}= {\bf x}(m)$ in this component of $\cal R$ corresponds to a branch of the A-polynomial. Moreover, if such a solution ${\bf x}^{(\alpha)}$ (corresponding to branch $\alpha$) can be lifted to a real critical point ${\bf p}^{(\alpha)} (u)$ of $V$, then one must have relations [\[lValpha\]](#lValpha){reference-type="eqref" reference="lValpha"} and [\[SValpha\]](#SValpha){reference-type="eqref" reference="SValpha"}.

This simple algebraic analysis shows that *some* solutions of [\[x_saddles\]](#x_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="x_saddles"} will cover an entire irreducible component of the curve ${\cal L}$ defined by $A(l,m)=0$. We cannot push the general argument further without knowing more about the reducibility of $\cal R$. However, we can look at some actual examples. Computing $V ({\bf p},u)$ for thirteen hyperbolic manifolds with a single torus boundary,

[^2]

we found in every case that solutions of [\[x_saddles\]](#x_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="x_saddles"} completely covered all non-abelian branches $\alpha \neq {\rm abel}$; in other words, $\overline{\phi(\cal R)} = {\cal L}'$, with ${\cal L}' = \{ A(l,m) / (l-1)=0 \}$. For six of these manifolds, we found unique critical points ${\bf p} (u)$ corresponding to every non-abelian branch of ${\cal L}$ at $\arg(\hbar)=i\pi$. Motivated by these examples, it is natural to state the following conjecture:\
**Conjecture 2**: *Every critical point of $V$ corresponds to some branch $\alpha$, and all $\alpha \neq {\rm abel}$ are obtained in this way. Moreover, for every critical point ${\bf p}^{(\alpha)}(u)$ (corresponding to some branch $\alpha$) we have [\[lValpha\]](#lValpha){reference-type="eqref" reference="lValpha"}-[\[SValpha\]](#SValpha){reference-type="eqref" reference="SValpha"} and to all orders in perturbation theory: $$Z^{(\alpha)} (M; \hbar, u)
= \sqrt{2} \int_{C_{\alpha}}
\prod_{i=1}^{N} \Phi_{\hbar} \big( \, g_i ({\bf p}, 2u) +\epsilon_i (i \pi + \hbar) \,\big)^{\epsilon_i}\,
e^{\frac{1}{2\hbar} f({\bf p}, 2u, \hbar) - u} \; \prod_{j=1}^{N-1} \frac{dp_j}{\sqrt{4\pi\hbar}}\,,
\label{statesumdef}$$ where $C_{\alpha}$ is an arbitrary contour with fixed endpoints which passes through ${\bf p}^{(\alpha)}(u)$ and no other critical point.*\
A slightly more conservative version of this conjecture might state that only those $\alpha$ that belong to the same irreducible component of ${\cal L}$ as the geometric branch, $\alpha = {\rm geom}$, are covered by critical points of $V$. Indeed, the "abelian" branch with $l_{{\rm abel}} = 1$ is not covered by the critical points of $V$ and it belongs to the separate component $(l-1)$ of the curve $A(l,m)=0$. It would be interesting to study the relation between critical points of $V$ and irreducible components of ${\cal L}$ further, in particular by looking at examples with reducible A-polynomials aside from the universal $(l-1)$ factor.

The right-hand side of [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} is the proposed state integral model for the *exact* perturbative partition function of $SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})$ Chern-Simons theory on a hyperbolic 3-manifold $M$ with a single torus boundary $\Sigma = T^2$. (A generalization to 3-manifolds with an arbitrary number of boundary components is straightforward.) This state integral model is a modified version of Hikami's invariant [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"}. Just like its predecessor, eq. [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} is based on an ideal triangulation $\{\Delta_i\}_{i=1}^N$ of a hyperbolic 3-manifold $M$ and inherits topological invariance from the pentagon identity [\[qdl_pentagon\]](#qdl_pentagon){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl_pentagon"} of the quantum dilogarithm.

However, in writing [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} we made two important modifications to Hikami's invariant [\[gen_Hik_integral\]](#gen_Hik_integral){reference-type="eqref" reference="gen_Hik_integral"}. First, we introduced contours $C_{\alpha}$ running across the sadle points ${\bf p}_\alpha$, which now encode the choice of a classical solution in Chern-Simons theory. Second, in [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} we introduced an extra factor of $\sqrt{8 \pi \hbar} e^{-u}$, which is needed to reproduce the correct asymptotic behavior of $Z^{(\alpha)} (M; \hbar, u)$. To understand this correction factor, we must look at the higher-order terms in the expansion of $Z^{(\alpha)}(M;\hbar,u)$. By using [\[qdl-hbarexpansion\]](#qdl-hbarexpansion){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl-hbarexpansion"}, one can continue the saddle point approximations described above to arbitrary order in $\hbar$. The result has the expected form [\[zpert\]](#zpert){reference-type="eqref" reference="zpert"}, $$Z^{(\alpha)} (M;\hbar,u) =
\exp\left( \frac{1}{\hbar} S_0^{(\alpha)} (u) - \frac{1}{2}\delta^{(\alpha)} \log \hbar
+ \sum_{n=0}^\infty S_{n+1}^{(\alpha)} (u) \hbar^n  \right),
\label{zssspert}$$ with the correct leading term $S_0^{(\alpha)} (u)$ that we already analyzed above, *cf.* eq. [\[SValpha\]](#SValpha){reference-type="eqref" reference="SValpha"}.

Let us examine the next-leading logarithmic term. Its coefficient $\delta^{(\alpha)}$ receives contributions from two places: from the prefactor $(4\pi \hbar)^{-(N-1)/2}$ in [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"}, and from the standard Gaussian determinant. The former depends on the total number of tetrahedra, $N$, in the triangulation of $M$ and therefore must be cancelled (at least partially) since the total integral [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} is a topological invariant and cannot depend on $N$. This is indeed what happens. For example, in a saddle point approximation around a nondegenerate critical point ${\bf p}^{(\alpha)}(u)$, the contribution of the Gaussian determinant goes like $\sim \hbar^{(N-1)/2}$ and exactly cancels the contribution of the prefactor $\sim \hbar^{- (N-1)/2}$. An example of such critical point is the critical point ${\bf p}^{({\rm geom})} (u)$ corresponding to the geometric branch. Therefore, the asymptotic expansion of the integral [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} around the critical point ${\bf p}^{({\rm geom})} (u)$ has the form [\[zssspert\]](#zssspert){reference-type="eqref" reference="zssspert"} with $$\delta^{({\rm geom})} = 0\,,
\label{dgeomzero}$$ which is the expected result.[^3] Indeed, as explained *e.g.* in [@gukov-2003; @gukov-2006], the rigidity of the flat connection ${\cal A}^{({\rm geom})}$ associated with a hyperbolic structure on $M$ implies $h^0 = h^1 = 0$, so that [\[dhh\]](#dhh){reference-type="eqref" reference="dhh"} gives $\delta^{({\rm geom})} = 0$.

Using [\[qdl-hbarexpansion\]](#qdl-hbarexpansion){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl-hbarexpansion"} , one can also calculate the higher-order perturbative coefficients $S_n^{(\alpha)}(u)$, with $n \ge 1$. In the following section, we carry out this analysis to high order for the figure-8 knot complement and find perfect agreement with the results obtained by methods of Section [\[sec:pert\]](#sec:pert){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:pert"}. (Other interesting examples and further checks will appear elsewhere [@dimofte-2009].)

Note that the $S_n^{(\alpha)}(u)$'s do not depend on the details of the contours $C_{\alpha}$. The only part of [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} which actually depends on the details of $C_\alpha$ is exponentially suppressed and is not part of the perturbative series [\[zssspert\]](#zssspert){reference-type="eqref" reference="zssspert"}. Finally, we also note that we use only those critical points of the full integrand [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} which correspond to critical points of $V$ in the limit $\hbar \to 0$. For any fixed $\hbar > 0$, the actual integrand has many other critical points which become trapped in the half-line singularities of quantum dilogarithms as $\hbar \to 0$, so the integrals over them do not have a well-behaved limit.

We conclude this section by observing that Conjecture 2 implies Conjecture 1. From [\[qdl-hbarexpansion\]](#qdl-hbarexpansion){reference-type="eqref" reference="qdl-hbarexpansion"}, we can write an asymptotic double series expansion (for very small $p$):

$$\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{\hbar} (p_0 + p) &= \exp\left( \sum_{n=0}^\infty  B_n\bigl(\frac12+\frac
p{2\hbar}\bigr)\,\text{Li}_{2-n}\bigl(-e^{p_0}\bigr)
\,\frac{(2\hbar)^{n-1}}{n!}\right) \notag \\
 &= \exp \left( \sum_{k=-1}^\infty \sum_{j=0}^\infty
\frac{B_{k+1}(1/2)\,2^k}{(k+1)! j!} \text{Li}_{1-j-k} (-e^{p_0})\,\hbar^k p^j \right).
\label{qdl_exp}
\end{aligned}$$ Using this formula and taking into account the shifts by $\pm(i\pi+\hbar)$, we expand every quantum dilogarithm appearing in the integrand of [\[statesumdef\]](#statesumdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="statesumdef"} around a critical point ${\bf p}^{(\alpha)}$. At each order in $\hbar$, the state integral model then reduces to an integral of a polynomial in ${\bf p}$ with a Gaussian weight. Due to the fact that $\text{Li}_k$ is a rational function for $k\le 0$, the coefficients of these polynomials are all rational functions of the variables ${\bf x}^{(\alpha)} = \exp ({\bf p}^{(\alpha)})$ and $m$. Therefore, the resulting coefficients $S_n^{(\alpha)}(m)$, for $n>1$, will also be rational functions of ${\bf x}^{(\alpha)}$ and $m$. At $m=1$, the solutions ${\bf x}^{(\alpha)} (m)$ to the rational equations [\[x_saddles\]](#x_saddles){reference-type="eqref" reference="x_saddles"} all belong to some algebraic number field $\mathbb{K} \subset \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, leading immediately to Conjecture 1. In particular, for the geometric branch $\alpha = {\rm geom}$, the field $\mathbb{K}$ is nothing but the trace field $\mathbb{Q} ({\rm tr\,}\Gamma)$.
