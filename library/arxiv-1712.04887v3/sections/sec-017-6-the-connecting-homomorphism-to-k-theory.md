[]{#sec.K2F label="sec.K2F"}

In this section, we give a proof of Theorem [\[thm.K2\]](#thm.K2){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.K2"}. Assume that $F$ is a field of characteristic prime to $p$ which does not contain a $p$th root of unity. Recall that $Z(F)$ is the free abelian group on $F \smallsetminus\{0,1\}$ and $C(F)$ the subgroup generated by the $5$-term relation.

::: definition
Let $A(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)$ be the kernel of the map $$d: Z(F) \;\longrightarrow\;\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times} \otimes \mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z\,, \qquad 
[X] \mapsto X \wedge (1-X) \,.$$ The étale Bloch group $B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)$ is the quotient $$B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) =  A(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)/(nZ(F)+ C(F)) \,.$$ It is annihilated by $n$.
:::

There is a tautological exact sequence $$0 \rightarrow B(F) \rightarrow Z(F)/C(F) \rightarrow  \mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}
\rightarrow K_2(F) \rightarrow 0 \,.$$ For appropriately defined $R$, we may break this into the two short exact sequences as follows: $$\begin{diagram}
0 & \rTo & A(F) & \rTo &  Z(F) & \rTo &  R & \rTo &  0, \\
 & & \dOnto & & \dOnto & & \dEquals & & \\
0 & \rTo & B(F) & \rTo &  Z(F)/C(F) & \rTo &  R & \rTo &  0,
\end{diagram}$$ $$\label{rk2}
0 \rightarrow R \rightarrow \mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times} \rightarrow  K_2(F) 
\rightarrow 0.$$

Similarly, for some $Q$, with $Q \subseteq R$ and $nR \subseteq Q \subseteq n\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}$, we have corresponding short exact sequences: $$\begin{diagram}
0 & \rTo & A(F) & \rTo &   A(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) & \rTo &  Q & \rTo &  0, \\
 & & \dOnto & & \dOnto & & \dOnto & & \\
 0 & \rTo &  A(F)/(n Z(F) + C(F)) & \rTo &  
A(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)/(n Z(F) + C(F)) & \rTo &
   Q/nR & \rTo &  0\\
  & & \dEquals & & \dEquals & & \dEquals & & \\
0 & \rTo & B(F)/n B(F)  & \rTo &  B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) & \rTo &  Q/nR & \rTo &  0,
\end{diagram}$$

From now on, we make the assumption that the number field $F$ does not contain a $p$th root of unity for any $p$ dividing $n$. This implies from the previous inclusions that $Q$ and $R$ are all $p$-torsion free for $p | n$. Tensor the exact sequence [\[rk2\]](#rk2){reference-type="eqref" reference="rk2"} with $\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z$. The group $\mathrm{Tor}^1(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z,\wedge^2 F^{\times})$ vanishes by our assumption. Hence we have an exact sequence: $$\label{k2long}
0 \rightarrow K_2(F)[n] \rightarrow
R/nR \rightarrow \mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times} \otimes \mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z
\rightarrow K_2(F)/nK_2(F) \rightarrow 0.$$

Recall that $R$ is the image of $Z(F)$ in $\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}$ and $Q$ is the image of $A(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)$, which is precisely the kernel of the map from $R$ to $\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times} \otimes \mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z$. It follows that the image of $Q$ in $R/nR$ is the kernel of the map from $R/nR$ to $\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times} \otimes \mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z$. From the short exact sequence [\[k2long\]](#k2long){reference-type="eqref" reference="k2long"}, this may be identified with $K_2(F)[n]$. Since the image of $Q$ in $R/nR$ is precisely $Q/nR$, however, this shows that $Q/nR \simeq K_2(F)$, we obtain the exact sequence: $$0 \;\longrightarrow\;B(F)/nB(F) \;\longrightarrow\;B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) \;\longrightarrow\;K_2(F)[n] \;\longrightarrow\;0 \,,$$ completing the proof of Theorem [\[thm.K2\]](#thm.K2){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.K2"}.

The previous result was a diagram chase. The map $\delta: B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) \rightarrow K_2(F)$ can be given explicitly as follows: Lift $[x] \in B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)$ to an element $x$ of $A(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z)/C(F)$, which is unique up to an element of $nZ(F)$. The image of $x$ in $\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times} \otimes \mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z$ is zero by definition. Hence, because $\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}$ is $p$-torsion free for $p | n$, there exists an element $y \in \mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}$ such that the image of $z$ in $\mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}$ is $ny$, and now $y$ is unique up to an element in the image of $C(F)$. But the projection $z$ of $y \in \mbox{\small$\bigwedge^2$}F^{\times}$ to $K_2(F)$ sends this ambiguity $C(F)$ to zero, and so $\delta([x]):=z \in K_2(F)$ is well defined.

If we assume that $n$ is not divisible by any prime $p$ which divides $w_2(F)$, we have constructed a map $$\label{newchern}
R_{\zeta}: B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) \rightarrow 
(F^{\times}_{n}/F^{\times n}_{n})^{\chi^{-1}}
\simeq H^1(F,\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z(2)) \,.$$ Taking $n= p^{m}$ for various $m$, and using the fact that $B(F)$ is finitely generated and so $\projlim B(F)/p^{m} B(F) = B(F) \otimes \mathbf Z_p$, we obtain a commutative diagram as follows: $$\begin{diagram} \label{eq:kshort}
0 & \rTo & B(F)/nB(F) & \rTo & B(F;\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z) 
& \rTo & K_2(F)[n] & \rTo & 0\\
 & & \dTo & & \dTo & & \dTo & & \\



0 & \rTo & H^1(F,\mathbf Z_p(2))/n& \rTo & H^1(F,\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z(2))
& \rTo & H^2(F,\mathbf Z_p(2))[n] & \rTo & 0, 
\end{diagram}$$ The first vertical map is an isomorphism by Theorem [\[theorem:surjective2\]](#theorem:surjective2){reference-type="ref" reference="theorem:surjective2"}, taking into account the identification of $B(F)/n B(F)$ with $K_3(F)/n K_3(F)$ for $(n,w_F) = 1$ and equation ([\[useful\]](#useful){reference-type="ref" reference="useful"}). The last vertical map is also an isomorphism by a theorem of Tate [@TateK]. It follows that the map $R_{\zeta}$ in Equation [\[newchern\]](#newchern){reference-type="ref" reference="newchern"} is an isomorphism for $n$ prime to $w_2(F)$. This gives a link between our explicit construction of Chern class maps for $K_3(F)$ and the explicit construction of $K_2(F)$ in Galois cohomology by Tate [@TateK].

We end this section with a remark on circular units. Let $F = \mathbf Q(\zeta_D)$. Associated to a primitive $D$th root of unity $\zeta_D$, Beilinson (see §9 of [@Huber]) constructed special generating elements of $K_3(F)$, which correspond, on the Bloch group side, to the classes $D \cdot [\zeta_D] \in B(F)$. (Note that $D \cdot \zeta_D \wedge (1-\zeta_D)
= \zeta^D_D \wedge (1 - \zeta_D) = 0 \in \wedge^2 F^{\times}$ so $D \cdot [\zeta_D]$ does indeed lie in the Bloch group.) Soulé [@Soule3] proved that the images of these classes under the Chern class map consist exactly of the circular units. On the other hand, for $p$ not dividing $D$, we see that the images of $D [\zeta_D]$ under the maps $R_\zeta$ are unit multiples of the elements $$\prod_{k=0}^{p^{m-1}} (1 - \zeta^k\,\zeta_D)^k\,;$$ these are exactly the compatible sequences of circular units which yield a finite index subgroup of $H^1(F,\mathbf Z_p(2))$ --- the index being directly related to $K_2(\mathcal{O}_F)$ via the Quillen--Lichtenbaum conjectures.

# Nahm's conjecture and the asymptotics of Nahm sums at roots of unity {#sec.nahm}

In Section [\[sub.intro.nahm\]](#sub.intro.nahm){reference-type="ref" reference="sub.intro.nahm"} of the introduction, we saw that the $S$-units constructed in this paper from elements of the Bloch group appear naturally (although in general only conjecturally) in connection with the asymptotic properties of the Kashaev invariant of knots and its Galois twists. A second place where these units appear is in the radial asymptotics of so-called Nahm sums. This was shown in [@GZ:asymptotics] and is quoted (in a simplified form) in Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} below. In this section, we explain this and give two applications, the proof of Theorem [\[thm.eta\]](#thm.eta){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.eta"} and the proof of Nahm's conjecture relating the modularity of Nahm sums to the vanishing of certain elements in Bloch groups.

Nahm sums are special $q$-hypergeometric series whose summand involves a quadratic form, a linear form and a constant. They were introduced by Nahm [@Nahm] in connection with characters of rational conformal field theories, and led to his above-mentioned conjecture concerning their modularity. They have also appeared recently in quantum topology in relation to the stabilization of the coefficients of the colored Jones polynomial (see Garoufalidis-Le [@GL:Nahm]), and they are building blocks of the 3D-index of an ideally triangulated manifold due to Dimofte--Gaiotto--Gukov [@DGG1; @DGG2]. Further connections between quantum topological invariants and Nahm sums are given in [@GZ:qseries], where one sees once again the appearance of the units $R_\zeta(\xi)^{1/n}$.

In the first subsection of this section, we review Nahm sums and the Nahm conjecture and state Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} relating the asymptotics of Nahm sums at roots of unity to the near units of Theorem [\[thm.R\]](#thm.R){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.R"}. This is then applied in §[1.2](#sub.ag){reference-type="ref" reference="sub.ag"} to a particular Nahm sum (namely, the famous Andrews-Gordon generalization of the Rogers-Ramanujan identities) to prove Equation [\[CompRz\]](#CompRz){reference-type="eqref" reference="CompRz"} of the introduction (Theorem [\[thm.eta\]](#thm.eta){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.eta"}). In the final subsection, we use Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} together with Theorem [\[thm.R\]](#thm.R){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.R"} to give a proof of Nahm's conjecture.

## Nahm's conjecture and Nahm sums {#sub.nahm}

Nahm's conjecture gives a very surprising connection between modularity and algebraic $K$-theory. More precisely, it predicts that the modularity of certain $q$-hypergeometric series ("Nahm sums\") is controlled by the vanishing of certain associated elements in the Bloch group $B(\overline{\mathbf Q})$.

The definition of Nahm sums and the question of determining when they are modular were motivated by the famous Rogers-Ramanujan identities, which say that $$G(q)\,:=\,\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{q^{n^2}}{(q)_n}
 \,=\,\prod_{\substack{ n>0 \\ (\frac n5)=1}}\!\frac1{1-q^n},\qquad
H(q)\,:=\,\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{q^{n^2+n}}{(q)_n}
 \,=\,\prod_{\substack{ n>0 \\ (\frac n5)=-1}}\!\frac1{1-q^n}\,,$$ where $(q)_n=(1-q)\cdots(1-q^n)$ is the $q$-Pochhammer symbol or quantum $n$-factorial. These identities imply via the Jacobi triple product formula that the two functions $q^{-1/60}G(q)$ and $q^{11/60}H(q)$ are quotients of unary theta-series by the Dedekind eta-function and hence are modular functions. (Here and from now on we will allow ourselves the abuse of terminology of saying that a function $f(q)$ is modular if the function $\widetilde f(\tau)=f(e^{2\pi i\tau})$ is invariant under the action of some subgroup of finite index of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$.) To see how general this phenomenon might be, Nahm [@Nahm] considered the three-parameter family $$\label{eq.FABC1}
f_{A,B,C}(q) :=q^C f_{A,B}(q) := q^C
\sum_{m\ge0} \frac{q^{\frac A2m^2+Bm}}{(q)_m} \qquad(A\in\mathbf Q_{>0},\;B,\,C\in\mathbf Q)$$ These are formal power series with integer coefficients in some rational power of $q$, and are analytic in the unit disk $|q|<1$, but they are very seldom modular: apart from the two Rogers-Ramanujan cases $(A,B,C)=(2,0,-\frac1{60})$ or $(2,1,\frac{11}{60})$, only five further cases $(1,0,-\frac1{48})$, $(1,\pm\frac12,\frac1{24})$, $(\frac12,0,-\frac1{40})$ and $(\frac12,\frac12,\frac1{40})$ were known for which $f_{A,B,C}$ is modular, and it was later proved ([@Terhoeven], [@Zagier]) that these are in fact the only ones. Since this list of seven examples is not very enlightening, Nahm introduced also a higher-order version $$\label{eq.FABC}
f_{A,B,C}(q)  :=q^C f_{A,B}(q):= q^C
\sum_{m\in\mathbf Z_{\ge0}^r} \frac{q^{\frac12 m^tAm + Bm}}{(q)_{m_1}\cdots(q)_{m_r}}\,,$$ where $A=(a_{ij})$ is a symmetric positive definite $r \times r$ matrix with rational entries, $B \in \mathbf Q^r$ a column vector, and $C \in \mathbf Q$ a scalar, and asked for which triples $(A,B,C)$ the function $\widetilde f_{A,B,C}(\tau)=f_{A,B,C}(e^{2\pi i\tau})$ is modular. His conjecture gives a partial answer to this question.

To formulate this conjecture, Nahm made two preliminary observations.

\(i\) Let $X=(X_1,\dots,X_r)\in\mathbf C^r$ be a solution of *Nahm's equations* $$\label{NahmEq}
1 \,-\, X_i \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^r X_j^{a_{ij}} \qquad(1\le j\le r)$$ (or symbolically $1-X=X^A$), and let $F$ be the field they generate over $\mathbf Q$, which will typically be a number field since [\[NahmEq\]](#NahmEq){reference-type="eqref" reference="NahmEq"} is a system of $r$ equations in $r$ unknowns and generically defines a 0-dimensional variety. Then the element $[X]=[X_1]+\cdots[X_r]$ of $\mathbf Z[F]$ belongs to the kernel of the map [\[defd\]](#defd){reference-type="eqref" reference="defd"}, because $$d\bigl([X]\bigr) \;=\; \sum_i(X_i)\wedge(1-X_i) \;=\; 
\sum_{i,\,j} a_{ij}\,(X_i)\wedge(X_j)\;=\;0$$ by virtue of the symmetry of $A$. (This calculation makes sense as it stands if $A$ has integer entries; if the entries are only rational, we have to tensor everything with $\mathbf Q$.) Therefore $[X]$ determines an element of the Bloch group $B(F)\otimes\mathbf Q$ and it makes sense to ask whether this element vanishes. This is equivalent to the vanishing of the numbers $D(\sigma X)=\sum D(\sigma X_i)$ for all embeddings $\sigma:F\hookrightarrow\mathbf C$, where $D(x)$ is the Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm function, and this condition can be either tested numerically to any precision or else verified rigorously by writing a multiple of $[X]$ as a linear combination of 5-term relations.

\(ii\) The first observation is that if $A$ is a positive definite symmetric matrix, then there is a distinguished solution of the Nahm equations, namely the unique solution $X^A=(X_1^A,\dots,X_r^A)$ with $0<X_i^A<1$ for all $i$. We denote by $\xi_A$ the corresponding element $[X^A]$ of the Bloch group. Then since $X^A$ is real, we obtain a further characteristic property when this element is torsion, namely that the real number $\mathrm L(\xi_A)=\sum\mathrm L(X_i)$, where $\mathrm L(x)$ is the Rogers dilogarithm function as defined below, is a rational multiple of $\pi^2$. But it can be shown fairly easily that $f_{A,B,C}(e^{-h})$ has an asymptotic expansion as $\,e^{\mathrm L(\xi_A)/h+\text O(1)}$ as $h\to0^+$ for any $B$ and $C$ (in fact, a full asymptotic expansion of the form $\,e^{\mathrm L(\xi_A)/h+c_0+c_1h+\cdots}$ is given in [@Zagier]). Since a modular function must have an expansion $\,e^{c/h+\text O(1)}$ with $c\in\mathbf Q\pi^2$, this already gives a strong indication of a relation between the modularity of Nahm sums and the vanishing (up to torsion) of the associated elements of Bloch groups.

Based on these observations, one can consider the following three properties of a matrix $A$ as above:

1.  []{#nahma label="nahma"} The class $[X]\in B(\mathbf C)$ vanishes for all solutions $X$ of the Nahm equations [\[NahmEq\]](#NahmEq){reference-type="eqref" reference="NahmEq"}.

2.  []{#nahmb label="nahmb"} The special class $\xi_A\in B(\mathbf C)$ associated to the solution $X^A$ of [\[NahmEq\]](#NahmEq){reference-type="eqref" reference="NahmEq"} vanishes.

3.  []{#nahmc label="nahmc"} The function $f_{A,B,C}(q)$ is modular for some $B\in\mathbf Q^r$ and $C\in\mathbf Q$.

Trivially [\[nahma\]](#nahma){reference-type="ref" reference="nahma"} $\Rightarrow$ [\[nahmb\]](#nahmb){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmb"}. Nahm's conjecture (see [@Nahm] and [@Zagier]) says that [\[nahma\]](#nahma){reference-type="ref" reference="nahma"} $\Rightarrow$ [\[nahmc\]](#nahmc){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmc"} and [\[nahmc\]](#nahmc){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmc"} $\Rightarrow$ [\[nahmb\]](#nahmb){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmb"}. (The possible stronger hypothesis that [\[nahmb\]](#nahmb){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmb"} alone might already imply [\[nahmc\]](#nahmc){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmc"} was eliminated in [@Zagier] using the $2\times2$ matrix $A=(\begin{smallmatrix} 8 & 5 \\ 5 & 4\end{smallmatrix})$, and the other possible stronger assertion that [\[nahmc\]](#nahmc){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmc"} might require [\[nahma\]](#nahma){reference-type="ref" reference="nahma"} was shown to be false by Vlasenko and Zwegers [@VZ] with the counterexample $A=(\begin{smallmatrix} 3/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 3/2\end{smallmatrix})$.) This conjecture had a dual motivation: on the one hand, the above-mentioned fact that both [\[nahmb\]](#nahmb){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmb"} and [\[nahmc\]](#nahmc){reference-type="ref" reference="nahmc"} force the rationality of $L(\xi_A)/\pi^2$, which is most unlikely to happen "at random,\" and on the other hand, a large number of supporting examples coming from the characters of rational conformal field theories, which are always modular functions and where the condition in the Bloch group can also be verified in many cases. Here we are concerned with an extension of the first of these two aspects, namely the asymptotics of the Nahm sum $f_{A,B,C}(q)$ as $q$ tends radially to *any* root of unity, not just to 1.

In order to state the asymptotic formula, we need to define the Rogers dilogarithm. In our normalization (which is $\pi^2/6$ minus the standard one as given, for instance, in [@Zagier], §II.1A), this is the function defined on $\mathbf R\smallsetminus\{0,1\}$ by $$\label{normalization}
\mathrm L(x) \;=\; 
\begin{cases}
\frac{\pi^2}6 \,-\,\mathrm{Li}_2(x) \,-\,\frac12\,\log(x)\,\log(1-x) & \text{if $0<x<1$,}\\
\,-\,\mathrm L(1/x) & \text{if $x>1$,} \\  \frac{\pi^2}6 \,-\,\mathrm L(1-x) & \text{if $x<0$}
\end{cases}$$ (here $\mathrm{Li}_2(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n^2}$ is the standard dilogarithm) and extended by continuity to a function $\mathbf P^1(\mathbf R)\to\mathbf R/\frac{\pi^2}2\mathbf Z$ by sending the three points $0$, 1 and $\infty$ to $\frac{\pi^2}6$, 0, and $-\frac{\pi^2}6$. Its linear extension to $Z(\mathbf R)$ vanishes on the group $C(\mathbf R)$ as defined at the beginning of §[\[sub.intro.bloch\]](#sub.intro.bloch){reference-type="ref" reference="sub.intro.bloch"}.

We comment here that the specific choice of the definition of the Bloch group in

Definition [\[def.BF\]](#def.BF){reference-type="ref" reference="def.BF"}, which forces $3[0]=0$, $[X]+[1/X]=0$ and $[X]+[1-X]=[0]$ for any field $F$ and any element $X$ of $\mathbf P^1(F)$, was chosen precisely so that $L$ is well-defined on $B(\mathbf R)$ and takes values in the group $\mathbf R/\frac{\pi^2}2\mathbf Z$ rather than just its quotient $\mathbf R/\frac{\pi^2}6\mathbf Z$.

Specifically, let $A$, $B$ and $C$ be as above let $X=X^A$ be the distinguished solution of [\[NahmEq\]](#NahmEq){reference-type="eqref" reference="NahmEq"} as in (ii) and $F$ the corresponding number field, and for each integer $n$ choose a primitive $n$th root of unity $\zeta$, set $F_n=F(\zeta)$ and denote by $H=H_n$ the Kummer extension of $F_n$ obtained by adjoining the positive $n$th roots $x_i$ of the $X_i$. We are interested in the asymptotic expansion of $f_{A,B,C}(\zeta e^{-h/n})$ as $h\to0^+$. Strictly speaking, this only makes sense if $A$ has integral coefficients, $B$ is congruent to $\frac12\text{diag}(A)$ modulo $\mathbf Z^r$, and $C\in\mathbf Z$, since otherwise the quadratic function $q^{\frac12nAn^t+nB+C}$ occurring in the definition of $f_{A,B,C}$ is not uniquely defined. We get around this by picking a representation of $\zeta$ as $\mathbf{e}(a/n)$ for some $a\in\mathbf Z$ (where $\mathbf{e}(x)=e^{2\pi i x}$) and interpreting $f_{A,B,C}(\zeta e^{-h/n})$ as $\widetilde f_{A,B,C}\bigl(\frac{a+i\hbar}n\bigr)$, where $\hbar=\frac h{2\pi}$. The full asymptotic expansion of $f_{A,B,C}(\zeta e^{-h/n})$ as $h\to0^+$ was calculated in [@GZ:asymptotics] using the Euler--Maclaurin formula, generalizing an earlier result in [@Zagier] for the case $n=1$. We do not give the complete formula here, but only the simplified form as needed for the applications we will give. In the statement of the theorem we have abbreviated by $\Delta_X$ the diagonal matrix whose diagonal is a given vector $X$.

::: theorem
[@GZ:asymptotics Thm 3.1] []{#thm.nahmEM label="thm.nahmEM"}

Let $A \in M_r(\mathbf Q)$ and $B \in \mathbf Q^r$ be as above. Let $n$ be a positive integer coprime to the denominator of $A$ and $B$. Then for every primitive $n$th root of unity $\zeta$, we have $$\label{eq.nahmEM}
f_{A,B} \bigl(\zeta\,e^{-h/n}\bigr)  \;=\;  \mu \, \omega 
\,e^{\mathrm L(\xi_A)/nh}\, \bigl(\Phi_\zeta(h) \,+\, \text{O}(h^K)\bigr)$$ for all $K>0$ as $h\to0^+$, where $\omega^2 \in F^\times$, $\mu = \mathbf{e}(r(n-1)(n-2)/24 n)$, and $\Phi_\zeta(h)=\Phi_{A,B,\zeta}(h)$ is an explicit power series satisfying the two properties $\Phi_\zeta(h)^n\in F_n[[h]]$ and $$P_\zeta(\xi_A)^{1/n} {D_{\zeta}(1)^{r/n}} \,\Phi_\zeta(h)\in F_n[[h]].$$ Moreover, if $\Phi_\zeta(0)^n\neq 0$, then its image in $F_n^\times/F_n^{\times n}$ belongs to the $\chi^{-1}$ eigenspace.
:::

::: corollary
[]{#UnitCor label="UnitCor"} If $\Phi_\zeta(0) \neq 0$, then

the product of the power series $\Phi_\zeta(h)$ with $\varepsilon^{1/n}$ for any unit $\varepsilon$ representing $R_\zeta(\xi_A)$ belongs to $F_n[[h]]\,$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Let $\varepsilon\in F_n^\times$ denote a representative of $R_\zeta(\xi_A)$. On the one hand, Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} and Remark [\[rem.effectiveR\]](#rem.effectiveR){reference-type="ref" reference="rem.effectiveR"} imply that $\Phi_\zeta(0) \varepsilon^{1/n} \in F_n^\times$. On the other hand, Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} and our assumption implies that $(\Phi_\zeta(h)/\Phi_\zeta(0))^n \in F_n[[h]]$. Since $\Phi_\zeta(h)/\Phi_\zeta(0)$ is a power series with constant term $1$, it follows that $\Phi_\zeta(h)/\Phi_\zeta(0) \in F_n[[h]]$. Combining both conclusions, it follows that $\varepsilon^{1/n} \Phi_\zeta(h) \in F_n[[h]]$. ◻
:::

::: remark
In the theorem, we do *not* assert that the power series $\Phi$ cannot vanish identically (which is why we wrote an equality sign and $\Phi(h)+\text O(h^K)$ in [\[eq.nahmEM\]](#eq.nahmEM){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq.nahmEM"} rather than writing an asymptotic equality sign and putting simply $\Phi(h)$ on the right), and indeed this often happens, for instance, when $f_{A,B,C}$ is modular and we are expanding at a cusp not equivalent to 0. Of course, the corollary is vacuous if $\Phi$ vanishes.
:::

## Application to the calculation of $R_\zeta(\eta_\zeta)$ {#sub.ag}

In this subsection, we apply Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} and its corollary to a specific Nahm sum to prove equation [\[CompRz\]](#CompRz){reference-type="eqref" reference="CompRz"} in the introduction.

::: theorem
[]{#thm.eta label="thm.eta"} Let $n$ be odd and $\eta_\zeta$ be the $n$-torsion element in $B(\mathbf Q(\zeta)^{+})$ defined by [\[eq.etaz\]](#eq.etaz){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq.etaz"}, where $\zeta$ is a primitive $n$th root of unity. Then $R_\zeta(\eta_\zeta) \;=\; \zeta^2\,$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* The case $n=1$ is trivial, so assume that $n\ge 3$. Set $A_n=\bigl(2\min(i,j)\bigr)_{1\le i,j\le r}$, where $r = (n- 3)/2$. If $n\ge 5$, let $f_n$ be the $r$-dimensional Nahm sum $f_{A_n,0}$. If $n= 3$, we let $f_{3} = 1$, which is also the natural interpretation of the corresponding $0$-dimensional Nahm sum. By a famous identity of Andrews and Gordon [@Andrews], which reduces to the first Ramanujan-Rogers identity when $n=5$, we have the product expansion $$\label{AGid}
f_n(q) \;=\; 
\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}
  k>0\\2k\not\equiv0, \pm 1 \, (\;\text{\rm mod}\;n) \end{subarray}} \frac{1}{1-q^k}.$$ and this is modular up to a power of $q$ for the same reason as for $G(q)=f_5(q)$ (quotient of a theta series by the Dedekind eta-function). This modularity allows us to compute its asymptotics as $q\to\zeta_n$, and by comparing the result with the general asymptotics of Nahm sums as given in [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"}, we will obtain the desired evaluation of $\eta_n$. We now give details.

It is easy to check that all solutions $X$ of the Nahm equation $1-X=X^{A_n}$ have the form $$X=(X_{r+1},\dots,X_2),
\qquad X_k  \;=\; \frac{(1-\zeta^{k-1})(1-\zeta^{k+1})}{(1-\zeta^{k})^2}$$ with $\zeta$ a primitive $n$ root of unity, and hence form a single Galois orbit. The distinguished solution $X^{A_n}\in(0,1)^r$ corresponds to $\zeta=\mathbf{e}(1/n)=\zeta_n$. From the equation $$\label{eq:comparetorsion}
1-X_k=\left(\frac{\zeta^{1/2}-\zeta^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{k/2}-\zeta^{-k/2}}\right)^2$$ and the functional equation $L(1-X)=\frac{\pi^2}6-L(X)$, we find $$\mathrm L({[X]}) \;=\;  {\sum_{k=2}^{r+1} L(X_k)  }
= \frac12\,\sum_{{k=2}}^{{n-2}}\biggl(\frac{\pi^2}6 
\,-\,\mathrm L\biggl(\frac{\sin^2(\pi/n)}{\sin^2(k \pi/n)}\biggr)\biggr) 
=  \frac{(n-3)\pi^2}{6n}\,,$$ the invoked equality being a well-known identity for the Rogers dilogarithm of which a proof can be found at the end of [@Zagier], §II.2C.

Denote the right-hand side of this by $-4\pi^2C_n$ and set $\widetilde f_n(\tau)=\widetilde f_{A_n,0,C_n}(\tau)=q^{C_n}f_n(q)$.

Using the Jacobi theta function and the Jacobi triple product formula $$\theta(\tau,z) \;=\; \sum_{-\infty}^{\infty} (-1)^{k} q^{(2k+1)^2/8} y^{(2k+1)/2} 
\;=\; q^{1/8}\,y^{1/2}\,\prod_{k=1}^\infty 
\bigl(1-q^k\bigr)\bigl(1-q^ky\bigr)\bigl(1-q^{k-1}y^{-1}\bigr)$$ (where $\Im(\tau)>0$, $z\in\mathbf C$, $q=\mathbf{e}(\tau)$, and $y=\mathbf{e}(z)$), together with the Dedekind eta-function $\eta(\tau)=q^{1/24}\prod_{n>0}(1-q^k)$, we can rewrite [\[AGid\]](#AGid){reference-type="eqref" reference="AGid"} as

$$\widetilde f_n(\tau) \;=\; q^{(r+1)^2/2n}\,\frac{\theta(n\tau,\,-(r+1)\tau)}{\eta(\tau)}\,,$$ which in conjunction with the standard transformation properties of $\theta$ and $\eta$ implies that $\widetilde f_n(\tau)$ is a modular function (with multiplier system) on the congruence subgroup $\Gamma_0(n)$ of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$. We need only the special case $\tau\mapsto\frac\tau{n\tau+1}$, where the transformation law is given by $$\label{WasLemma}
\widetilde f_n\Bigl(\frac\tau{n\tau+1}\Bigr) 
\;=\; \mathbf{e}\Bigl(\frac{n-3}{24}\Bigr)\,\widetilde f_n(\tau)\,.$$ We sketch the proof of this for completeness. The well-known modular transformation properties of $\theta$ and $\eta$ under the generators $T=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix})$ and $S=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix})$ of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$ are given by $$\begin{aligned}
&\theta(\tau+1,z)\,=\, \mathbf{e}(1/8) \, \theta(\tau,z)\,, \quad
\theta(-1/\tau,\,z/\tau) \,=\, \sqrt{\tau/i} \; 
\mathbf{e}(z^2/2\tau)\,\theta(\tau,z)\, \\
& \eta(\tau+1)\,=\,\mathbf{e}(1/24) \,\eta(\tau)\,, \qquad\; \eta(-1/\tau) 
\,=\, \sqrt{\tau/i} \;\eta(\tau)\,. 
\end{aligned}$$ Hence, using$\;\stackrel T\sim\;$and$\;\stackrel S\sim\;$to denote an equality up to an elementary factor (the product of a power of $\tau$ with the exponential of a linear combination of 1, $\tau$ and $z^2/\tau$) that can be deduced from the $T$- or $S$-transformation behavior of the function in question, we have $$\theta\Bigl(\frac{n\tau}{n\tau+1}, \frac{(r+1)\tau}{n\tau+1}\Bigr) 
\;\stackrel T\sim\;\theta\Bigl(\frac{-1}{n\tau+1}, \frac{(r+1)\tau}{n\tau+1}\Bigr) 
\;\stackrel S\sim\;\theta\left(n\tau+1, (r+1)\tau \right)  
\;\stackrel T\sim\;\theta\left(n\tau, (r+1)\tau \right) \,,$$ $$\eta\Bigl(\frac{\tau}{n\tau+1}\Bigr) 
\;\stackrel S\sim\;\eta\Bigl(-n-\frac{1}{\tau}\Bigr) 
\;\stackrel T\sim\;\eta\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\tau}\Bigr) \;\stackrel S\sim\;\eta(\tau) \,.$$ Inserting all omitted factors and dividing the first equations by the second, we obtain [\[WasLemma\]](#WasLemma){reference-type="eqref" reference="WasLemma"}.

Now applying [\[WasLemma\]](#WasLemma){reference-type="eqref" reference="WasLemma"} to $\tau=\frac{-1+i/\hbar}{n}$, with $\hbar=\frac h{2\pi}$, where $h$ positive and small, we find $$\begin{aligned}
f_{A_n,0}\left(\zeta_ne^{-h/n}\right)
= & 
\ \mathbf{e}\left(-C_n\frac{1+i\hbar}n\right)
\  \widetilde f_n\left(\frac{1+i\hbar}n\right) \\
= & 
\ \mathbf{e}\left(-C_n\frac{1+i\hbar}n+\frac{n-3}{24}\right) 
\widetilde f_n\left(\frac{-1+i/\hbar}n\right) 
\\
\label{pfnc}










= & 
\  \mathbf{e}\left(\frac n{24}-\frac18+\frac1{12n}-\frac1{4n^2}\right)
  e^{\mathrm L(X^{A_n})/nh} (1+ O(\hbar)) \,.

\end{aligned}$$ Taking the $4n$-th power of this and combining with Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} and its Corollary [\[UnitCor\]](#UnitCor){reference-type="ref" reference="UnitCor"}, we have an equality $$\label{compareexpansions}
\mathbf{e}\left(\frac{n^2}{6} - \frac{n}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{n} \right) (1 + \ldots)
= 
\mathbf{e}\left(\frac{r(n-1)(n-2)}{6} \right) \omega^{4n} 
(\Phi^{4n}_{\zeta}(h) + \ldots ).$$ Writing $\xi_{\zeta}$ for the Bloch element corresponding to $A_n$, we know from Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} that there is an inclusion $P_{\zeta}(\xi)^{1/n} D_{\zeta}(1)^{r/n} \Phi_{\zeta}(h) \in F_n[[h]]$, where in this case $F = \mathbf Q(\zeta+ \zeta^{-1})$ and $F_n = \mathbf Q(\zeta)$. Thus from equation ([\[compareexpansions\]](#compareexpansions){reference-type="ref" reference="compareexpansions"}) we deduce that $$R_{\zeta}(\xi)^4 D_{\zeta}(1)^{4r} \mathbf{e}\left(- \frac{r(n-1)(n-2)}{6} \right) 
 = \mathbf{e}\left(- \frac{n^2}{6} + \frac{n}{2} - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{n} \right)  \;\text{\rm mod}\;F^{\times n}_n.$$ Since $r = (n-3)/2$ and $D_{\zeta}(1) = \mathbf{e}(n/3)$ by Lemma [\[lem.Rz\]](#lem.Rz){reference-type="ref" reference="lem.Rz"}[\[partb.Rz\]](#partb.Rz){reference-type="ref" reference="partb.Rz"}, we deduce that $$R_{\zeta}(\xi)^4  = 
   \mathbf{e}\left(- \frac{n^2}{6} + \frac{n}{2} - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{n}  + \frac{r(n-1)(n-2)}{6}  - \frac{4rn}{3} \right)
   \;\text{\rm mod}\;F^{\times n}_n,$$ If $(3,n) = 1$, the only term which is non-trivial modulo $n$th powers is $\mathbf{e}(1/n)$. (Recall that $n$ is odd.) If $3|n$, then $3|r$, so the only terms which are non-trivial modulo $n$th powers are now $\mathbf{e}(1/n)$ and $\mathbf{e}(-1/3)$. Hence we deduce that $$\label{usedbelow}
   R_{\zeta}(\xi)^4 = \begin{cases} \mathbf{e}(1/n), & (n,3) = 1, \\
   \mathbf{e}(1/n)\mathbf{e}(-1/3), & 3|n. \end{cases}$$ From equation [\[eq:comparetorsion\]](#eq:comparetorsion){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:comparetorsion"}, we find that $$\xi_\zeta= 
\sum_{k=2}^{(n-1)/2}
{\left[ 1 - \left(\frac{\zeta^{1/2}-\zeta^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{k/2}-\zeta^{-k/2}}\right)^2 \right]}.$$ Using the $k \mapsto -k$ symmetry in Equation [\[eq.etaz\]](#eq.etaz){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq.etaz"}, we deduce that $$\eta_\zeta= 
{[\infty] + 2 [0] + 
2 
\sum_{k=2}^{(n-1)/2}
\left[ 1 - \left(\frac{\zeta-\zeta^{-1}}{\zeta^{k}-\zeta^{-k}}\right)^2 \right]
},$$ and hence (using the identities $2[\infty] = [0]$ and $[\infty] = 2[0]$) we deduce that $$\label{quicker}
2 \xi_{\zeta} =  \eta_{\zeta^{1/2}} {- 4 [0] =\eta_{\zeta^{1/2}}  - [0]} \,.$$

It remains to show that $\eta_{\zeta^{1/2}} = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \eta_{\zeta}$ because then, by Equations [\[usedbelow\]](#usedbelow){reference-type="eqref" reference="usedbelow"} and [\[quicker\]](#quicker){reference-type="eqref" reference="quicker"}, and the fact that $R_{\zeta}([0])^{-2} = D_{\zeta}(1)^{2} = \mathbf{e}(2/3)$ if $3|n$ and $1$ otherwise, we will have $$R_{\zeta}(\eta_{\zeta}) = R_{\zeta}(\zeta_{\zeta^{1/2}})^4
=
{ R_{\zeta}(\xi)^8  R_{\zeta}([0])^{-2} = \mathbf{e}(2/n)}

= \zeta^2.$$ which is the desired conclusion. In fact, more generally, we show that $\eta_{\zeta^k} = k^2 \eta_{\zeta}$ for $k$ prime to $n$. Suppose that $R_{\zeta}(\eta_{\zeta}) = \zeta^{m}$. Since this does not depend on the choice of $\zeta$, it must also be true that $R_{\zeta^k}(\eta_{\zeta^k}) = \zeta^{km}$. By Lemma [\[lemma:compatibility\]](#lemma:compatibility){reference-type="ref" reference="lemma:compatibility"}([\[lemma:compatibilitytwo\]](#lemma:compatibilitytwo){reference-type="ref" reference="lemma:compatibilitytwo"}), we have $$R_{\zeta^k}(\eta_{\zeta}) = R_{\zeta}(\eta_{\zeta})^{1/k} = \zeta^{m/k} \,,$$ and thus $$\zeta^{mk} = R_{\zeta^k}(k^2 \eta_{\zeta}) = R_{\zeta^k}(\eta_{\zeta^k}) \,.$$ By Lemma [\[invertible\]](#invertible){reference-type="ref" reference="invertible"}, the map $R_{\zeta^k}$ is injective on the torsion subgroup of $B(\mathbf Q(\zeta)^{+})$, and thus $\eta_{\zeta^k} = k^2 \eta_{\zeta}$, as desired. As a consistency check, note that the Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbf Q(\zeta)/\mathbf Q(\zeta)^{+})$ should act trivially on $B(\mathbf Q(\zeta)^{+})$, and we indeed see that the non-trivial element $\sigma: \zeta \mapsto \zeta^{-1}$ satisfies $$\sigma \eta_{\zeta} = \eta_{\zeta^{-1}} = (-1)^2 \eta_{\zeta} = \eta_{\zeta} \,.$$ ◻

## Application to Nahm's conjecture {#sub.nahmconj}

In this final subsection, we give an application of the asymptotic Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} and Theorem [\[thm.R\]](#thm.R){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.R"} to proving one direction of Nahm's conjecture about the modularity of Nahm sums. The notations and assumptions are as before, but for convenience we repeat them here.

Let $A\in M_r(\mathbf Q)$ be a positive definite symmetric matrix, $B\in\mathbf Q^r$, and $C\in\mathbf Q$.

We denote $X^A=(X_1,\dots,X_r)$ denote the unique solution in $(0,1)^r$ to the Nahm equation, by $F=F_A$ the real number field generated by the $X_i$ and by $\xi_A =\sum_i [X_i] \in B(F_A)$ the corresponding element of the Bloch group. Finally, when we say that $F_{A,B,C}$ is modular, we mean that the function $\widetilde f(\tau)=f_{A,B,C}(\mathbf{e}(\tau))$ is invariant with respect to a subgroup of finite index of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$.

::: theorem
[]{#thm.nahm label="thm.nahm"} If $f_{A,B,C}(\tau)$ is a modular function, then $\xi_A \in B(F_A)$ is a torsion element.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* On p. 56 of [@Zagier], it is shown that any Nahm sum has an expansion near $q=1$ of the form $$\label{eq.nc2}
f_{A,B,C}(e^{-\epsilon}) \;=\; e^{\mathrm L(\xi_A)/\epsilon}\,\bigl(K \,+\, \text O(\epsilon)) 
\qquad (\epsilon\to 0)\,,$$ where $K$ (given explicitly in eq. (29) of [@Zagier]) is a non-zero algebraic number some power of which belongs to $F=F_A$. Moreover, if $f_{A,B,C}$ is assumed to be modular, the error term $\,\text O(\epsilon)\,$ can be replaced by $\,\text O(e^{-c/\epsilon})\,$ with some $c>0$ ([@Zagier], eq. (28)). Notice that in this case the number $\lambda=\mathrm L(\xi_A)/4\pi^2$ must be rational, since the modularity of $\widetilde f(\tau)=f_{A,B,C}(\mathbf{e}(\tau))$ implies that the function $\widetilde f(-1/\tau)$ is invariant under some power of $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$.

Now assume that $\widetilde f$ is modular with respect to a finite index subgroup $\Gamma$ of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$. Then for $h\to0^+$, $\hbar=\frac h{2\pi}$, and any $\gamma=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in\Gamma$, taking $\epsilon=\frac{dh}{1-ic\hbar}$, we find $$f_{A,B,C}(e^{-\epsilon}) \,=\, \widetilde f\Bigl(\frac{i\epsilon}{2\pi}\Bigr) 
\,=\, \widetilde f\Bigl(\frac{ai\epsilon/2\pi+b}{ci\epsilon/2\pi+d}\Bigr)
\,=\,\widetilde f\Bigl(\frac{b+i\hbar}d\Bigr) \,=\, f_{A,B,C}(\zeta e^{-h/d}),$$ where $\zeta=\mathbf{e}(b/d)$, and now comparing the asymptotic formulas [\[eq.nc2\]](#eq.nc2){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq.nc2"} and [\[eq.nahmEM\]](#eq.nahmEM){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq.nahmEM"} (with $n=d$), we find $$\mu\,e^{\mathrm L(\xi_A)/hd}\,\Phi(h) \;=\; e^{L(\xi_A)/hd}\,
\bigl(K \mathbf{e}(\lambda c/d)\,+\,\text O(h)\bigr)$$ or $\Phi_\zeta(0)=\mu^{-1}K\mathbf{e}(\lambda c/d)$, with $\lambda\in\mathbf Q$ as above. This implies in particular that $\Phi_\zeta(0)\ne0$, and now, using that some bounded power of both $\mu$ and $K$ belong to $F_n$, we deduce that $\Phi(0)^r$ belongs to $F_n$ for some fixed integer $r>0$ independent of $n=d$. We can also assume that $d$ is prime to $M$ for any fixed integer $M$, since by intersecting $\Gamma$ with the full congruence subgroup $\Gamma(M)$, we may assume that $\Gamma$ is contained in $\Gamma(M)$. This shows that there are infinitely many integers $n$ and primitive $n$th roots of unity $\zeta$ for which $\Phi_\zeta(0)^r$ in Theorem [\[thm.nahmEM\]](#thm.nahmEM){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahmEM"} is a non-zero element of $F_n$. Now Corollary [\[UnitCor\]](#UnitCor){reference-type="ref" reference="UnitCor"} implies that the $r$th power of $R_\zeta(\xi_A)$ has trivial image in $F_n^\times/F_n^{\times n}$ for infinitely many $n$, and in view of the injectivity statement in Theorem [\[thm.R\]](#thm.R){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.R"} this proves that $\xi_A$ is a torsion element in the finitely generated group $B(F)$. ◻
:::

::: remark
The proof of the theorem would have been marginally shorter if we had assumed that $f_{A,B,C}$ was a modular function on a congruence subgroup, rather than just a subgroup of finite index of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$. We did not make this assumption since it was not needed, but should mention that $f_{A,B,C}$, if modular at all, is expected automatically to be modular for a congruence subgroup, because it has a Fourier expansion with integral coefficients in some rational power of $q$ and a standard conjecture says that the Fourier expansion of a modular function on a non-congruence subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf Z)$ always has unbounded denominators.
:::

::: remark
Conversely, we could have stated Theorem [\[thm.nahm\]](#thm.nahm){reference-type="ref" reference="thm.nahm"} in an apparently more general form by writing "modular form\" instead of "modular function.\" We did not do this since it is easy to see that if a Nahm sum is modular at all, it is actually a modular function, because if it were a modular form of non-zero rational weight $k$, there would be an extra factor $h^{-k}$ in the right-hand side of [\[eq.nc2\]](#eq.nc2){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq.nc2"}.
:::
