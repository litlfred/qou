[]{#sec:preliminaries label="sec:preliminaries"}

All definitions in this section can be found in [@Sagan].

In what follows we use $$\widehat{G}=\{\rho_1,\rho_2,\ldots,\rho_k\}$$ to denote a complete set of inequivalent (complex) irreducible matrix representations of a group $G$, with the convention that $\rho_1$ is the trivial representation. For any $\rho_i \in \widehat{G}$, the map $$\chi_i: g \mapsto \mathrm{Trace}(\rho_i(g)),\;\, g \in G$$ is the *character* of $\rho_i$, and the ratio $$\tilde{\chi}_i(g):=\frac{\chi_i(g)}{\chi_i(e)}$$ is known as the *normalized character* of $\rho_i$ on $g\in G$, where $\chi_i(e)$ equals the dimension $\dim\rho_i$ of $\rho_i$. Note that $\dim \rho_1 = 1$ for the trivial representation $\rho_1$.

The following proposition enables us to express the eigenvalues of any normal Cayley graph on $G$ in terms of the irreducible characters of $G$.

::: pr
[@PS; @Z][]{#prop:normal_eigenvalue label="prop:normal_eigenvalue"} Let $\{\chi_1,\chi_2,\ldots,\chi_k\}$ be a complete set of inequivalent irreducible characters of a group $G$. Then the eigenvalues of any normal Cayley graph $\mathrm{Cay}(G, S)$ on $G$ are given by $$\mu_j=\frac{1}{\chi_j(e)}\sum_{s\in S}\chi_j(s)=\sum_{s\in S} \tilde{\chi}_j(s),\quad j=1,2,\ldots,k.$$ Moreover, the multiplicity of $\mu_j$ is equal to $\sum_{1\leq i\leq k,\, \mu_i=\mu_j}\chi_i(e)^2.$
:::

We say that the strictly second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is *attained* or *achieved* by $\rho_i \in \widehat{G}$ if $$\lambda_{c+1}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))=\sum_{s\in S} \tilde{\chi}_i(s),$$ where $c=[G:\langle S\rangle ]$ is the index of the subgroup $\langle S\rangle$ in $G$. Note that the largest eigenvalue $|S|$ of $\mathrm{Cay}(G, S)$ has multiplicity $c$ as $\mathrm{Cay}(G, S)$ is the union of $c$ copies of the connected Cayley graph $\mathrm{Cay}(\langle S\rangle, S)$ with degree $|S|$. So $\lambda_{c+1}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))$ is indeed the strictly second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(G, S)$.

A *partition* of a positive integer $n$ is a sequence of positive integers $\gamma=(\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\ldots,\gamma_m)$ satisfying $\gamma_1\ge \gamma_2\ge\cdots\ge\gamma_m$ and $n=\gamma_1+\gamma_2+\cdots+\gamma_m$. We use $\gamma \vdash n$ to indicate that $\gamma$ is a partition of $n$ and let $c_i(\gamma)$ denote the number of terms in $\gamma$ which are equal to $i$. A *Young diagram* is a finite collection of boxes arranged in left-justified rows, with the row sizes weakly decreasing. The Young diagram associated to the partition $\gamma=(\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\ldots,\gamma_m)$ is the one that has $m$ rows and $\gamma_i$ boxes on the $i$-th row. Since there is a clear one-to-one correspondence between partitions and Young diagrams, we use the two terms interchangeably.

Every permutation $\sigma$ of $S_n$ has a decomposition into disjoint cycles. The *cycle type* of $\sigma$ is the partition of $n$ whose parts are the lengths of the cycles in its decomposition. It is widely known that two elements of $S_n$ are conjugates if and only if they have the same cycle type. This means that the conjugacy classes of $S_n$ are characterized by the cycle types and thus correspond to the partitions of $n$. Denote by $C(S_n,\gamma)$ the conjugacy class of $S_n$ that corresponds to the partition $\gamma\vdash n$. We use $\mathrm{sgn}(\gamma)$ to denote the sign of the permutations in $C(S_n,\gamma)$.

For each partition $\zeta\vdash n$, we use $\rho_\zeta$ to denote the Specht module of $S_n$ that corresponds to $\zeta$. It is well known that $\widehat{S_n}=\{\rho_\zeta~|~\zeta\vdash n\}$ is a complete list of inequivalent irreducible representations of $S_n$. The Specht modules $\rho_{(n)}$ and $\rho_{(1^n)}$ are called the *trivial* and the *sign representations* of $S_n$, respectively. The *standard representation* of $S_n$ just refers to $\rho_{(n-1,1)}$. Let $\chi_\zeta(\cdot)$ and $\tilde{\chi}_\zeta(\cdot)$ denote the character and normalized character of $\rho_\zeta$, respectively. We have $\chi_{(n)}(\sigma)=\tilde{\chi}_{(n)}(\sigma)= 1$ and $\chi_{(1^n)}(\sigma)=\tilde{\chi}_{(1^n)}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$ for any $\sigma\in S_n$. As $\chi_{\zeta}(\cdot)$ (respectively, $\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}(\cdot)$) is a class function on $S_n$, we use $\chi_\zeta(\gamma)$ (respectively, $\tilde{\chi}_\zeta(\gamma)$) to indicate the value of $\chi_\zeta(\cdot)$ (respectively, $\tilde{\chi}_\zeta(\cdot)$) on the conjugacy class $C(S_n,\gamma)$.

Then it has *hook* $$H_{a}=H_{i, j}=\left\{\left(i, j^{\prime}\right)\in \zeta: j^{\prime} \geq j\right\} \cup\left\{\left(i^{\prime}, j\right)\in \zeta: i^{\prime} \geq i\right\}$$ with corresponding *hook length* $$h_{a}=h_{i, j}=\left|H_{i, j}\right|.$$ To illustrate, if $\zeta=\left(4^{2}, 3^{3}, 1\right)$, then the dotted boxes in

The *conjugate* or *transpose* of a partition $\zeta=(\zeta_1,\zeta_2,\ldots,\zeta_m)\vdash n$ is defined as $\zeta'=(\zeta_1',~\zeta_2',~\ldots,\zeta_h') \vdash n$, where $\zeta_i'$ is the length of the $i$-th column of $\zeta$. In other words, the Young diagram of $\zeta'$ is just the transpose of that of $\zeta$. The relation between $\chi_\zeta(\cdot)$ and $\chi_{\zeta'}(\cdot)$ is reflected in the following lemma.

::: lem
([@JK 2.1.8]) []{#lem:conjugate_character label="lem:conjugate_character"} For any $\zeta \vdash n$ and $\gamma\vdash n$, we have $$\chi_{\zeta'}(\gamma)=\mathrm{sgn}(\gamma)\cdot\chi_\zeta(\gamma).$$
:::

In particular, we have $\mathrm{dim} \rho_{\zeta'}=\chi_{\zeta'}(\mathbf{1})=\chi_{\zeta}(\mathbf{1})=\mathrm{dim} \rho_\zeta$. That is, $\rho_{\zeta'}$ has the same dimension as $\rho_\zeta$. The following two lemmas give the normalized characters on $n$-cycles and $(n-1)$-cycles, respectively.

::: lem
[@Sagan Lemma 4.10.3] []{#lem:character_for_n_cycles label="lem:character_for_n_cycles"} Suppose $\zeta$ and $\gamma$ are two partitions of $n$. If $\gamma=(n)$, then $$\chi_\zeta(\gamma)=\begin{cases}
        (-1)^m,& \text{if}~\zeta=(n-m,1^m)~\text{with}~0\leq m\leq n-1;\\
        0,& \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}$$
:::

::: lem
[@SZ Lemma 4.3] []{#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles label="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"} Suppose $\zeta$ and $\gamma$ are two partitions of $n$. If $\gamma=(n-1,1)$, then $$\chi_\zeta(\gamma)=\begin{cases}
        1,& \text{if}~\zeta=(n);\\
        (-1)^{n-2},&\text{if}~\zeta=(1^n);\\
        (-1)^{m-1},& \text{if}~\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})~\text{with}~2\leq m\leq n-2;\\
        0,& \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}$$
:::

The *skew diagram* $\zeta / \mu$ is defined to be the set of boxes in $\zeta$ but not in $\mu$. Denote by $\mathrm{ht}(\zeta / \mu)$ the number of nonempty rows of $\zeta / \mu$ minus one. The main tool in this paper is as follows.

::: thm
[@Sagan Theorem 4.10.2][]{#thm:MN label="thm:MN"} Given positive integers $m$ and $n$, let $\rho \in S_{m+n}$ be an $m$-cycle and let $\pi$ be a permutation of the remaining $n$ elements of $[m+n]$. Then for any $\zeta\vdash m+n$, $$\chi_{\zeta}(\pi \rho)=\sum(-1)^{\operatorname{ht}(\zeta /\mu)} \chi_{\mu}(\pi),$$ where the sum is over all $\mu\vdash n$ such that $\mu \subset \zeta$ and $\zeta / \mu$ is a border strip.
:::

The special case of the Murnaghan-Nakayama Rule that $\rho$ is just a $1$-cycle, that is, a fixed point, is called the *Branching Rule*. To be specific, if $\zeta$ and $\gamma$ are parititions of $n+1$ with $c_1(\gamma)\ge 1$, letting $\gamma'$ be the partition of $n$ with all $c_i(\gamma')=c_i(\gamma)$ except for $c_1(\gamma')=c_1(\gamma)-1$, then $$\chi_\zeta(\gamma)=\sum_{\zeta-\Box} \chi_{\zeta-\Box}(\gamma'),$$ where the sum is taken over all partitions of $n$ which are obtained from $\zeta$ by removing one box.

Recall from [\[eq:CnI\]](#eq:CnI){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:CnI"} that $C(n,I)$ is the set of all cycles in $S_n$ with lengths in $I$, where $\emptyset\ne I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Since $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is normal, by Proposition [\[prop:normal_eigenvalue\]](#prop:normal_eigenvalue){reference-type="ref" reference="prop:normal_eigenvalue"} we can express its eigenvalues in terms of the irreducible characters of $S_n$. More specifically, if we denote by $\lambda_\zeta^I$ the eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ corresponding to $\zeta\vdash n$, then $$\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{\zeta}^I&=& \sum_{\sigma\in C(n,I)} \tilde{\chi}_\zeta(\sigma)\nonumber\\
    &=& \sum_{k\in I}|C(n,k)|\cdot \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))\nonumber\\
    &=&\sum_{k\in I}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})).\label{eq:eigenvalue}
\end{aligned}$$ Moreover, the multiplicity of $\lambda_\zeta^I$ is equal to $$\label{eq:multi}
\sum_{\substack{\mu\vdash n\\\lambda_{\mu}^I=\lambda_\zeta^I}}\chi_{\mu}(\mathbf{1})^2.$$ In particular, by ([\[eq:eigenvalue\]](#eq:eigenvalue){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:eigenvalue"}), Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"} and Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"}, for any $\emptyset \ne I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots, n\}$, the two eigenvalues of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ corresponding to the sign and standard representations are $$\label{eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)}
\lambda_{(1^n)}^I = \sum_{k\in I} |C(n,k)|\cdot \tilde{\chi}_{(1^n)}((k,1^{n-k})) = \sum_{k\in I}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!  \cdot (-1)^{k-1}$$ and $$\label{eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)}
\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I = \sum_{k\in I}  |C(n,k)|\cdot \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k})) 
= \sum_{k\in I}   \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot \frac{n-k-1}{n-1},$$ respectively.

# $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ with $I\cap \{n-1,n\}=\emptyset$ {#sec:proofs}

The next two lemmas compare the eigenvalues $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I$ and $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$ of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ for any $I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-1\}$.

::: lem
[]{#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd label="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd"} Suppose $n\ge 7$. If $I=\{2,3\}$, then $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I=\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$; if $\{2,3\}\ne I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-1\}$ and the largest number in $I$ is odd, then $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I >\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* If $I=\{2,3\}$, then a straightforward calculation using [\[eq: odd_eigenvalue\_(1\^n)\]](#eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)"} and [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"} yields $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I = \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$.

Now suppose $I\ne \{2,3\}$ and the largest number in $I$, say, $k_0$, is odd. If $k_0 = 3$, then $I = \{3\}$ and $\lambda_{(1^n)}^{\{3\}}>\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^{\{3\}}$ by [\[eq: odd_eigenvalue\_(1\^n)\]](#eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)"} and [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"}. It remains to consider the case where $5\leq k_0\leq n-1$. In this case, by [\[eq: odd_eigenvalue\_(1\^n)\]](#eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)"} and [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"}, we have $$\begin{aligned}
& & \lambda_{(1^n)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I \nonumber  \\ 
&=&\binom{n}{k_0}(k_0-1)!\frac{k_0}{n-1}+\sum_{k\in I\setminus\{k_0\}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left((-1)^{k-1}-\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}\right)\nonumber\\
&\ge& \binom{n}{k_0}(k_0-1)!\frac{k_0}{n-1}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq k_0-1\\ k\text{~is~even}}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\frac{2n-k-2}{n-1}\nonumber\\
&=& n(n-2)!\left(\frac{1}{(n-k_0)!}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq k_0-1\\ k\text{~is~even}}} \frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right) \nonumber\\
&=& n(n-2)!\left(\frac{1}{(n-5)!}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq 5\\ k\text{~is~even}}} \frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right) + \nonumber\\
     & & n(n-2)!\left(\sum\limits_{\substack{6\leq k\leq k_0-1\\ k\text{~is~even}}}\left(\frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k+1)!}-\frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right)\right). \label{ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd}
\end{aligned}$$ Since $n\ge 7$, we see that the first part of the lower bound [\[ineq:diff_1\^n_n-1,1_odd\]](#ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd){reference-type="eqref" reference="ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd"} is positive. Note that when $k_0=5$ the second part of this lower bound valishes. Note also that, for $6\leq k\leq n-2$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
    & \frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k+1)!}- \frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\nonumber\\
    &>\frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}- \frac{k-2}{k(n-k)!} -\frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\nonumber \\
    &= \frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}- \frac{2n-4}{k(n-k)!} \nonumber \\
    &\ge 0. \nonumber
  
\end{aligned}$$ Therefore, the second part of the lower bound [\[ineq:diff_1\^n_n-1,1_odd\]](#ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd){reference-type="eqref" reference="ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd"} is also positive as required to complete the proof. ◻
:::

::: lem
[]{#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even label="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even"} Suppose $n\ge 7$. If $\emptyset \ne I\subseteq\{2,3,\ldots,n-1\}$ and the largest number in $I$ is even, then $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\lambda_{(1^n)}^I$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Denote by $k_0$ the largest number in $I$. By our assumption, $k_0$ is even. If $k_0 = 2$, then $I = \{2\}$ and $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^{\{2\}}>\lambda_{(1^n)}^{\{2\}}$ by [\[eq: odd_eigenvalue\_(1\^n)\]](#eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)"} and [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"}.

Now suppose $4\leq k_0\leq n-1$. By ([\[eq: odd_eigenvalue\_(1\^n)\]](#eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)){reference-type="ref" reference="eq: odd_eigenvalue_(1^n)"}) and ([\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"}), we have $$\begin{aligned}
& &\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I-\lambda_{(1^n)}^I \nonumber \\
&=& \binom{n}{k_0}(k_0-1)!\frac{2n-k_0-2}{n-1}+\sum\limits_{k\in I\setminus\{k_0\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left(\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}+(-1)^k\right)\nonumber\\
    &\ge& \binom{n}{k_0}(k_0-1)!\frac{2n-k_0-2}{n-1}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq k_0-1\\k~\text{is~odd}}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\frac{k}{n-1} \nonumber\\
    &=& n(n-2)!\left(\frac{2n-k_0-2}{k_0(n-k_0)!}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq k_0-1\\k~\text{is~odd}}}\frac{1}{(n-k)!} \right) \nonumber \\
    &=& n(n-2)!\left(\frac{2n-6}{4(n-4)!}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq 4\\k~\text{is~odd}}}\frac{1}{(n-k)!} \right) + \nonumber\\
    & &n(n-2)!\left( \sum\limits_{\substack{5\leq k\leq k_0-1 \\k~\text{is~odd} }} \left(\frac{2n-k-3}{(k+1)(n-k-1)!}-\frac{2n-k-1}{(k-1)(n-k+1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k)!}\right)\right).
\label{eq:9}
\end{aligned}$$ Since $n\ge 7$, the first part of the lower bound ([\[eq:9\]](#eq:9){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:9"}) is positive. Note that the second part of ([\[eq:9\]](#eq:9){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:9"}) vanishes when $k_0 = 4$. Note also that, for $5\leq k\leq n-2$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
& \frac{2n-k-3}{(k+1)(n-k-1)!}-\frac{2n-k-1}{(k-1)(n-k+1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k)!}\nonumber\\
    &> \frac{2n-k-3}{(k+1)(n-k-1)!}-\frac{2}{(n-k)!}\nonumber\\
    &= \frac{(2n-k-3)(n-k)-2(k+1)}{(k+1)(n-k)!}\nonumber\\
    &\ge 0. \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ Thus, the second part of the lower bound ([\[eq:9\]](#eq:9){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:9"}) is also positive. This completes the proof. ◻
:::

The main result in this section is as follows.

::: thm
[]{#cx:I_(n-2) label="cx:I_(n-2)"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $\emptyset\ne I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-2\}$. Then the following statements hold:

-   if $I$ only contains odd numbers, then $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ has two connected components and its strictly second largest eigenvalue is attained by $(n-1,1)$ and $(2,1^{n-2})$ with multiplicity $2(n-1)^2$;

-   $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,\{2,3\}))$ is connected and its second largest eigenvalue is attained by $(n-1,1)$ and $(1^n)$ with multiplicity $(n-1)^2+1$;

-   if $I$ contains both even and odd numbers with the largest one odd and at least $5$, then $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is connected and its second largest eigenvalue is attained uniquely by $(1^{n})$ with multiplicity $1$;

-   if the largest number in $I$ is even, then $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is connected and its second largest eigenvalue is attained uniquely by $(n-1,1)$ with multiplicity $(n-1)^2$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* By [\[eq:eigenvalue\]](#eq:eigenvalue){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:eigenvalue"} and Lemma [\[lem:cycle_n-2\]](#lem:cycle_n-2){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:cycle_n-2"}, for any $\zeta\vdash n$ other than $(n),(1^n),(n-1,1)$ and $(2,1^{n-2})$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_\zeta^I &=& \sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber \\
                    &<& \sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber \\
                    &=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I. \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ On the other hand, by [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"} we see that $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$ is strictly smaller than $$\lambda_{(n)}^I=\sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!=|C(n,I)|,$$ which is the degree of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$. Therefore, the strictly second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by partitions among $(1^n),(n-1,1)$ and $(2,1^{n-2})$. According to Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"}, we have $$\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I&=&\sum_{k\in I}  \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot \tilde{\chi}_{(2,1^{n-2})}((k,1^{n-k}))\nonumber\\
                       &=& \sum_{k\in I}  \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot (-1)^{k-1} \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))\nonumber\\
                       &=&\sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot (-1)^{k-1}\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}. \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ Comparing with [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"}, we obtain that $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I\ge \lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I$ and the strict inequality $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I> \lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I$ holds if $I$ contains at least one even number.

In the case when $I$ contains only odd numbers, $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ has two connected components each isomorphic to $\mathrm{Cay}(A_n, C(n,I))$ and $\lambda_{(n)}^I=\lambda_{(1^n)}^I>\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I=\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I$. So its strictly second largest eigenvalue is only achieved by $(n-1,1)$ and $(2,1^{n-2})$. Note that both $\rho_{(n-1,1)}$ and $\rho_{(2,1^{n-2})}$ have dimension $n-1$ according to Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"} and Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"}. We further deduce from [\[eq:multi\]](#eq:multi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:multi"} that the multiplicity of the strictly second largest eigenvalue is $2(n-1)^2$. This proves statement (a). In the other three cases there is at least one even number in $I$, and hence $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is connected and $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I$. So the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(1^n)$ or $(n-1,1)$. Combining this with Lemmas [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_odd\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd"} and [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_even\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even"}, we obtain (b), (c) and (d), where the multiplicities are calculated directly with the help of equation [\[eq:multi\]](#eq:multi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:multi"}. ◻
:::

# $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ with $I\cap\{n-1,n\}=\{n-1\}$ {#sec:n-1}

::: lem
[]{#cor: gap_between_(1^n)_and_(n-1,1) label="cor: gap_between_(1^n)_and_(n-1,1)"} Suppose $n\ge 8$ is even and $\{n-1\}\subseteq I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-1\}$. Then $$\lambda_{(1^n)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\frac{n(n-5)}{3}(n-3)!.$$

*Proof.* Since $n \ge 8$ is even, the largest number $k_0 = n-1$ in $I$ is odd and hence ([\[ineq:diff_1\^n_n-1,1_odd\]](#ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd"}) can be applied to the current situation. By this inequality, we obtain $$\begin{aligned}

   & & n(n-2)!\left(\sum\limits_{\substack{6\leq k\leq n-2\\ k\text{~is~even}}}\frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k+1)!}-\frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right) \\
   &>& n(n-2)!\left(\sum\limits_{\substack{k=n-2\\ k\text{~is~even}}}\frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k+1)!}-\frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right) \\
   &=& \frac{n(n-5)}{3}(n-3)!
  
\end{aligned}$$ as desired. ◻

::: lem
[]{#lem:(n-m,1^m) label="lem:(n-m,1^m)"} Suppose $n\ge 7$. The following hold:

-   if $k=n-1,n-2$ or $n-3$ and $\zeta=(n-m,1^{m})$ with $3\leq m\leq n-4$, then $$\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))=0;$$

-   if $2\leq k\leq n-4$ and $\zeta=(n-m,1^{m})$ with $4\leq m\leq n-5$, then $$\label{eq:3chi}
    \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))< \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,1^3)}((k,1^{n-k}))< \tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k})).$$
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* (a) If $k=n-1,n-2$ or $n-3$, then $\zeta=(n-m,1^{m})$ with $3\leq m\leq n-4$ does not contain any $k$-border strip. Hence $\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))=0$ by the Murnaghan-Nakayama Rule.

\(b\) Suppose $2\leq k\leq n-4$ and $\zeta=(n-m,1^{m})$, where $n \ge 7$ and $4\leq m\leq n-5$. According to Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"}, we have $$\begin{aligned}
\label{eq:n-3,1,1,1}
    \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,1^3)}((k,1^{n-k}))=\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)(n-k-3)-6(n-k-1)c_2+6c_3}{(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}
\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}
    \tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))=\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)-2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)},
\end{aligned}$$ where $c_i$ is the number of terms in $(k,1^{n-k})$ that are equal to $i$. Using these expressions, one can easily verify the second inequality in [\[eq:3chi\]](#eq:3chi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:3chi"}.

It remains to prove the first inequality in [\[eq:3chi\]](#eq:3chi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:3chi"} for $n \ge 9$. (Note that this inequality vanishes when $n = 7$ or $8$ as $4 \le m \le n-5$ in $\zeta$.) We achieve this by induction on $n\ge 9$. Note from ([\[eq:n-3,1,1,1\]](#eq:n-3,1,1,1){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:n-3,1,1,1"}) and Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"} that $$\begin{aligned}
\label{ine:m=3_m=2}
    0< \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,1^3)}((k,1^{n-k}))=|\tilde{\chi}_{(4,1^{n-4})}((k,1^{n-k}))|.
\end{aligned}$$ It is straightforward to verify that the first inequality in [\[eq:3chi\]](#eq:3chi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:3chi"} holds when $n=9$, $2\leq k\leq 5$ and $\zeta=(5,1^{4})$. Assume that $n-1\ge 9$ and for every $\zeta=(n-1-m,1^m)$ with $4\leq m\leq n-6$ and any $2\leq k\leq n-5$ the following holds: $$\begin{aligned}
\label{ine:induction_assumption}
    \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-1-k}))< \tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,1^3)}((k,1^{n-1-k}))=|\tilde{\chi}_{(4,1^{n-5})}((k,1^{n-1-k}))|.
  
\end{aligned}$$ Now let us consider $\zeta=(n-m,1^m)\vdash n$ with $4\leq m\leq n-5$ and $2\leq k\leq n-4$. If $k=n-4$, then for any $\zeta=(n-m,1^{m})$ with $4\leq m\leq n-5$, the Young diagram of $\zeta$ contains no $(n-4)$-border strip. Thus we know from the Murnaghan-Nakayama Rule and inequality [\[ine:m=3_m=2\]](#ine:m=3_m=2){reference-type="eqref" reference="ine:m=3_m=2"} that for $4\leq m\leq n-5,$ $$0=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,1^m)}((n-4,1^{4}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,1^3)}((n-4,1^{4})).$$ If $2\leq k\leq n-5$, then for any $\zeta=(n-m,1^m)$ with $4\leq m\leq n-5$, we apply the Branching Rule to the following normalized character and obtain $$\begin{aligned}
    & &\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber\\
    
                                   &=& \frac{\sum\limits_{\zeta-\Box} \chi_{\zeta-\Box}((k,1^{n-1-k}))}{\sum\limits_{\zeta-\Box} \chi_{\zeta-\Box}((1^{n-1}))} \nonumber\\ 
                                   &=& \frac{ \chi_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((k,1^{n-1-k})) +  \chi_{(n-m-1,1^{m})}((k,1^{n-1-k})) }{\chi_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((1^{n-1})) +  \chi_{(n-m-1,1^{m})}((1^{n-1}))} \nonumber\\ 
                                   &\leq& \max\left\{ \frac{ \chi_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((k,1^{n-1-k}))}{\chi_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((1^{n-1})) } , \frac{\chi_{(n-m-1,1^{m})}((k,1^{n-1-k})) }{\chi_{(n-m-1,1^{m})}((1^{n-1}))}\right\} \nonumber\\ 
                                   
                                   &\leq &  \tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,1^3)}((k,1^{n-1-k})) \label{ine:by_induction_assumption} \\ 
                                   &=& \frac{(n-1-k-1)(n-1-k-2)(n-1-k-3)-6(n-1-k-1)c_2+6c_3}{(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)}  \nonumber\\
                                   &<& \frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)(n-k-3)-6(n-k-1)c_2+6c_3}{(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)} \nonumber\\ 
                                   &=& \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,1^3)}((k,1^{n-k})), \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ where ([\[ine:by_induction_assumption\]](#ine:by_induction_assumption){reference-type="ref" reference="ine:by_induction_assumption"}) follows from the induction hypothesis ([\[ine:induction_assumption\]](#ine:induction_assumption){reference-type="ref" reference="ine:induction_assumption"}). ◻

::: lem
[]{#re:n-m,1^m label="re:n-m,1^m"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $n\in I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Then the following hold:

-   $\max_{1\leq m\leq n-1}{\lambda_{(n-m,1^m)}^I}$ can only be attained by $m=1,2,n-3,n-2$ or $n-1$;

-   $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I\ge\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$ and the equality holds if and only if $I=\{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$ or $I=\{2,3,\ldots,n-1,n\}$;

-   if $n$ is even and $I=\{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$ or $\{2,3,\ldots,n-1,n\}$, then $\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I>\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I=\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$;

-   $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I\ge \lambda_{(3,1^{n-3})}^I$ and the equality holds if and only if $I$ contains only odd numbers other than $n-1$ and $n-2$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* (a) Note from Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"} that $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((n-3,1^{3}))>0$ and $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((n-2,1^{2}))=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((n-1,1))=0$. Combining the previous lemma with Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n_cycles"}, we obtain that for $2\leq k\leq n-3$ or $k=n$, $$\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,1^{m})}((k,1^{n-k}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k})), \quad 3\leq m\leq n-4$$ and for $k=n-1$ or $n-2$, $$\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,1^{m})}((k,1^{n-k}))=0, \quad 2\leq m\leq n-3.$$ Thus equation ([\[eq:eigenvalue\]](#eq:eigenvalue){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:eigenvalue"}) implies that whenever $n\in I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$ we have $\lambda_{(n-m,1^m)}^I<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ for every $3\leq m\leq n-4$, and so the maximum of $\lambda_{(n-m,1^m)}^I$ for $1\leq m\leq n-1$ can only be attained by $m=1,2,n-3,n-2$ or $n-1$.

\(b\) We have $$\begin{aligned}
    & & \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I \nonumber \\
    &=& \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left(\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)-2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)}-\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}\right) + 2(n-3)!+(n-2)! \nonumber \\
    &\ge& -\sum_{2\leq k\leq n-2} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \frac{k(n-k-1)+2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)}+2(n-3)!+(n-2)!\nonumber \\
    &=& n(n-3)!-\sum_{2\leq k\leq n-2} \frac{n(n-3)!}{(n-k)(n-k-2)!}-\frac{n}{n-2} \nonumber \\
    &=& n(n-3)!\left(1-\sum_{2\leq k\leq n-2} \frac{1}{(n-k)(n-k-2)!}\right) -\frac{n}{n-2} \nonumber\\
    &=& n(n-3)!\cdot \frac{1}{(n-2)!} -\frac{n}{n-2} \nonumber \\
    &=& 0. \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ Thus $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I\ge\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$ and the equality holds if and only if $I=\{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$ or $I=\{2,3,\ldots,n-1,n\}$.

\(c\) We have $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I=\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ from (b). We claim that the partition $(2,1^{n-2})$ yields a larger eigenvalue than $(n-2,1^2)$ and $(n-1,1)$ in this case. In fact, $$\begin{aligned}
    & &\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I \nonumber\\
    &=& \sum_{k=2}^{n-2} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!(-1)^{k-1}\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}+(n-2)! \nonumber \\
    & & -\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n-2} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}-(n-2)!\right) \nonumber \\
    &=&-2 \sum_{\substack{2\leq k \leq n-2\\ k~\mathrm{is~even}}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\frac{n-k-1}{n-1} +2(n-2)!\nonumber \\
    &=& 2(n-2)!\left(1-\sum_{\substack{2\leq k \leq n-2\\ k~\mathrm{is~even}}} \frac{n}{k(n-k)(n-k-2)!}\right) \nonumber \\
    &>& 0. \nonumber 
\end{aligned}$$ The last inequality above is deduced from the fact that $n$ is at least $8$ and $$\begin{aligned}
    \sum_{\substack{2\leq k \leq n-2\\ k~\mathrm{is~even}}} \frac{n}{k(n-k)(n-k-2)!}&=& \frac{n}{2(n-2)}+\frac{n}{8(n-4)}+\sum_{\substack{2\leq k \leq n-6\\ k~\mathrm{is~even}}} \frac{n}{k(n-k)(n-k-2)!} \nonumber \\
    &<& \frac{n}{2(n-2)}+\frac{n}{8(n-4)}+\sum_{\substack{2\leq k \leq n-6\\ k~\mathrm{is~even}}} \frac{1}{(n-k-2)!} \nonumber \\
    &<& \frac{8}{12}+\frac{8}{32}+\frac{2}{4!} \nonumber \\
    &=& 1. \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$

\(d\) Note from Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"} that $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))\ge 0$ for any $2\leq k\leq n$ and that $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))= 0$ if and only if $k=n-1$ or $n-2$. Thus we have $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))\ge \tilde{\chi}_{(3,1^{n-3})}((k,1^{n-k}))=(-1)^{k-1}\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))$ for any $2\leq k\leq n$. This implies that $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I\ge \lambda_{(3,1^{n-3})}^I$ and the equality holds if and only if $I$ contains only odd numbers other than $n-1$ and $n-2$. ◻

[]{#lem:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_1 label="lem:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_1"} Suppose $n\ge 7$. If $2\leq k\leq n-5$ and $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $4\leq m\leq n-4$, then $$\label{ineq: n-m,2,1}

::: proof
*Proof.* By Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"}, we have $$\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))=\frac{(n-k)(n-k-2)(n-k-4)-3c_3}{n(n-2)(n-4)},$$ $$\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((k,1^{n-k}))=\frac{(n-k)(n-k-3)+2c_2}{n(n-3)},$$ where $c_i$ is the number of terms in $(k,1^{n-k})$ which are equal to $i$. Using these expressions and Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"}, one can easily verify that $$|\tilde{\chi}_{(3,2,1^{n-5})}((k,1^{n-k}))|=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((k,1^{n-k}))$$ for $n\ge 7$ and $2\leq k\leq n-5$. Note that the first inequality in [\[ineq: n-m,2,1\]](#ineq: n-m,2,1){reference-type="eqref" reference="ineq: n-m,2,1"} vanishes when $n = 7$ as we require $4 \le m \le n-4$ in $\zeta$. One can check that this holds for $n=8$ and $9$. Suppose the above inequality holds for some $n-1\ge 9$, that is, for $2\leq k\leq n-6$, $$\begin{aligned}
     \label{eq:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_assumption}
        \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1-m,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-1-k}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,2,1)}((k,1^{n-1-k}))=|\tilde{\chi}_{(3,2,1^{n-6})}((k,1^{n-1-k}))|
     
\end{aligned}$$ holds for $4\leq m\leq n-5$.

Now we consider $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-k}))$ with $4\leq m\leq n-4$ and $2\leq k\leq n-5$. Note that $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))>0$ for $n\ge 8$ and $2\leq k\leq n-5$. First, let $k=n-5$. By the Murnaghan-Nakayama Rule we see that if $m\ne 5, n-5$, then $\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((n-5,1^{5}))=0<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-5,1^{5}))$ as there is no $(n-5)$-border strip in $\zeta$. If $m=5$ or $n-5$, then by a simple computation we still have $$0<|\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((n-5,1^{5}))|<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-5,1^{5})).$$ Thus, for $4\leq m\leq n-4$, $$\begin{aligned}
        \tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((n-5,1^{5}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-5,1^{5})).
     
\end{aligned}$$ Next, let $2\leq k\leq n-6$. For every $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $4\leq m\leq n-4$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
        & &\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((k,1^{n-k}))\nonumber \\ \nonumber
        
        &=&\frac{\sum\limits_{\zeta-\Box} \chi_{\zeta-\Box}((k,1^{n-1-k}))}{\sum\limits_{\zeta-\Box} \chi_{\zeta-\Box}((1^{n-1}))} \\ \nonumber
        &=& \frac{ \chi_{(n-m-1,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-1-k})) +  \chi_{(n-m,2,1^{m-3})}((k,1^{n-1-k})) +  \chi_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((k,1^{n-1-k}))}{\chi_{(n-m-1,2,1^{m-2})}((1^{n-1})) +  \chi_{(n-m,2,1^{m-3})}((1^{n-1})) + \chi_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((1^{n-1}))} \\ \nonumber
        &\leq & \max\left\{ \tilde{\chi}_{(n-m-1,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-1-k})),  \tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-3})}((k,1^{n-1-k})),  \tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,1^{m-1})}((k,1^{n-1-k})\right\} \\ \label{ineq:induction_assumption}
        &\leq & \max\left\{ \tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,2,1)}((k,1^{n-1-k})), \tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,1^3)}((k,1^{n-1-k}) \right\} \\ \nonumber
        &=& \max\Bigg\{ \frac{(n-1-k)(n-1-k-2)(n-1-k-4)-3c_3}{(n-1)(n-3)(n-5)}, \\ \nonumber
        & & \frac{(n-k-2)(n-k-3)(n-k-4)-6(n-k-2)c_2+6c_3}{(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)}\Bigg\} \\ \nonumber
        &<& \frac{(n-k)(n-k-2)(n-k-4)-3c_3}{n(n-2)(n-4)} \\ \nonumber
        &=& \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((k,1^{n-k})),
    
\end{aligned}$$ where ([\[ineq:induction_assumption\]](#ineq:induction_assumption){reference-type="ref" reference="ineq:induction_assumption"}) is deduced from the induction hypothesis ([\[eq:(n-m,2,1\^m-2)\_assumption\]](#eq:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_assumption){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_assumption"}) as well as Lemma [\[lem:(n-m,1\^m)\]](#lem:(n-m,1^m)){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:(n-m,1^m)"}. ◻

::: lem
[]{#lem:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_2 label="lem:(n-m,2,1^m-2)_2"} Suppose $n\ge 7$. Then for any $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $4\leq m\leq n-4$ the following hold:

*Proof.* (a) We can obtain the following facts by using Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n-1_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"} and the Hook-Length Formula directly: If $n=7$, then $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-1,1))<|\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((n-1,1))|$; if $n=8$ or $9$, then $|\tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,2,1^2)}((n-1,1))|< \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-1,1))< |\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((n-1,1))|$; if $n\ge 10$, then for any $5\leq m\leq n-5$, $$\begin{aligned}

        &<& \tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-1,1)) \nonumber \\
        &<& |\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((n-1,1))|. \nonumber

\(b\) One can check that the inequality holds for $n = 8, 9$. Now suppose $n\ge 10$. If $k=n-4$, then for $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $3\leq m\leq n-3$, we have $\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((n-4,1^4))\ne 0$ if and only if $m=4$ or $n-4$. Using Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"}, we obtain that $$(-1)^{n-5}\tilde{\chi}_{(4,2,1^{n-6}))}((n-4,1^{4}))=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-4,2,1^2))}((n-4,1^{4}))< 0=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1))}((n-4,1^{4})).$$ If $k=n-3$, then $\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-3,1^3))=0$ for $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $4\leq m\leq n-4$ and $$(-1)^{n-4}\cdot\tilde{\chi}_{(3,2,1^{n-5})}((n-3,1^3))=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-3,2,1)}((n-3,1^3))=\frac{-3}{n(n-2)(n-4)}.$$ Combining these facts with the first part of this lemma, we have for every $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $5\leq m\leq n-5$ and every $I$ with $n-1\in I\subseteq \{n-4,n-3,n-1\}$, $$\sum_{k\in I } \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((k,1^{n-k}))\leq\sum_{k\in I } \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(n-5,2,1^3)}((k,1^{n-k})).$$ To complete the proof, it remains to establish the required inequality for $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $m=4,5$ or $n-4$ and $n-1\in I\subseteq \{n-4,n-3,n-1\}$. This can be done by straightforward computations with the help of Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n-1_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"} and Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"}. ◻

Now we are ready to prove our main result in this section.

::: thm
[]{#thm:n-1 label="thm:n-1"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $\{n-1\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-1\}$. Then the following statements hold:

-   if $n$ is even and $I$ contains at least one even number, then the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained uniquely by $(1^n)$, and moreover the multiplicity of this eigenvalue is $1$;

-   if $n$ is even and $I$ contains only odd numbers, then the strictly second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by either $(n-1,1)$ and $(2,1^{n-2})$ or $(n-3,2,1)$ and $(3,2,1^{n-5})$;

-   if $n$ is odd, then the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained by $(n-1,1)$, $(n-3,2,1)$, $(2^2,1^{n-4}))$ or $(2,1^{n-2})$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* (a) For any $\zeta\vdash n$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
\lambda_\zeta^I&=& \sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) \\
&=& \sum_{\substack{k\in I\setminus\{n-1\}}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))  + n(n-2)!\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-1,1)).
\end{aligned}$$ According to Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n-1_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"}, the second term above vanishes unless $\zeta = (n),(1^n)$ or $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$. This together with Lemma [\[lem:cycle_n-2\]](#lem:cycle_n-2){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:cycle_n-2"} implies that, for $\zeta\ne (n),(1^n),(n-1,1),(2,1^{n-2})$, $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
\lambda_\zeta^I&=&\sum_{\substack{k\in I\setminus\{n-1\}}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber\\
&<& \sum_{\substack{k\in I\setminus\{n-1\}}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber\\
&=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I. \label{ineq:thm_n-1-a}
\end{aligned}$$ Since $n$ is even and the largest number $n-1$ in $I$ is odd, Lemma [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_odd\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd"} implies that $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I>\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$. Moreover, as $I$ contains at least one even number smaller than $n-1$, we also have $$\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I &=& \sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot \frac{n-k-1}{n-1} \nonumber\\
                        &>& \sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\cdot (-1)^{k-1}\frac{n-k-1}{n-1} \nonumber\\
                        &=& \lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I. \label{eq:25}
\end{aligned}$$ So the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(1^n)$ or $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$.

In the following we assume $\zeta=(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$, and we aim to show that $\zeta$ does not give the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$. In fact, by Lemma [\[lem:cycle_n-2\]](#lem:cycle_n-2){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:cycle_n-2"}, $$\begin{aligned}
\lambda_\zeta^I &=& \sum_{\substack{k\in I\setminus\{n-1\}}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))  + n(n-2)!\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-1,1))\nonumber \\
&<& \sum_{\substack{k\in I\setminus\{n-1\}}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))  + n(n-2)!\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-1,1)) \nonumber \\
&=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I + n(n-2)!\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-1,1)). \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ On the other hand, by Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n-1_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"}, $$\begin{aligned}
n(n-2)!\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-1,1))&=& n(n-2)! \frac{\chi_\zeta((n-1,1))}{\chi_\zeta((1^n))} \\
& = & n(n-2)! \frac{(-1)^{m-1}(n-1)(n-m)(n-m-2)!m(m-2)!}{n!} \\
& \leq & n(n-2)! \frac{3(n-1)(n-3)(n-5)!}{n!} \\
& = & 3(n-3)(n-5)!,  
\end{aligned}$$ where the second last step follows from the fact that $n$ is even and thus the maximum $$\max_{2\leq m\leq n-2} \frac{(-1)^{m-1}(n-1)(n-m)(n-m-2)!m(m-2)!}{n!}$$ is achieved by $m \in \{3, n-3\}$. Combining these with Lemma [\[cor: gap_between\_(1\^n)\_and\_(n-1,1)\]](#cor: gap_between_(1^n)_and_(n-1,1)){reference-type="ref" reference="cor: gap_between_(1^n)_and_(n-1,1)"}, we obtain $$\begin{aligned}
\lambda_{(1^n)}^I & > & \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I + \frac{n(n-5)}{3}(n-3)! \\
& > & \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I + 3(n-3)(n-5)! \\
& \ge & \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I + n(n-2)!\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n-1,1)) \\
& > & \lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I
\end{aligned}$$ for $2\leq m\leq n-2$. Therefore, the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained uniquely by $(1^n)$. Moreover, the multiplicity of this eigenvalue is equal to the square of the dimension of $\rho_{(1^n)}$, namely $1$.

\(b\) Since $I$ contains only odd numbers, by Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"} we have $\lambda_\zeta^I=\lambda_{\zeta'}^I$ for any $\zeta \vdash n$. According to [\[ineq:thm_n-1-a\]](#ineq:thm_n-1-a){reference-type="eqref" reference="ineq:thm_n-1-a"}, the strictly second largest eigenvalue can only be attained by $(n-1,1),(2,1^{n-2})$ or $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$. By a direct computation using Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"}, one can verify that for $2\leq k\leq n-1$ and $m=2$ or $n-2$, we have $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-k}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))$, which implies $\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I<\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I=\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I$ when $m = 2$ or $n-2$. On the other hand, by Remark [\[re:(n-m,2,1\^m-2)\]](#re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)){reference-type="ref" reference="re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)"}, we have $\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I<\lambda_{(n-3,2,1)}^I=\lambda_{(3,2,1^{n-5})}^I$ for $4\leq m\leq n-4$. The result follows from these inequalities.

\(c\) Similarly to the proof of (a) above, for $\zeta\ne (n),(1^n),(n-1,1),(2,1^{n-2})$, $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$, we have $\lambda_\zeta^I < \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$. Lemma [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_even\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even"} implies $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\lambda_{(1^n)}^I$ when $n\ge 7$ is odd and $n-1$ is the largest number in $I$. Thus the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(n-1,1),(2,1^{n-2})$ or $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$.

According to Remark [\[re:(n-m,2,1\^m-2)\]](#re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)){reference-type="ref" reference="re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)"}, the maximum of $\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I$ for $2\leq m\leq n-2$ is attained by $m=2,3,n-3$ or $n-2$. Furthermore, when $m=2, n-3$, Lemmas [\[lem:character_for_n-1_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"} and [\[lem:cycle_n-2\]](#lem:cycle_n-2){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:cycle_n-2"} imply $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((n-1,1))<0=\tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((n-1,1))$ and $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-k}))<\tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k}))$ for $2\leq k\leq n-2$, respectively. Therefore, for $m=2, n-3$, we have $\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I<\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$. Thus the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(n-1,1),(2,1^{n-2})$ or $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $m=3,n-2$, as desired. ◻
:::

This shows that both cases in part (b) of Theorem [\[thm:n-1\]](#thm:n-1){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:n-1"} can occur. However, we do not know whether the four partitions in part (b) of Theorem [\[thm:n-1\]](#thm:n-1){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:n-1"} can achieve the strictly second largest eigenvalue simultaneously.

In [\[eq:25\]](#eq:25){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:25"} we saw that $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I > \lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I$ whenever $I\subseteq \{2,3\ldots,n-1\}$ contains at least one even number smaller than $n-1$. In general, by Lemma [\[lem:conjugate_character\]](#lem:conjugate_character){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:conjugate_character"} and [\[eq:odd_eigenvalue\_(n-1,1)\]](#eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:odd_eigenvalue_(n-1,1)"}, for any $I\subseteq \{2,3\ldots,n-1\}$ we have $$\begin{aligned}
    \lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I&=\sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(2,1^{n-2})}((k,1^{n-k})) \notag \\
                           &=\sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! (-1)^{k-1}\tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k})) \notag \\
                           &=\sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! (-1)^{k-1} \frac{n-k-1}{n-1}, \notag \\
                           &\leq \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I. \notag 
\end{aligned}$$ We conjecture that the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ in part (c) of Theorem [\[thm:n-1\]](#thm:n-1){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:n-1"} can only be attained by $(n-1,1)$, $(2^2,1^{n-4})$ or $(2,1^{n-2})$:

# $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ with $I\cap\{n-1,n\}=\{n\}$ {#sec:n}

::: lem
[]{#cor:lower_bound_1^n_n-1,1_odd label="cor:lower_bound_1^n_n-1,1_odd"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ is odd and $\{n\}\subseteq I \subseteq\{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$. Then $$\lambda_{(1^n)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\frac{n}{2}(n-2)!.$$
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Similarly to the proof of Lemma [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_odd\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd"}, we have $$\begin{aligned}
  \lambda_{(1^n)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I&=&n(n-2)!+\sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left((-1)^{k-1}-\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}\right)\nonumber\\
   &\ge& n(n-2)!-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq n-3\\ k\text{~is~even}}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\frac{2n-k-2}{n-1}\nonumber\\
   &=& n(n-2)!\left(1-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq n-3\\ k\text{~is~even}}} \frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right)\nonumber \\
   &=& n(n-2)!\left(\frac{1}{(n-5)!}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq 5\\ k\text{~is~even}}} \frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right) \nonumber\\
   & & + n(n-2)!\left(\sum\limits_{\substack{6\leq k\leq n-3\\ k\text{~is~even}}}\frac{1}{(n-k-1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k+1)!}-\frac{2n-k-2}{k(n-k)!}\right)  \nonumber \\
   & & +n(n-2)!\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right) \nonumber \\
   &>& \frac{n}{2}(n-2)!, \nonumber
\end{aligned}$$ where the last step follows from the fact that the right-hand side of ([\[ineq:diff_1\^n_n-1,1_odd\]](#ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="ineq:diff_1^n_n-1,1_odd"}) is positive when taking $k_0=n-2$. ◻
:::

::: lem
[]{#cor:n_even_1^n_n-1,1 label="cor:n_even_1^n_n-1,1"} Suppose $n\ge 8$ is even and $\{n\}\subseteq I\subseteq\{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$. Then $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\lambda_{(1^n)}^I$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Similarly to the proof of Lemma [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_even\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even"}, we have $$\begin{aligned}
  & \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I-\lambda_{(1^n)}^I \nonumber \\
  &\ge   n(n-2)!\left(\frac{n-2}{n}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq n-3\\k~\text{is~odd}}}\frac{1}{(n-k)!} \right) \nonumber \\
  &= n(n-2)!\left(\frac{2n-6}{4(n-4)!}-\sum\limits_{\substack{2\leq k\leq 4\\k~\text{is~odd}}}\frac{1}{(n-k)!} \right) \nonumber\\
  &  + n(n-2)!\left( \sum\limits_{\substack{5\leq k\leq n-3 \\k~\text{is~odd} }} \frac{2n-k-3}{(k+1)(n-k-1)!}-\frac{2n-k-1}{(k-1)(n-k+1)!}-\frac{1}{(n-k)!}\right) \nonumber \\
  & + n(n-2)!\left(\frac{n-2}{n}-\frac{n}{2(n-2)}\right) \nonumber \\
  &> 0. \nonumber \qedhere
 
\end{aligned}$$ ◻
:::

::: lem
[]{#lem:n-2,1,1 label="lem:n-2,1,1"} Suppose $n\ge 9$ and $\{n\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-2, n\}$. Then for any $\zeta\ne (2^2,1^{n-4})$, $(n-m,1^m)$ with $0\leq m\leq n-1$, we have $\lambda_\zeta^I<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Suppose $\zeta$ is any partition of $n$ other than $(2^2,1^{n-4})$ and $(n-m,1^m)$ with $0\leq m\leq n-1$. By Lemma [\[lem:upper_bound\]](#lem:upper_bound){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:upper_bound"}, we have $\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((k,1^{n-k}))<\frac{(n-k)(n-k-1)}{n(n-1)}$ for $2\leq k\leq n-3$. According to Table [3](#tab:tab4){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab4"}, we have $\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((n-2,1^2))\leq \frac{6}{n(n-1)(n-5)}$. Define $$f(n,k)=\begin{cases}
        \frac{(n-k)(n-k-1)}{n(n-1)}, & \text{if}~2\leq k\leq n-3;\\ 
        \frac{6}{n(n-1)(n-5)}, & \text{if}~k=n-2.    
\end{cases}$$ Note that $\tilde{\chi}_{\zeta}((n))=0$ by Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n_cycles"}. Therefore, $$\label{ineq:n-2,1,1_1}
\lambda_\zeta^I = \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) \leq \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! f(n,k). \nonumber$$ One can further verify that $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))< f(n,k)$ for $2\leq k\leq n-2$. Hence $$\begin{aligned}
        & & \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I-\lambda_{\zeta}^I \nonumber \\
        &\ge& 2(n-3)!+ \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \left(\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)-2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)}-f(n,k) \right)\nonumber \\
    &\ge& 2(n-3)!+ \sum_{k=2}^{n-2} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \left(\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)-2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)}-f(n,k) \right)\nonumber \\
        &= & 2(n-3)!+ \sum_{k=2}^{n-3} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \left(\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)-2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)}- \frac{(n-k)(n-k-1)}{n(n-1)}\right)  \nonumber \\
        & & - \frac{3(n-3)!}{n-5} \nonumber \\
        &=& 2(n-3)!- \sum_{k=2}^{n-3} \frac{2(n-3)!}{(n-k)(n-k-2)!} -\frac{n}{n-2}- \frac{3(n-3)!}{n-5} \nonumber \\
        &=& 2(n-3)!\left(1-\sum_{k=2}^{n-3} \frac{1}{(n-k)(n-k-2)!} \right) -\frac{n}{n-2}- \frac{3(n-3)!}{n-5}\nonumber \\ 
        &=& 2(n-3)!\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{(n-2)!}\right) -\frac{n}{n-2}- \frac{3(n-3)!}{n-5} \nonumber \\
        &>& 0.
    
\end{aligned}$$ This completes the proof. ◻
:::

The following is the main result in this section.

::: thm
[]{#thm:n label="thm:n"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $\{n\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$. Then the following statements hold:

-   if $n$ is odd and $I$ contains at least one even number, then the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained uniquely by $(1^n)$, and moreover the multiplicity of this eigenvalue is $1$;

-   if $n$ is odd and $I$ only contains odd numbers, then the strictly second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained by $(n-2,1^2)$ and $(3,1^{n-3})$, and moreover the multiplicity of this eigenvalue is $\frac{(n-1)^2(n-2)^2}{2}$;

-   if $n$ is even, then the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained by $(n-2,1^2)$ or $(2,1^{n-2})$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* (a) For any $\zeta\vdash n$, we have $$\begin{aligned}
        \lambda_\zeta^I= \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k}))+ (n-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n)). \nonumber
    
\end{aligned}$$ According to Lemma [\[lem:character_for_n_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n_cycles"}, if $\zeta\ne (n-m,1^m)$ with $0\leq m\leq n-1$, then $\tilde{\chi}_\zeta((n))=0$. Thus, by Lemma [\[lem:cycle_n-2\]](#lem:cycle_n-2){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:cycle_n-2"}, for any $\zeta\ne (n-m,1^m)$ with $0\leq m\leq n-1$, $$\begin{aligned}
        \lambda_\zeta^I &=& \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber\\
                        &<& \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k})) \nonumber \\
                        &=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I-(n-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((n)) \nonumber\\
                        &=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I+(n-2)!. \label{eq:21}
    
\end{aligned}$$

Now suppose $\zeta=(n-m,1^m)$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$. Since $n\ge 7$ is odd, $\{n\}\subset I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$ and $I$ contains at least one even number less than $n-2$, by Lemmas [\[lem:character_for_n_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n_cycles"} and [\[lem:cycle_n-2\]](#lem:cycle_n-2){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:cycle_n-2"} we obtain $$\begin{aligned}
        \lambda_\zeta^I&=&\sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) + (n-1)! \frac{\chi_\zeta((n))}{\chi_\zeta(\mathbf{1})}\nonumber \\
        &=& \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_\zeta((k,1^{n-k})) + (-1)^m (n-m-1)!m! \nonumber \\
        &<& \sum_{k\in I\setminus\{n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)! \tilde{\chi}_{(n-1,1)}((k,1^{n-k})) + 2(n-3)! \nonumber \\
        &=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I +(n-2)!+2 (n-3)! \nonumber \\
        &=& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I+n(n-3)!  \nonumber \\
        &<& \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I+2(n-2)! .\label{eq:22}
    
\end{aligned}$$ On the other hand, by Lemma [\[cor:lower_bound_1\^n_n-1,1_odd\]](#cor:lower_bound_1^n_n-1,1_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="cor:lower_bound_1^n_n-1,1_odd"} we have $$\begin{aligned}
\label{eq:23}
    \lambda_{(1^n)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\frac{n}{2}(n-2)! .
\end{aligned}$$ It follows from ([\[eq:21\]](#eq:21){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:21"}), ([\[eq:22\]](#eq:22){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:22"}) and ([\[eq:23\]](#eq:23){reference-type="ref" reference="eq:23"}) that $(1^n)$ is the unique partition of $n$ whose corresponding Specht module achieves the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$. By [\[eq:multi\]](#eq:multi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:multi"}, the multiplicity of this eigenvalue is equal to the square of the degree of the sign representation $\rho_{(1^n)}$, which is equal to $1$.

\(b\) One can easily verify the result for $n=7$. Now suppose $n\ge 9$. As there are only odd numbers in $I$, $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ has exactly two connected components and $\lambda_\zeta^I=\lambda_{\zeta'}^I$ for any $\zeta\vdash n$. Thus the largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained by $(n)$ and $(1^n)$. Since $n$ is odd, one can verify that the function $f(n,k)$ defined in the proof of Lemma [\[lem:n-2,1,1\]](#lem:n-2,1,1){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:n-2,1,1"} also satisfies $f(n,k)\ge \tilde{\chi}_{(2^2,1^{n-4})}((k,1^{n-k}))$ for $2\leq k\leq n-2$. Thus the result in Lemma [\[lem:n-2,1,1\]](#lem:n-2,1,1){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:n-2,1,1"} actually applies to any $\zeta\ne (n-m,1^m)$ with $0\leq m\leq n-1$, that is, $\lambda_{\zeta}^I<\lambda^I_{(n-2,1^2)}$. Since $I$ contains only odd numbers, we have $I\ne \{2,3,\ldots,n-2,n\}$. So by parts (a) and (b) of Lemma [\[re:n-m,1\^m\]](#re:n-m,1^m){reference-type="ref" reference="re:n-m,1^m"}, we get $\lambda^I_{(n-2,1^2)}>\lambda^I_{(n-1,1)}=\lambda^I_{(2,1^{n-2})}$ and the maximum of $\lambda_{(n-m,1^m)}^I$ for $1\leq m\leq n-2$ can only be attained by $m=2,n-3$. That is, the strictly second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ is attained by $(n-2,1^2)$ and $(3,1^{n-3})$. As the dimensions of $\rho_{(n-2,1^2)}$ and $\rho_{(3,1^{n-3})}$ are both $\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}$, the multiplicity of this eigenvalue is $\frac{(n-1)^2(n-2)^2}{2}$ by equation [\[eq:multi\]](#eq:multi){reference-type="eqref" reference="eq:multi"}.

\(c\) One can verify that the result is true for $n=8$. Now suppose $n\ge 10$. First, by Lemma [\[lem:n-2,1,1\]](#lem:n-2,1,1){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:n-2,1,1"}, for any $\zeta\ne (2^2,1^{n-4})$, $(n-m,1^m)$ with $0\leq m\leq n-1$, we have $\lambda_{\zeta}^I<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$. Second, by parts (a) and (b) of Lemma [\[re:n-m,1\^m\]](#re:n-m,1^m){reference-type="ref" reference="re:n-m,1^m"}, the maximum of $\lambda_{(n-m,1^m)}^I$ for $1\leq m\leq n-1$ can only be attained by $m=1,2,n-3,n-2$ or $n-1$, and $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(3,1^{n-3})}^I$ as $I$ contains the even number $n$. Thirdly, by Lemma [\[cor:n_even_1\^n_n-1,1\]](#cor:n_even_1^n_n-1,1){reference-type="ref" reference="cor:n_even_1^n_n-1,1"} and parts (b) and (c) of Lemma [\[re:n-m,1\^m\]](#re:n-m,1^m){reference-type="ref" reference="re:n-m,1^m"}, we have $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I<\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I<\max\left\{\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I,\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I\right\}$. Therefore, the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(n-2,1^2),~(2,1^{n-2})$ and $(2^2,1^{n-4})$.

We now show that we can rule out $(2^2,1^{n-4})$. In fact, since $n$ is even, we have $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))>\tilde{\chi}_{(2^2,1^{n-4})}((k,1^{n-k}))$ for every $2\leq k\leq n$ except $k=n-2$. Thus, $$\begin{aligned}
    & &\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I-\lambda_{(2^2,1^{n-4})}^I\nonumber \\
    &=&\sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left(\frac{(n-k-1)(n-k-2)-2c_2}{(n-1)(n-2)}+(-1)^k\frac{(n-k)(n-k-3)+2c_2}{n(n-3)}  \right) \nonumber\\ 
    &\ge& \sum_{k\in \{n-2,n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left(\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))-\tilde{\chi}_{(2^2,1^{n-4})}((k,1^{n-k}))\right) \nonumber \\
    &=& 2(n-3)!-(n-1)(n-4)! \nonumber\\
    &>&0,  \label{ineq:36}
\end{aligned}$$ from which the desired result follows. ◻
:::

# $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ with $\{n-1,n\}\subseteq I$ {#sec:n1n}

::: lem
[]{#cor:odd_n_n-1 label="cor:odd_n_n-1"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ is odd and $\{n-1,n\}\subseteq I \subseteq\{2,3,\ldots,n-1,n\}$. Then the following statements hold:

-   if the largest number in $I\setminus \{n-1,n\}$ is odd and $I\ne\{2,3,n-1,n\}$, then $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I>\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$;

-   if the largest number in $I\setminus \{n-1,n\}$ is even, then $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I>\lambda_{(1^n)}^I$;

-   if $I=\{2,3,n-1,n\}$ or $\{n-1,n\}$, then $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I=\lambda_{(1^n)}^I$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Note that $$\begin{aligned}
  \lambda_{(1^n)}^I-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I
  &=& \sum_{k\in I} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left((-1)^{k-1}-\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}\right) \nonumber \\
  &=& \sum_{k\in I\setminus \{n-1,n\}} \binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\left((-1)^{k-1}-\frac{n-k-1}{n-1}\right) \nonumber \\
  &=& \lambda_{(1^n)}^{I\setminus \{n-1,n\}}-\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^{I\setminus \{n-1,n\}}.
\end{aligned}$$ Thus, if $I=\{n-1,n\}$, then $\lambda_{(1^n)}^{I}=\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$, and if $I\ne \{n-1,n\}$, then we obtain the desired results by applying Lemmas [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_odd\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_odd"} and [\[lem:diff\_(1\^n)\_(n-1,1)\_even\]](#lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:diff_(1^n)_(n-1,1)_even"} directly to $I \setminus \{n-1,n\} \neq \emptyset$. ◻
:::

::: lem
[]{#lem:last_one label="lem:last_one"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $\{n,n-1\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. If $n$ is even, then $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I$ for $m=2,3,n-3,n-2$; if $n$ is odd, then $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I$ for $m=2,3,n-3$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* With the help of Table [1](#tab:tab1){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:tab1"} one can verify that for $m=3, n-3$ we have $$\sum_{k=n-1}^n\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))>\sum_{k=n-1}^n\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-k}))$$ and $$\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))\ge \tilde{\chi}_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}((k,1^{n-k})) \quad \text{for~any~}2\leq k\leq n-2.$$ Since $\{n-1,n\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$, it follows that $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I$ for $m=3, n-3$.

We have $\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))>\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((k,1^{n-k}))$ for $3\leq k\leq n$. We also have $\sum_{k\in\{2,n\}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,1^2)}((k,1^{n-k}))>\sum_{k\in \{2,n\}}\binom{n}{k}(k-1)!\tilde{\chi}_{(n-2,2)}((k,1^{n-k}))$. Thus $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(n-2,2)}^I$ whenever $n \in I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Inequality ([\[ineq:36\]](#ineq:36){reference-type="ref" reference="ineq:36"}) implies that $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(2^2,1^{n-4})}^I$ whenever $n$ is even and $n \in I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. ◻
:::

The main result in this section is as follows.

::: thm
[]{#thm:n_n-1 label="thm:n_n-1"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $\{n,n-1\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Then the following statements hold:

-   if $n$ is even, then the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n, C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(1^n),(n-2,1^2)$ or $(2,1^{n-2})$;

-   if $n$ is odd, then the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n, C(n,I))$ can only be achieved by $(1^n),(n-2,1^2),(3,1^{n-3})$ or $(2^2,1^{n-4})$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* (a) One can verify that the result is true for $n=8$. Now suppose $n\ge 10$ and $n$ is even. According to Lemmas [\[lem:character_for_n-1_cycles\]](#lem:character_for_n-1_cycles){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:character_for_n-1_cycles"} and [\[lem:n-2,1,1\]](#lem:n-2,1,1){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:n-2,1,1"}, if $\zeta\ne (n-m,1^m), (n-m,2,1^{m-2})$, then $\lambda_{\zeta}^I=\lambda_\zeta^{I\setminus\{n-1\}}<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^{I\setminus \{n-1\}}=\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$. On the other hand, by Lemma [\[re:n-m,1\^m\]](#re:n-m,1^m){reference-type="ref" reference="re:n-m,1^m"} we have $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I<\max\left\{\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I,\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I\right\}$ and $\lambda_\zeta^I<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ for $\zeta=(n-m,1^m)$ with $3\leq m\leq n-3$. Hence $\lambda_{\zeta}^I<\max\left\{\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I,\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I\right\}$ for any $\zeta \ne (n),(1^n),(n-2,1^2),(2,1^{n-2})$, $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$ with $2\leq m\leq n-2$.

Since $n-1\in I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n-1,n\}$, by Remark [\[re:(n-m,2,1\^m-2)\]](#re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)){reference-type="ref" reference="re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)"} the maximum of $\lambda^I_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}$ for $2\leq m\leq n-2$ can only be attained by $m=2,3,n-3$ or $n-2$. Moreover, Lemma [\[lem:last_one\]](#lem:last_one){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:last_one"} implies that $\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ for $m=2,3,n-3,n-2$. Therefore, the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n, C(n,I))$ can only be attained by $(1^n),(n-2,1^2)$ or $(2,1^{n-2})$.

\(b\) One can easily verify this result for $n=7$. Now suppose $n\ge 9$ and $n$ is odd. Similarly to the proof of part (a) above, one can prove that $\lambda_{\zeta}^I=\lambda_\zeta^{I\setminus\{n-1\}}<\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^{I\setminus \{n-1\}}=\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ for any $\zeta$ other than $(n-m,1^m)$ and $(n-m,2,1^{m-2})$. By Lemma [\[re:n-m,1\^m\]](#re:n-m,1^m){reference-type="ref" reference="re:n-m,1^m"}, we have $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I\leq \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ and $\lambda_{(n-m,1^m)}^I < \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ for $3\leq m\leq n-4$. Note that, if $\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I= \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$, then $I = \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$ and thus $\lambda_{(1^n)}^I>\lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I$ by Lemma [\[cor:odd_n_n-1\]](#cor:odd_n_n-1){reference-type="ref" reference="cor:odd_n_n-1"}. A straightforward computation shows that $\lambda_{(2,1^{n-2})}^I\leq \lambda_{(n-1,1)}^I<\max\left\{\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I,\lambda_{(1^n)}^I\right\}$. Finally, by Lemma [\[lem:last_one\]](#lem:last_one){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:last_one"} we have $\lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I>\lambda_{(n-m,2,1^{m-2})}^I$ for $m=2,3,n-3$. Combining all these with Remark [\[re:(n-m,2,1\^m-2)\]](#re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)){reference-type="ref" reference="re:(n-m,2,1^m-2)"}, we obtain the desired result. ◻
:::

Theorem [\[thm:n_n-1\]](#thm:n_n-1){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:n_n-1"} implies the following result.

::: cor
[]{#cor:aldous_n-1_n label="cor:aldous_n-1_n"} Suppose $n\ge 7$ and $\{n,n-1\}\subseteq I \subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ does not have the Aldous property.
:::

We conjecture that the second largest eigenvalue of $\mathrm{Cay}(S_n,C(n,I))$ in part (b) of Theorem [\[thm:n_n-1\]](#thm:n_n-1){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:n_n-1"} can only be achieved by $(1^n),(n-2,1^2)$ or $(2^2,1^{n-4})$:

::: cx
Suppose $n\ge 7$ is odd and $\{n-1,n\}\subseteq I\subseteq \{2,3,\ldots,n\}$. Then $$\lambda_{(3,1^{n-3})}^I<\max\left\{\lambda_{(1^n)}^I, \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I, \lambda_{(2^2, 1^{n-4})}^I\right\}.$$
:::

Note that, by Lemma [\[re:n-m,1\^m\]](#re:n-m,1^m){reference-type="ref" reference="re:n-m,1^m"}, we already know that $\lambda_{(3,1^{n-3})}^I \le \lambda_{(n-2,1^2)}^I$ and the equality holds if and only if $I$ contains only odd numbers other than $n-1$ and $n-2$.
