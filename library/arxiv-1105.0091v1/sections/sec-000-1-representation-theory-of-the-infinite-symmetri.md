In this first paragraph, we recall the representation theory of the infinite symmetric group, and a basic tool that we will use throughout the paper, namely, the Hopf algebra of symmetric functions (later, we shall also need the algebras of quasi-symmetric and free quasi-symmetric functions).

## Representation theory of the symmetric groups $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$

To begin with, let us recall the representation theory of the finite symmetric groups $\mathfrak{S}_{n}=\mathfrak{S}(\left[\!\left[1,n\right]\!\right])$. If $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ is a permutation, it can be written uniquely as a product of disjoint cycles; for example, $$41728635=(1,4,2)(3,7)(5,8)(6).$$ Then, two permutations are conjugated in $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ if and only if the lengths or their cycles form the same integer partition (here, $(3,2,2,1)$). Thus, the conjugacy classes of $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ are labelled by the elements of $\mathfrak{Y}_{n}$. The irreducible representations of $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ are also labelled by integer partitions of size $n$. Indeed, if $\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r})$, let us denote $$\begin{aligned}
\Delta^{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})&=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\Delta(x_{\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i-1}+1},\ldots,x_{\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i}})\\
&=\prod_{i=1}^{r} \left(\prod_{\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i-1}+1\leq j<k\leq \lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i}}x_{j}-x_{k}\right).
\end{aligned}$$ The symmetric group acts (on the right) on $\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]$ by permutation of the variables, and the complex vector space $V$ generated by the $(\Delta^{\lambda})\cdot \sigma$ with $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ happens to be an irreducible module over $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$; moreover, all the isomorphism classes of irreducibles can be obtained this way (*cf.* [@JK81]).

If $\lambda$ is a partition of size $n$, we represent it by its **Young diagram**: this is the array of $n$ boxes with $\lambda_{1}$ boxes on the first row, $\lambda_{2}$ boxes on the second row, *etc.* $$(5,4,2,2) \quad \leftrightarrow \quad \yng(2,2,4,5)\,.$$ The conjugate of a partition $\lambda$ is the partition $\lambda'$ obtained by symmetrizing its Young diagram with respect to the first diagonal; for instance, $(5,4,2,2)'=(4,4,2,2,1)$. In the following, we shall denote by $V^{\lambda}$ the irreducible module over $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ associated to the polynomial $\Delta^{\lambda'}$; this is the usual convention for encoding the values of the irreducible characters in the algebra of symmetric functions, see §[0.2](#symfunc){reference-type="ref" reference="symfunc"}. The two one-dimensional representations of $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ correspond to the partitions $(n)$ and $(1,1,\ldots,1)=1^{n}$: the first one gives the trivial representation and the second one is the sign representation.

The Young diagrams yield a simple description of the branching rules of the irreducible modules of $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ and $\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}$. If $\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$ and $\Lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n+1}$, we denote $\lambda \nearrow \Lambda$ if $\Lambda$ can be obtained from $\lambda$ by adding a single box to the Young diagram.

::: example
If $\lambda=(5,4,2,2)$, the partitions $\Lambda$ of size $14=13+1$ such that $\lambda \nearrow \Lambda$ are $(6,4,2,2)$, $(5,5,2,2)$, $(5,4,3,2)$ and $(5,4,2,2,1)$.
:::

The **branching rules** of the symmetric groups are the following (see [@OV04], [@Zel81]): $$\begin{aligned}
\forall \lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n},&\,\,\mathrm{Ind}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}(V^{\lambda})=\sum_{\Lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n+1}\,\,|\,\,\lambda \nearrow \Lambda}V^{\Lambda}\\
 \forall \Lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n+1},&\,\,\mathrm{Res}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}(V^{\Lambda})=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}\,\,|\,\,\lambda \nearrow \Lambda}V^{\lambda}.
 
\end{aligned}$$ Since the restriction of representations preserves the dimension, by applying $n$ times the second branching rule, one sees that $$\dim \lambda=\dim V^{\lambda}=\mathrm{card}\,\{\text{standard tableaux of shape }\lambda\},$$ where a standard tableau of shape $\lambda$ is a numbering of the boxes of the Young diagram of $\lambda$ that is strictly increasing along the rows and along the columns.

::: example
The five standard tableaux of shape $(3,2)$ are $$\young(45,123)\quad;\quad\young(35,124)\quad;\quad\young(25,134)\quad;\quad\young(34,125)\quad;\quad\young(24,135)\,.$$ Hence, $\dim (3,2)=5$.
:::

There is also a "closed" formula for $\dim \lambda$ known as the **hook length formula**. If $\oblong=(i,j)$ is a box of the Young diagram of $\lambda$, its hook length is $h(\oblong)=\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}'-i-j+1$. For instance, in $(5,4,2,2)$, the hook length of the box $(3,1)$ appearing on the first row and the third column is $4$. Then, $$\dim \lambda =\frac{n!}{\prod_{\oblong \in \lambda} h(\oblong)},$$ see for instance [@Mac95 §1.7]. This can be proved by relating the character theory of the symmetric groups to the theory of symmetric functions that we are going to review in the next paragraph.

## The Hopf algebra of symmetric functions {#symfunc}

A formal power series[^1] $f$ of bounded degree in an infinite sequence of variables $X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots\}$ is called a **symmetric function** if, for any finite permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$, $$f(x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\ldots)=f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)=f(X).$$ In other words, for any $i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq \cdots \neq i_{r}$ and $j_{1}\neq j_{2}\neq \cdots \neq j_{r}$, and any $(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in \mathbb{N}^{r}$, $$[x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{a_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}^{a_{r}}](f)=[x_{j_{1}}^{a_{1}}x_{j_{2}}^{a_{2}}\cdots x_{j_{r}}^{a_{r}}](f).$$ The set of symmetric functions will be denoted by $\mathbf{Sym}$; this is an infinite-dimensional complex commutative algebra. A transcendance basis of $\mathbf{Sym}$ consists in the **power sums** $$p_{k}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \,(x_{i})^{k}.$$ If $\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r})$ is a partition, we shall denote $p_{\mu}=p_{\mu_{1}}\cdots p_{\mu_{r}}$; a linear basis of $\mathbf{Sym}$ is therefore $(p_{\mu})_{\mu}$, where $\mu$ runs over $\mathfrak{Y}=\bigsqcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{Y}_{n}$ ([@Mac95 §1.2]).

Another important basis of $\mathbf{Sym}$ is given by the **Schur functions**, see [@Mac95 §1.3]. If $\lambda$ is a partition, $\ell(\lambda)$ will denote its number of parts. Then, given variables $x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}$ with $N \geq \ell(\lambda)$, we define the antisymmetric polynomial of type $\lambda$ by $$a_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})=\sum_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{N}} \varepsilon(\sigma)\, x^{\sigma(\lambda)},$$ where $x^{\sigma(\lambda)}=x_{1}^{\lambda_{\sigma(1)}}x_{2}^{\lambda_{\sigma(2)}}\cdots x_{N}^{\lambda_{\sigma(N)}}$ --- here, we complete $\lambda$ with parts of size $0$ to obtain a sequence of length $N$. Finally, the Schur polynomial is defined as the quotient $$s_{\lambda,N}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})=\frac{a_{\lambda+\delta}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})}{a_{\delta}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})},$$ where $\delta=(N-1,N-2,\ldots,1,0)$, and $$\lambda+\delta=(\lambda_{1}+N-1,\lambda_{2}+N-2,\ldots,\lambda_{N-1}+1,\lambda_{N}).$$ If $M$ is a matrix in $\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})$ with eigenvalues $x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}$, then by Weyl's character formula, $s_{\lambda,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})$ is the character of the action of $M$ on the irreducible $\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})$-module of highest weight $\lambda$. On the other hand, for any $N$, $$s_{\lambda,N+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},x_{N+1}=0)=s_{\lambda,N}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),$$ so there is a formal power series $s_{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)$ that is the inductive limit of the $s_{\lambda,N}$'s. Since these polynomials are defined as quotients of antisymmetric polynomials, $s_{\lambda}$ is always a symmetric function in $\mathbf{Sym}$. Actually, $(s_{\lambda})_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}}$ is a linear basis of $\mathbf{Sym}$, and by using the Schur-Weyl duality between $\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ on $(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\otimes n}$, one can show that the transition matrix between the two bases $(s_{\lambda})_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}}$ and $(p_{\mu})_{\mu \in \mathfrak{Y}}$ is given by the tables of characters of the symmetric groups (see [@Mac95 §1.7]).

::: proposition
[]{#frobenius label="frobenius"} For $\lambda$ and $\mu$ in $\mathfrak{Y}_{n}$, we denote by $\zeta^{\lambda}(\mu)$ the value of the (non-normalized) character of $V^{\lambda}$ on a permutation of cycle type $\mu$. Then, for any partition $\mu$, $$p_{\mu}(X)=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}} \zeta^{\lambda}(\mu)\,s_{\lambda}(X).$$
:::

This result is the celebrated **Frobenius formula**.

The aforementioned link between symmetric functions and the representation theory of the symmetric groups gives rise to an isomorphism between two self-adjoint **Hopf algebras**, see [@Zel81]. On the one hand, let us denote by $K(\mathfrak{S}_{n})$ the complex vector space built from the Grothendieck group of the category of representations of $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$; it means that $K(\mathfrak{S}_{n})$ has for linear basis the irreducible modules $V^{\lambda}$ with $\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$. The graded vector space $K(\mathfrak{S})=\bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{N}}K(\mathfrak{S}_{n})$ is a graded connected Hopf algebra for the following operations: $$\begin{aligned}
\text{product: }&\forall \lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{p}, \,\,\,\forall \mu \in \mathfrak{Y}_{q},\,\,\,V^{\lambda}\times V^{\mu}= \mathrm{Ind}_{\mathfrak{S}_{p}\times \mathfrak{S}_{q}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{p+q}}(V^{\lambda}\otimes V^{\mu}) \,;\\
\text{coproduct: }&\forall \lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n},\,\,\,\Delta(V^{\lambda})=\sum_{p+q=n} \mathrm{Res}_{\mathfrak{S}_{p}\times \mathfrak{S}_{q}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(V^{\lambda})\,;\\
\text{involution: }&\forall \lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n},\,\,\,\nu(V^{\lambda})=V^{1^{n}}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}} V^{\lambda}=V^{\lambda'}.
\end{aligned}$$ The involution $\nu$ defined above is equal to the antipode of the Hopf algebra up to sign. Moreover, there is a scalar product on $K(\mathfrak{S})$ defined by $\left\langle V^{\lambda}\vphantom{V^{\mu}}\,\right |\left.V^{\mu} \vphantom{V^{\lambda}}\right\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}$, and $K(\mathfrak{S})$ is a self-adjoint Hopf algebra with respect to this pairing. On the other hand, $\mathbf{Sym}$ has also a structure of self-adjoint Hopf algebra if one sets: $$\Delta(p_{k})=p_{k}\otimes 1 + 1 \otimes p_{k}\qquad;\qquad \nu(p_{k})=(-1)^{k-1}p_{k}\qquad;\qquad \left\langle p_{\mu}\vphantom{p_{\rho}}\,\right |\left.p_{\rho} \vphantom{p_{\mu}}\right\rangle=\delta_{\mu\rho}\,z_{\mu}$$ where $z_{\mu}=\prod_{i\geq 1}i^{m_{i}}\,m_{i}!$ if $\mu=1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\cdots s^{m_{s}}$. Then, it is shown in [@Zel81] that:

::: proposition
The rule $V^{\lambda}\mapsto s_{\lambda}$ provides an isomorphism of self-adjoint graded Hopf algebras between $K(\mathfrak{S})$ and $\mathbf{Sym}$. With some additional conditions, this isomorphism is unique up to the multiplication by the antipode.
:::

In particular, the Schur functions form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbf{Sym}$, and the character values of the symmetric groups satisfy $\zeta^{\lambda}(\mu)=\left\langle s_{\lambda}\vphantom{p_{\mu}}\,\right |\left.p_{\mu} \vphantom{s_{\lambda}}\right\rangle$.

In $\mathbf{Sym}$, the structure of Hopf algebra can be related to operations on alphabets. Indeed, given two infinite alphabets $X$ and $Y$, if $\overline{X}=\{-x_{1},-x_{2},\ldots\}$ and $X+Y=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,y_{1},y_{2},\ldots\}$, then $$(\Delta p_{k})(X,Y)=p_{k}(X)+p_{k}(Y)=p_{k}(X+Y)\qquad;\qquad (\nu( p_{k}))(X)=-p_{k}(\overline{X}).$$ Hence, the coproduct $\Delta$ in $\mathbf{Sym}$ corresponds simply to the addition of alphabets, and if the (formal) difference of two alphabets is defined by $p_{k}(X-Y)=p_{k}(X)-p_{k}(Y)$ for any $k\geq 1$, then for any symmetric function $f$, $$f(X-Y)=[(1\otimes \nu) \circ \Delta]f(X,\overline{Y}).$$

To conclude this paragraph, let us speak about **specializations**. By a formal specialization of $\mathbf{Sym}$, we just mean a morphism of $\mathbb{C}$-algebras between $\mathbf{Sym}$ and $\mathbb{C}$. Since $\mathbf{Sym}=\mathbb{C}[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k},\ldots]$, it amounts to give the images of the $p_{k}$'s. Another way to define a specialization is to give values to the variables of the alphabet $X$; however, not all the specializations of $\mathbf{Sym}$ can be obtained in this way.

::: example
We set $$X_{q}=\frac{1}{1-[q]}=(1,q,q^{2},q^{3},\ldots).$$ Then, $p_{k}(X_{q})=\frac{1}{1-q^{k}}$ for any $k$, and there is a hook-length formula for the specializations $s_{\lambda}(X_{q})$ of the Schur functions corresponding to this alphabet: $$s_{\lambda}(X_{q})=\frac{q^{n(\lambda)}}{\prod_{\oblong \in \lambda}1-q^{h(\oblong)}},$$ where $n(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}(i-1)\lambda_{i}$.
:::

Given two specializations denoted by formal alphabets $A$ and $B$, one can as before construct new specializations $A+B$ and $A-B$ by setting: $$\forall k\geq 1,\,\,\,p_{k}(A+B)=p_{k}(A)+p_{k}(B)\qquad;\qquad p_{k}(A-B)=p_{k}(A)-p_{k}(B).$$ Even if $A$ and $B$ are true alphabets, the specialization $A-B$ may not come from a true alphabet; hence, differences of alphabets give rise to "new" specializations of $\mathbf{Sym}$, and we will see hereafter that this operation is important in the setting of the representation theory of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$. Another important specialization of $\mathbf{Sym}$ that will be used in the next paragraph is the so-called exponential alphabet $E$. This formal alphabet satisfies: $$p_{1}(E)=1\qquad;\qquad\forall k \geq 2,\,\,\,p_{k}(E)=0.$$ Using the reciprocal of the Frobenius formula, one sees that for any $\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$, $s_{\lambda}(E)=\frac{\dim \lambda}{n!}$.

## Coherent systems of measures and Thoma's theorem for the characters of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$

We denote by $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ the set of finite permutations of $\mathbb{N}^{*}=\left[\!\left[1,+\infty\right]\!\right]$; this is also the inductive limit of the $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$'s with respect to the natural embeddings $\mathfrak{S}_{n}\hookrightarrow \mathfrak{S}_{n+k}$. As explained in the introduction, there is no hope to have a complete knowledge of the representation theory of this group: indeed, the existence of factor representations of type II or III implies that a unitary representation of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ does not in general have a *unique* expansion as a direct integral of factors. However, one can classify the **characters** of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$, that is to say the functions $\chi : \mathfrak{S}_{\infty} \to \mathbb{C}$ that satisfy the three following conditions:

1.  $\chi$ is constant on conjugacy classes: $\forall \sigma,\tau,\,\,\chi(\tau\sigma\tau^{-1})=\chi(\sigma)$.

2.  $\chi$ is normalized so that $\chi(1)=1$.

3.  $\chi$ is non-negative definite: $\forall \sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n},\,\,(\chi(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}^{-1}))_{1\leq i,j \leq n}$ is hermitian and non-negative definite.

Such a character is always the trace of a unitary representation of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ in a Von Neumann algebra of finite type. If one considers only finite type *factor* representations of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$, then one obtained the **extremal characters** that satisfy the additional condition:

4.  $\chi$ cannot be written as a combination $a\chi_{1}+(1-a)\chi_{2}$ with $\chi_{1}\neq \chi_{2}$ characters and $a \in\, ]0,1[$.

We will denote by $\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ the set of characters of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$, and by $\mathcal{X}^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ the set of extremal characters. Then, $\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ is a convex compact set (for the topology of simple convergence), and $$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{X}^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})&=\mathrm{Extr}(\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}))=\{\text{extremal points of }\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})\}\,;\\
\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})&=\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{X}^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}))=\{\text{borelian probability measures on }\mathcal{X}^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})\}.
\end{aligned}$$ Hence, in order to understand the character theory of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$, it is sufficient to determine the set of extremal (or irreducible) characters of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$. As explained beautifully in [@KOV04; @Oko97], this has been done by E. Thoma ([@Tho64]), who relates this problem to the classification of totally positive sequences. We define the **Thoma simplex** $\Omega$ as the set of pairs $\omega=(\alpha,\beta)$ of non-increasing sequences of non-negative real numbers $$\alpha=(\alpha_{1}\geq \alpha_{2}\geq \cdots \geq 0)\qquad;\qquad\beta=(\beta_{1}\geq \beta_{2}\geq \cdots \geq 0)$$ such that $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i}=1-\gamma$ with $\gamma \geq0$. To any point $\omega$ of this infinite dimensional simplex, we associate the formal alphabet $A-B+\gamma E$, where $A=\alpha=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots\}$, $B=\overline{\beta}=\{-\beta_{1},-\beta_{2},\ldots\}$, and $E$ is the exponential alphabet. Thus, $$\begin{aligned}
p_{1}(A-B+\gamma E)&=p_{1}(\alpha)-p_{1}(\overline{\beta})+\gamma p_{1}(E)=p_{1}(\alpha)+p_{1}(\beta)+\gamma=1\,;\\
p_{k\geq 2}(A-B+\gamma E)&=p_{k}(\alpha)-p_{k}(\overline{\beta})=p_{k}(\alpha)+(-1)^{k-1}p_{k}(\beta)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\alpha_{i})^{k}+(-1)^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\beta_{i})^{k}.
\end{aligned}$$

::: theorem
[]{#thomatheorem label="thomatheorem"} The set of extremal characters $\mathcal{X}^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ can be identified (homeomorphically) with the Thoma simplex $\Omega$, and the character $\chi^{\omega}$ corresponding to a point $\omega=(\alpha,\beta) \in \Omega$ writes as $$\chi^{\omega}(\sigma)=p_{\mu}(A-B+\gamma E)$$ if $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ is of cycle type $\mu \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$ --- since $p_{1}(A-B+\gamma E)=1$, this definition does not depend on the choice of $n$.
:::

In particular, a remarkable property of extremal characters of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ is that they factorize on disjoint cycles. Actually, it has been proved that these two conditions are equivalent, see [@Tho64; @KV84; @Oko97], and also [@GK10] for a proof that relates this property to the notion of exchangeability of (non-commutative) random variables. Assuming only this multiplicativity, the methods of Kerov and Vershik (which are reviewed in Section [\[largenumbers\]](#largenumbers){reference-type="ref" reference="largenumbers"}) allow to reprove Thoma's theorem. That said, we shall assume in the following that Theorem [\[thomatheorem\]](#thomatheorem){reference-type="ref" reference="thomatheorem"} is already known, and use it as the starting point of our new results.

Another way of describing $\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ and $\mathcal{X}^{*}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ is by using the notion of **coherent systems of measures** on $\mathfrak{Y}=\bigsqcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{Y}_{n}$. A function $H$ on $\mathfrak{Y}$ is said harmonic if, for any partition $\lambda$, $$H(\lambda)=\sum_{\lambda \nearrow \Lambda}H(\Lambda).$$ Let us suppose that $H$ is positive and that $H(\emptyset)=1$; we then set $P_{n}(\lambda)=H(\lambda)\times \dim\lambda$. In this setting, it is an easy recurrence to show that on each level $\mathfrak{Y}_{n}$ of the Young graph $\mathfrak{Y}$, $P_{n}$ is a probability measure. Indeed, for any $n$, $$\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\Lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n+1}}P_{n+1}(\Lambda)&=\sum_{\Lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n+1}} H(\Lambda)\,\dim \Lambda=\sum_{\Lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n+1}} \sum_{\lambda\nearrow \Lambda} H(\Lambda)\,\dim\lambda\\
&=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}} H(\lambda)\,\dim \lambda=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}} P_{n}(\lambda).
\end{aligned}$$

We call coherent system of probability measures on $\mathfrak{Y}$ a sequence of probability measures $(P_{n})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ coming from a positive normalized harmonic function on $\mathfrak{Y}$.

::: proposition
The coherent systems of probability measures provide a bijection between $\mathcal{X}(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty})$ and the set of positive normalized harmonic functions on $\mathfrak{Y}$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* If $\chi$ is character of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$, its restrictions $\chi_{n}=\chi_{| \mathfrak{S}_{n}}$ are non-negative normalized characters of the symmetric groups, so they are positive linear combinations of the normalized irreducible characters $\chi^{\lambda}$: $$\forall n,\,\,\,\chi_{|\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}} P_{n,\chi}(\lambda)\,\chi^{\lambda}.$$ Since $(\chi_{|\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}})_{|\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\chi_{|\mathfrak{S}_{n}}$, the $P_{n,\chi}$'s form a coherent system. Conversely, given a coherent system of probability measures, one can define on each group $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ a non-negative character by using the formula written above. The coherence of the system implies that these definitions are compatible, whence a well-defined character of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$. ◻
:::

Let us denote by $(\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega})_{n\in \mathbb{N}}$ the coherent system associated to a point $\omega$ in the Thoma simplex. For any permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}\subset \mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ of cycle type $\mu \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$, $$\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}}\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega}[\lambda]\,\,\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)&=\chi^{\omega}_{|\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\sigma)=\chi^{\omega}(\sigma)=p_{\mu}(A-B+\gamma E)\\
&=_{(\text{Frobenius formula})} \sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}} s_{\lambda}(A-B+\gamma E)\,\zeta^{\lambda}(\mu).
\end{aligned}$$ Since this is true for any permutation, the coherent system of probability measures on $\mathfrak{Y}$ associated to $\omega$ is therefore given by the specialization $A-B+\gamma E$ of the Schur functions:

::: proposition
[]{#csspezialschur label="csspezialschur"} $\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega}[\lambda]=(\dim \lambda)\,s_{\lambda}(A-B+\gamma E)$.
:::

::: example
Take $\omega=\mathbf{0}=((0,0,\ldots),(0,0,\ldots))$. Then, $\chi^{\mathbf{0}}$ is the regular trace $\tau$ of the infinite symmetric group: $$\tau(\omega)=\begin{cases}
1 &\text{if }\omega=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{N}},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}$$ The corresponding measures are the so-called **Plancherel measures** of the symmetric groups: $$\mathbb{P}_{n}[\lambda]=\mathbb{P}_{n,\mathbf{0}}[\lambda]=(\dim\lambda)\,s_{\lambda}(E)=\frac{(\dim \lambda)^{2}}{n!}.$$ The asymptotics of these measures have been studied in [@LS77; @KV77; @Ker93; @IO02]. It turns out that this point of the Thoma simplex will be the only one for which our methods won't give a sufficiently precise result (more on this at the end of the paper).
:::

Proposition [\[csspezialschur\]](#csspezialschur){reference-type="ref" reference="csspezialschur"} is very important from a combinatorial point of view: it enables us to relate the coherent systems of probability measures coming from characters of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ to very general models of random permutations. The goal of the next section is to explain this link.

[^1]: In the following, all the coefficients of the functions and power series are supposed complex, though one could define everything over $\mathbb{Q}$.
