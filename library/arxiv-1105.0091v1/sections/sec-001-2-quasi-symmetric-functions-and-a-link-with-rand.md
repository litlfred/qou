[]{#randompermutation label="randompermutation"} In this section, we shall explain how the coherent systems of measures on $\mathfrak{Y}$ are related to models of random permutations that can be defined by using generalized riffle shuffles in the sense of [@Stan01; @Ful02].

## The Hopf algebras $\mathbf{QSym}$ and $\mathbf{FQSym}$ {#fqsym}

To begin with, we have to present two new combinatorial Hopf algebras, namely, $\mathbf{QSym}$ and $\mathbf{FQSym}$. In the following, $X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}$ is an infinite ordered alphabet, but this time with non-commuting letters. The algebra $\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle$ generated by these noncommutative variables has for linear basis the words $w=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\ldots x_{i_{r}}$. The algebra of **free quasi-symmetric functions**, first introduced in [@MR95] and redefined in [@NCSF6], is a subalgebra of $\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle$ that happens to have a natural structure of Hopf algebra and to be related to numerous other combinatorial Hopf algebras. It is defined by using the **standardization** of words. Given a word $w=x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\ldots x_{i_{r}}$, let us denote by $\{j_{1} <j_{2}<\cdots <j_{s}\}$ the set of distinct indices $i$ appearing in $w$, and by $m_{1},\ldots,m_{s}$ their multiplicities. In $w$, we replace from left to right the occurrences of $x_{j_{1}}$ by $1,2,\ldots, m_{1}$; then, we replace from left to right the occurrences of $x_{j_{2}}$ by $m_{1}+1,m_{1}+2,\ldots,m_{1}+m_{2}$; *etc.* We thus obtain a permutation of size $n$, called the standardization of the word $w$.

::: example
If $w=x_{4}x_{3}x_{1}x_{5}x_{1}x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}$, then $\mathrm{Std}(w)=64182357$.
:::

Now, for any permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$, we define elements $F_{\sigma}$ and $G_{\sigma}$ in $\mathbb{C}\langle X \rangle$ by $$G_{\sigma}=\sum_{w\,\,|\,\,\mathrm{Std}(w)=\sigma}w\qquad;\qquad F_{\sigma}=G_{\sigma^{-1}}.$$ It turns out that the $F_{\sigma}$'s with $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}=\bigsqcup_{n \in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ generate linearly a subalgebra of $\mathbb{C}\langle X \rangle$. For two permutations $\sigma=\sigma(1)\cdots \sigma(p)$ and $\tau=\tau(1)\cdots \tau(q)$, we define their shifted **shuffle product** $\sigma \cshuffle \tau$ as the set of permutations $\upsilon$ of size $p+q$ such that $\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\ldots, \sigma(p)$ appear in this order in the word of $\upsilon$, and $\tau(1)+p,\tau(2)+p,\ldots,\tau(q)+p$ also appear in this order in the word of $\upsilon$.

::: example
If $\sigma=21$ and $\tau=231$, then $$\begin{aligned}
\sigma \cshuffle \tau&= 21 \shuffle 453\\
&\!\!\!=\{21453,24153,24513,24531,42153,42513,42531,45213,45231,45321\}.
\end{aligned}$$
:::

For all permutations $\sigma,\tau \in \mathfrak{S}$, by sorting the words appearing in the product $F_{\sigma}F_{\tau}$ according to their standardization, one sees readily that $F_{\sigma}F_{\tau}=\sum_{\upsilon \in \sigma\cshuffle\tau}F_{\upsilon}.$

Let us denote by $\mathbf{FQSym}$ the algebra generated by the $F_{\sigma}$'s. The ordered sum of ordered alphabets $X+Y$ defines a coproduct on $\mathbf{FQSym}$: $\Delta f(X,Y)=f(X+Y)$. Endowed with this new operation, $\mathbf{FQSym}$ becomes an Hopf algebra (see [@NCSF6 §3] and [@AS05]) which is neither commutative nor cocommutative. Moreover, $\mathbf{FQSym}$ is self-dual with respect to the pairing $$\left\langle F_{\sigma}\vphantom{G_{\tau}}\,\right |\left.G_{\tau} \vphantom{F_{\sigma}}\right\rangle=\delta_{\sigma\tau},$$ and in the basis $(F_{\sigma})_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}}$, the coproduct of $\mathbf{FQSym}$ can be written in terms of deconcatenation and standardization. More precisely, $$\Delta F_{\sigma}=\sum_{\sigma=\tau\cdot \nu}F_{\mathrm{Std}(\tau)}\otimes F_{\mathrm{Std}(\nu)}.$$

::: example
If $\sigma$ is in $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$, one will always obtain $n+1$ terms in $\Delta(F_{\sigma})$. For instance, $$\Delta(F_{4132})=1\otimes F_{4132}+F_{1}\otimes F_{132}+F_{21}\otimes F_{21}+F_{312}\otimes F_{1}+F_{4132}\otimes 1.$$
:::

If $\sigma\in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$, its **descents** are the $i$'s in $\left[\!\left[1,n-1\right]\!\right]$ such that $\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)$, and its **recoils** are the descents of $\sigma^{-1}$. For instance, if $\sigma=64182357$, then $$D(\sigma)=\{1,2,4\}\qquad;\qquad R(\sigma)=\{3,5,7\}.$$ To a subset $I \subset \left[\!\left[1,n-1\right]\!\right]$, one can associate bijectively a **composition** of size $n$, that is to say a sequence of positive integers $c=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r})$ such that $c_{1}+\cdots+c_{r}=n$. Indeed, such a sequence is entirely determined by the integers $$c_{1}<c_{1}+c_{2}<\cdots<c_{1}+\cdots+c_{r-1},$$ and these integers form an arbitrary subset of $\left[\!\left[1,n-1\right]\!\right]$. We will denote by $c(\sigma)$ the composition associated to the descent set of a permutation $\sigma$. It is easy to compute $c(\sigma)$ by writing down the **ribbon** of $\sigma$, which is the unique ribbon tableau with reading word $\sigma$ and increasing rows and columns.

::: example
The ribbon of $64182357$ is $$\young(6,4,18,:2357)\,.$$
:::

Then, the parts of $c(\sigma)$ are the lengths of the rows of the ribbon of $\sigma$. In the following, for any integer $n$, we will denote $\mathfrak{C}_{n}$ the set of compositions of size $n$, and if $c\in \mathfrak{C}_{n}$, we will denote $D(c)$ the associated subset of $\left[\!\left[1,n-1\right]\!\right]$ --- it is called the descent set of $c$.

The algebra of **quasi-symmetric functions** $\mathbf{QSym}$ (see for instance [@Ges84] and [@Stan91 §7.19]) is obtained from $\mathbf{FQSym}$ by making the variables $x_{i}$ commutative. If $X$ is now a commutative alphabet, then it is not hard to convince oneself that $G_{\sigma}$ and $G_{\tau}$ become equal if and only if $\sigma$ and $\tau$ have the same recoils. More precisely, under the morphism $\mathbb{C}\langle X \rangle \to \mathbb{C}[X]$, a free quasi-symmetric function $F_{\sigma}$ becomes $L_{c(\sigma)}$, where for any composition $c \in \mathfrak{C}_{n}$, $L_{c}$ is defined as $$L_{c}=\sum_{\substack{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq \cdots \leq i_{n}\\ i_{j}<i_{j+1}\text{ if }j \in D(c)}} x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}.$$ A formal power series $f$ in commutative variables $x_{1},x_{2},\ldots$ belongs to the vector space generated by the $L_{c}$'s, $c \in \mathfrak{C}=\bigsqcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{C}_{n}$ if and only if it is quasi-symmetric, meaning that for any $i_{1}< i_{2}< \cdots < i_{r}$ and $j_{1}< j_{2}< \cdots < j_{r}$, and any $(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in \mathbb{N}^{r}$, $$[x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{a_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}^{a_{r}}](f)=[x_{j_{1}}^{a_{1}}x_{j_{2}}^{a_{2}}\cdots x_{j_{r}}^{a_{r}}](f).$$ The reader should compare this definition of quasi-symmetric functions with the definition of symmetric functions given on page . The space $\mathbf{QSym}$ of quasi-symmetric functions is stable by product, and in fact, it is again an Hopf algebra; it contains $\mathbf{Sym}$ as a Hopf subalgebra, and it is the quotient of $\mathbf{FQSym}$ by the congruences $$F_{\sigma}\equiv F_{\tau} \quad\iff \quad c(\sigma)=c(\tau).$$ The product of two fundamental quasi-symmetric functions $L_{c}$ and $L_{d}$ is not in general multiplicity-free as in $\mathbf{FQSym}$[^1]; however, the formula for a coproduct $\Delta(L_{c})$ remains essentially the same as the one given in $\mathbf{FQSym}$ for $\Delta(F_{\sigma})$. More precisely, for $c\in \mathfrak{C}_{n}$ and $i,j \in \left[\!\left[0,n\right]\!\right]$, let us denote $c_{\left[\!\left[i+1,j\right]\!\right]}$ the composition of size $j-i$ that satisfies: $$D(c_{\left[\!\left[i+1,j\right]\!\right]})=\big(D(c)\cap \left[\!\left[i+1,j-1\right]\!\right]\big)-i.$$ In terms of ribbons, it amounts to cut the ribbon of $c$ before the box $i+1$ and after the box $j$.

::: example
If $c=(1,1,2,4)$ and $\left[\!\left[i+1,j\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[3,7\right]\!\right]$, then the ribbon of $c$ is $$\young(~,~,~~,:~~~~)$$ and it is then easy to see that $c_{\left[\!\left[3,7\right]\!\right]}=(2,3)$.
:::

The translation in $\mathbf{QSym}$ of the formula given previously for a coproduct $\Delta(F_{\sigma})$ is quite obviously: $$\Delta(L_{c})=\sum_{i=0}^{n} L_{c_{\left[\!\left[1,i\right]\!\right]}}\otimes L_{c_{\left[\!\left[i+1,n\right]\!\right]}}.$$

::: example
$$\Delta(L_{1,2,1})=L_{\emptyset}\otimes L_{1,2,1}+L_{1}\otimes L_{2,1}+L_{1,1}\otimes L_{1,1}+L_{1,2}\otimes L_{1}+L_{1,2,1}\otimes L_{\emptyset}.$$
:::

We shall also need the formula for the antipode of $\mathbf{QSym}$. If $c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})$ is a composition, let us denote by $c^{*}$ the composition whose descent set is $(c_{r},c_{r}+c_{r-1},\ldots,c_{r}+\cdots+c_{2})$; this is the reverse composition of $c$. Then, we define the conjugate $\overline{c}$ of $c$ by $D(\overline{c})=\left[\!\left[1,n-1\right]\!\right]\setminus D(c^{*})$. The operation $c \mapsto \overline{c}$ may seem complicate, but in terms of ribbons, it amounts simply to symmetrize $c$ with respect to the first diagonal.

::: example
The conjugate of $c=(1,1,2,4)$ has for ribbon $$\young(~,~,~,~~,:~~~)$$ so $\overline{c}=(1,1,1,2,3)$.
:::

Then, it can be shown that the antipode of $\mathbf{QSym}$ is up to sign equal to $\nu$, where $\nu(L_{c})=L_{\overline{c}}$ for any composition. In $\mathbf{FQSym}$, the antipode of is much more difficult to compute; see [@AS05 §5]. Fortunately, we shall only need the following property: $$\nu(F_{12\ldots n})=F_{n\ldots 21}$$ which is an immediate consequence[^2] of [@AS05 Theorem 5.4].

To conclude this paragraph, let us explain how the bases of $\mathbf{Sym}$, $\mathbf{QSym}$ and $\mathbf{FQSym}$ are related. If $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ is a permutation, the Robinson-Schensted-Knuth (RSK) algorithm ([@Rob38; @Sch61]) provides a pair of standard tableaux $(P(\sigma),Q(\sigma))$ of same shape $\Lambda(\sigma) \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$, and it is actually a bijection between $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ and $\bigsqcup_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}}\mathrm{ST}(\lambda)^{2}$. Given a permutation[^3] $\sigma=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}$, one constructs the first tableau $P(\sigma)$ as follows:

1.  One reads the word from left to right, and one inserts the letters successively in the standard tableau $P$, starting from $P=\emptyset$.

2.  When inserting the letter $a$, one compare $a$ to the entries of the first row. If $a$ is greater than all the entries of the first row, one adds $a$ at the end of the row. Otherwise, if $b$ is the first entry strictly greater than $a$, one replaces $b$ by $a$ in the first row, and one inserts $b$ in the next row (with the same rules).

The tableau $Q(\sigma)$ is constructed by recording the order of creation of the boxes of $P$. The correspondence $\sigma \mapsto (P(\sigma),Q(\sigma))$ has the following additional properties ([@Ful97]):

1.  For any permutation $\sigma$, $P(\sigma)=Q(\sigma^{-1})$.

2.  The parts of the common shape $\Lambda(\sigma)$ of $P(\sigma)$ and $Q(\sigma)$ are related to the so-called **Greene invariants** of the permutation (see *e.g.* [@LLT02]). Hence, for any $i \leq \ell(\Lambda(\sigma))$, $$\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}+\cdots+\Lambda_{i}=\max\{\ell(iw_{1})+\ell(iw_{2})+\cdots+\ell(iw_{i})\}$$ where the maximum is taken on the set of $i$-tuples of disjoint increasing subwords of $\sigma$. In particular, $\Lambda_{1}$ is the length $\ell(\sigma)$ of a longest increasing subword of $\sigma$. One has the same result if one replaces $\Lambda(\sigma)$ by its conjugate and the longest increasing subwords by the longest decreasing subwords.

3.  A descent of a standard tableau $T$ is an index $i$ such that $i+1$ appears in a row strictly above the one that contains $i$. This notion allows to associate to $T$ a subset of $\left[\!\left[1,n-1\right]\!\right]$, and therefore a composition $c(T)$. Then, for any permutation $\sigma$, $c(\sigma)=c(Q(\sigma))$.

::: example
If $\sigma=64182357$, the two corresponding tableaux are $$\young(6,48,12357)\quad\text{and}\quad\young(3,25,14678)\,.$$ The length of the first part is $5$, and it is indeed the length of a longest increasing subword, here, $12357$. The descents of $Q(\sigma)$ are $\{1,2,4\}$, and they are indeed the descents of $\sigma$.
:::

Let us denote $\pi: \mathbf{FQSym}\to \mathbf{QSym}$ the quotient map coming from the morphism $\mathbb{C}\langle X\rangle \to \mathbb{C}[X]$. For any $n$, notice that in $\mathbf{FQSym}$, $$(F_{1})^{n}=\sum_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}}F_{\sigma}.$$ We decompose this sum in blocks according to the shape $\Lambda(\sigma)$ of the two standard tableaux corresponding to $\sigma$: $$(F_{1})^{n}=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}} \left(\sum_{\sigma \,\,|\,\,\Lambda(\sigma)=\lambda}F_{\sigma}\right).$$ If one applies $\pi$ to the left-hand side of this expansion, one obtains $(L_{(1)})^{n}$. On the other hand, if one applies $\pi$ to a block $B_{\lambda}$ in the right hand side, one gets $$\pi(B_{\lambda})=\sum_{P,Q \in \mathrm{ST}(\lambda)}L_{c(Q)}=(\dim \lambda)\,\sum_{Q \in \mathrm{ST}(\lambda)}L_{c(Q)}.$$ But in fact, after applying $\pi$, both members of the equation are symmetric functions (not just *quasi-symmetric*), and the decomposition is simply the Frobenius formula for $(p_{1})^{n}$: $$(p_{1})^{n}=\sum_{\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}}(\dim \lambda)\,s_{\lambda}.$$ In particular, $s_{\lambda}=\sum_{Q \in \mathrm{ST}(\lambda)}L_{c(Q)}$, see [@Stan91 p. 361]. This simple fact allows to lift the coherent systems of measures to systems of probability measures on the symmetric groups $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$.

## Coherent systems as push-forwards of measures on the symmetric groups {#pushforward}

To do this, we will lift the specializations $A-B+\gamma E$ to specializations of $\mathbf{FQSym}$. First, remark that for any symmetric function $f$, one can write: $$f(A-B+\gamma E)= \Delta f(A-B,\gamma E)=[(\mathrm{id}\otimes \nu \otimes \mathrm{id})\circ (\Delta \otimes \mathrm{id})\circ \Delta]f(A,\overline{B},\gamma E).$$ Suppose that the specializations $A$, $\overline{B}$ and $\gamma E$ are lifted up to $\mathbf{QSym}$; this means that one has morphisms of algebras $A : \mathbf{QSym}\to \mathbb{C}$, $B : \mathbf{QSym}\to \mathbb{C}$ and $\gamma E : \mathbf{QSym}\to \mathbb{C}$, whose restrictions to $\mathbf{Sym}\subset \mathbf{QSym}$ are those defined in Theorem [\[thomatheorem\]](#thomatheorem){reference-type="ref" reference="thomatheorem"}. Then, since $\nu$ and $\Delta$ are morphisms of the commutative algebra $\mathbf{QSym}$, the formula above defines a specialization of $\mathbf{QSym}$ that is compatible with the specialization $A-B+\gamma E$ of $\mathbf{Sym}$. Moreover, by composition by the quotient map $\pi: \mathbf{FQSym}\to \mathbf{QSym}$, one gets a specialization of $\mathbf{FQSym}$. Hence, it is sufficient to define $f(A)$, $f(\overline{B})$ and $f(\gamma E)$ for any quasi-symmetric function $f$.

For the two first, exactly as in the case of $\mathbf{Sym}$, it amounts to replace the variables $X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}$ by either $A=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\}$ or $\overline{B}=\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots\}$. Notice that since $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i}\leq 1$, for any $f \in \mathbf{QSym}$, the series $f(A)$ and $f(\overline{B})$ converge absolutely. As for the exponential alphabet, in $\mathbf{FQSym}$ or $\mathbf{QSym}$, it is defined by the rule: $$F_{\sigma}(\gamma E)=L_{c(\sigma)}(\gamma E)=\frac{\gamma^{n}}{n!}.$$ Since a product $F_{\sigma}F_{\tau}$ involves $\binom{|\sigma|+|\tau|}{|\sigma|,|\tau|}$ terms, this rule gives indeed a morphism of algebras. So, the specialization $A-B+\gamma E$ is well-defined on $\mathbf{QSym}$ and $\mathbf{FQSym}$. Moreover, notice that for any permutation $\sigma$, $F_{\sigma}(A)$, $F_{\sigma}(\overline{B})$ and $F_{\sigma}(\gamma E)$ are non-negative. Since $\Delta$ and $\nu$ are positive operators on $\mathbf{QSym}$ with respect to the basis $(L_{c})_{c \in \mathfrak{C}}$, $$\forall \omega=(\alpha,\beta)\in \Omega,\,\,\,\forall \sigma \in \mathfrak{S},\,\,\,F_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)>0.$$ On the other hand, since $F_{1}(A-B+\gamma E)=p_{1}(A-B+\gamma E)=1$, $$\sum_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}}G_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)=\sum_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}}F_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)=(F_{1}(A-B+\gamma E))^{n}=1.$$ Thus, by using the Hopf algebras $\mathbf{QSym}$ and $\mathbf{FQSym}$, for any point $\omega$ of the Thoma simplex, one can define a (positive) probability measure $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}$ on the symmetric group $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ corresponding *via* RSK to the coherent measure $\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega}$:

::: proposition
[]{#frompermtopar label="frompermtopar"} For any $\omega \in \Omega$, and any permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}$, set $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=G_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)$.

1.  For any $n$, $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}$ is a probability measure on $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$, and for any partition $\lambda$, $$\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega}[\lambda]=\Lambda_{*}\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\lambda]=\sum_{\sigma\,\,|\,\,\Lambda(\sigma)=\lambda}\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma].$$

2.  If $c$ is the composition of size $n$ encoding the recoils of $\sigma$, then $$\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=\sum_{i+j+k=n}\frac{\gamma^{k}}{k!}\,L_{c_{\left[\!\left[1,i\right]\!\right]}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots)\,L_{\overline{c_{\left[\!\left[i+1,i+j\right]\!\right]}}}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots).$$
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* We have seen just before that $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=G_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)$ defines a probability measure on $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$. Now, for any partition $\lambda \in \mathfrak{Y}_{n}$, we can write: $$\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\Lambda(\sigma)=\lambda}\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]&=\sum_{\Lambda(\sigma)=\lambda} L_{c(\sigma^{-1})}(A-B+\gamma E)=\sum_{P,Q \in \mathrm{ST}(\lambda)}L_{c(P)}(A-B+\gamma E)\\
&=(\dim \lambda)\,\,s_{\lambda}(A-B+\gamma E)=\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega}[\lambda].
\end{aligned}$$ Finally, the specialization $A-B+\gamma E$ of $\mathbf{FQSym}$ factors through the quotient map $\pi : \mathbf{FQSym}\to \mathbf{QSym}$, and in this latter algebra, it is not difficult to compute it: $$\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]&=G_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)=L_{c(\sigma^{-1})}(A-B+\gamma E)\\
&=[(\mathrm{id}\otimes \nu \otimes \mathrm{id})\circ (\Delta \otimes \mathrm{id})\circ \Delta]L_{c}(A,\overline{B},\gamma E)\\
&=\sum_{i+j+k=n} L_{c_{\left[\!\left[1,i\right]\!\right]}}(A)\,(\nu L_{c_{\left[\!\left[i+1,i+j\right]\!\right]}})(\overline{B})\,L_{c_{\left[\!\left[i+j+1,n\right]\!\right]}}(\gamma E)\\
&=\sum_{i+j+k=n}L_{c_{\left[\!\left[1,i\right]\!\right]}}(\alpha)\,L_{\overline{c_{\left[\!\left[i+1,i+j\right]\!\right]}}}(\beta)\,\frac{\gamma^{k}}{k!}.
\end{aligned}$$ ◻
:::

::: example
If $\omega=((0,0,\ldots),(0,0,\ldots))$, then $\gamma =1$ and $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=\frac{1}{n!}$ for any permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ (in the triple sum, the only non-zero term corresponds to $i=j=0$ and $k=n$). Thus, one obtains the uniform measures.

Similarly, suppose that $\omega=((1-\gamma,0,0,\ldots),(0,0,\ldots))$ with $\gamma >0$. Then, $L_{c_{\left[\!\left[1,i\right]\!\right]}}(\alpha)$ equals $0$ unless $c_{\left[\!\left[1,i\right]\!\right]}$ has no descents, which is equivalent to ask that the first recoil of $\sigma$ is after $i$. So, if $\mathrm{FR}(\sigma)$ denotes this first recoil, then $$\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=\sum_{i=0}^{\mathrm{FR}(\sigma)}\frac{(1-\gamma)^{i}\,\gamma^{n-i}}{n-i!}.$$

Suppose now that $\omega=((1/d,1/d,\ldots,1/d,0,0,\ldots),(0,0,\ldots))$, with $d$ terms $1/d$ in $\alpha$. Then, $$\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=L_{c(\sigma)^{-1}}(\alpha)=\!\!\!\!\sum_{\substack{i_{1}\leq i_{2} \leq \ldots\leq i_{n} \leq d\\
i_{j}<i_{j+1}\text{ if }j \in D(c(\sigma^{-1}))}}\!\! \frac{1}{d^{n}}=\frac{1}{d^{n}}\,\binom{n-1+d-\mathrm{card}\,R(\sigma)}{n}.$$ For the two last examples, exchanging $\alpha$ and $\beta$ amounts to replace $\sigma$ by $\sigma\omega_{0}$, where $\omega_{0}=(n,n-1,\ldots,1)$.

Finally, suppose that $\omega=(((1-q),(1-q)q,(1-q)q^{2},\ldots),(0,0,\ldots))$. Then, $$\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=L_{c(\sigma)^{-1}}(\alpha)=(1-q)^{n}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\substack{i_{1} \leq \ldots\leq i_{n} \leq d\\
i_{j}<i_{j+1}\text{ if }j \in D(c(\sigma^{-1}))}}\!\!\!\!\! q^{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}-n}=\frac{q^{\mathrm{comaj}(\sigma^{-1})}}{\{n!\}_{q}},$$ where $\mathrm{comaj}(\sigma)=\sum_{d \in D(\sigma)}n-d$ and $\{n!\}_{q}=\prod_{i=1}^{n }\frac{1-q^{i}}{1-q}.$
:::

## Link with the generalized riffle shuffles {#riffleshuffle}

A remarkable fact is that the probability measures $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}$ defined in the previous paragraph can be described practically by random **generalized riffle shuffles**; see [@Ful02]. We fix a point $\omega=(\alpha,\beta)$ of the Thoma simplex and an integer $n$, and we consider a deck of cards ordered from $1$ to $n$. We then construct a random permutation $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ by using the following algorithm:

1.  First, we split the deck in blocks of size $n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+l=n$, the sizes of the blocks being chosen randomly according to a multinomial law of parameter $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$: $$\mathbb{P}[n_{1},\ldots,m_{1},\ldots,l]=\binom{n}{n_{1},\ldots,m_{1},\ldots,l}\,\left(\prod_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}^{n_{i}}\right)\,\left(\prod_{j\geq 1}\beta_{j}^{m_{j}}\right)\,\gamma^{l}.$$

2.  In the blocks of sizes $m_{1},m_{2},\ldots$, we reverse the order of the cards. In the block of size $l$, we randomize the order of the cards, so that every permutation of the $l$ last cards becomes equiprobable.

3.  Finally, we randomly shuffle the blocks with the following rule. If two blocks of size $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ are shuffled together, then the first card of the new deck comes from the top of the first block with probability $\frac{k_{1}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}$, and from the top of the second block with probability $\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}$; then we start again with the remaining $k_{1}+k_{2}-1$ cards. Notice that this recursive rule is equivalent to ask that all the $\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1},k_{2}}$ shuffles of the blocks are equiprobable.

This algorithm is called in [@Ful02] an $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$-shuffle.

::: proposition
[]{#apocalyptica label="apocalyptica"} The probability of a permutation $\sigma$ obtained by the $\omega$-shuffle presented above is exactly $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]$.
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Let us give a purely algebraic proof. If $X$ is a formal alphabet, we denote by $\phi_{X} : \mathbf{FQSym}\to \mathbb{C}$ the corresponding specialization, and by $u_{X}$ and $t_{X}$ the maps $$\begin{aligned}
u_{X}:\mathbf{FQSym}&\to \mathbf{FQSym}\qquad\qquad;\qquad\qquad t_{X}:\mathbf{FQSym}\to \mathbf{FQSym}\\
F_{\sigma} &\mapsto F_{\sigma}(X)\, F_{12\ldots n}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\,\qquad F_{\sigma}\mapsto F_{\sigma}(X)\,F_{\sigma}.
\end{aligned}$$ Notice that if $X=(\zeta)$ is a single variable, then $u_{(\zeta)}=t_{(\zeta)}$. We start from the identity $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]=G_{\sigma}(A-B+\gamma E)$, and we expand it: $$\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]&=\phi_{A-B+\gamma E}(G_{\sigma})=m^{3}\circ (\phi_{A}\otimes \phi_{-B}\otimes \phi_{\gamma E})\circ \Delta^{3} (G_{\sigma})\\
&=m^{3}\circ (\phi_{A}\otimes \phi_{\overline{B}}\otimes \phi_{\gamma E})\circ(1\otimes \nu \otimes 1)\circ \Delta^{3} (G_{\sigma})
\end{aligned}$$ The operator applied to $G_{\sigma}$ can be further expanded in $$m^{2\infty +1}\circ \left(\left(\otimes_{i}\,\phi_{(\alpha_{i})}\right) \otimes \left(\otimes_{j}\,\phi_{(\beta_{j})}\right) \otimes \phi_{\gamma E}\right)\circ\left(1^{\otimes \infty}\otimes \nu^{\otimes \infty} \otimes 1\right)\circ \Delta^{2\infty +1}$$ where the meaning of the symbols $2\infty+1$ is quite clear from what precedes. Let us denote $I^{2\infty+1} = 1^{\otimes \infty}\otimes \nu^{\otimes \infty}\otimes 1$. Then, $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}[\sigma]$ can be rewritten as: $$\begin{aligned}
&\left\langle \Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})\vphantom{\left(\left(\otimes_{i}\,u_{(\alpha_{i})}\right) \otimes \left(\otimes_{j}\,u_{(\beta_{j})}\right) \otimes u_{\gamma E}\right)\circ I^{2\infty+1} \circ \Delta^{2\infty +1} (G_{\sigma})}\,\right |\left.\left(\left(\otimes_{i}\,u_{(\alpha_{i})}\right) \otimes \left(\otimes_{j}\,u_{(\beta_{j})}\right) \otimes u_{\gamma E}\right)\circ I^{2\infty+1} \circ \Delta^{2\infty +1} (G_{\sigma}) \vphantom{\Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})}\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle \Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})\vphantom{\big(\big(\!\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}\big) \otimes \big(\!\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}\big) \otimes u_{\gamma E}\big)\circ I^{2\infty+1} \!\circ \Delta^{2\infty +1} (G_{\sigma})}\,\right |\left.\big(\big(\!\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}\big) \otimes \big(\!\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}\big) \otimes u_{\gamma E}\big)\circ I^{2\infty+1} \!\circ \Delta^{2\infty +1} (G_{\sigma}) \vphantom{\Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})}\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle m^{2\infty+1} \circ I^{2\infty+1} \circ \! \big(\big(\!\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}^{*}\big) \otimes \big(\!\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}^{*}\big) \otimes u_{\gamma E}^{*}\big) \! \circ \Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})\vphantom{G_{\sigma}}\,\right |\left.G_{\sigma} \vphantom{m^{2\infty+1} \circ I^{2\infty+1} \circ \! \big(\big(\!\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}^{*}\big) \otimes \big(\!\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}^{*}\big) \otimes u_{\gamma E}^{*}\big) \! \circ \Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})}\right\rangle
\end{aligned}$$ Here, we have used extensively the property of self-adjointness of $\mathbf{FQSym}$. Now, we have to check that the operator applied to $F_{12\ldots n}$ encodes the random steps of the $\omega$-shuffle. We describe a probability distribution $\mathbb{P}$ on $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ by an element $\sum_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}}\mathbb{P}[\sigma]\,F_{\sigma} \in \mathbf{FQSym}$, and the effect of our random steps by matrices $$M=\sum_{\sigma,\tau}c_{\sigma\tau}\,F_{\sigma}\otimes G_{\tau},$$ where the $G$'s are considered as elements of the dual space of $\mathbf{FQSym}$. We also introduce the operator of uniformization $$U=\sum_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\sum_{\sigma\in \mathfrak{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\,F_{\sigma}\otimes G_{12\ldots n}.$$ Notice that for any $n$, $$u_{\gamma E}^{*}(F_{12\ldots n})=\frac{\gamma^{n}}{n!}\,F_{12\ldots n}=(U \circ t_{(\gamma)})(F_{12\ldots n})\qquad;\qquad F_{12\ldots n}=G_{12\ldots n}.$$ For this reason, we can rewrite the probability $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}$ as $$\left\langle m^{2\infty+1} \circ \widetilde{I}^{2\infty+1} \circ \big(\big(\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}\big) \otimes \big(\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}\big) \otimes t_{(\gamma)}\big) \circ \Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})\vphantom{G_{\sigma}}\,\right |\left.G_{\sigma} \vphantom{m^{2\infty+1} \circ \widetilde{I}^{2\infty+1} \circ \big(\big(\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}\big) \otimes \big(\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}\big) \otimes t_{(\gamma)}\big) \circ \Delta^{2 \infty +1}(F_{12\ldots n})}\right\rangle,$$ where $\widetilde{I}^{2\infty+1}=1^{\otimes \infty}\otimes \nu^{\otimes \infty} \otimes U$.

1.  Starting from the state $F_{12\ldots n}$, the first step of our algorithm can be written $$S_{1}=\left(\left(\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}\right) \otimes \left(\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}\right) \otimes t_{(\gamma)}\right) \circ t_{E}^{\otimes 2\infty+1} \circ \Delta^{2\infty+1} \circ t_{E^{-1}}.$$ Indeed, $\Delta^{2\infty+1}$ enables us to cut the deck in every possible way $$n=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+l,$$ and the other operators take account of the probabilities (we use the $t_{(\zeta)}$'s for the powers and the $t_{E}$'s for the factorials).

2.  The second step is obviously $S_{2}=\widetilde{I}^{2\infty+1}=1^{\otimes \infty}\otimes \nu^{\otimes \infty} \otimes U$. Here, we use the aforementioned fact that the involution $\nu$ reverses the term $F_{12\ldots n}$ in $F_{n(n-1)\ldots 1}$.

3.  Finally, since all the shuffles are equiprobable, by the very definition of the multiplication in $\mathbf{FQSym}$, the third step amounts to apply $$S_{3}=t_{E}\circ m^{2\infty +1} \circ t_{E^{-1}}^{\otimes 2\infty +1}.$$

Finally, in $S_{3}\circ S_{2}\circ S_{1}$, an operator $t_{E}$ commutes with every degree-preserving operator, so one can simplify them. One gets therefore $$S_{3}\circ S_{2}\circ S_{1}=m^{2\infty+1} \circ \widetilde{I}^{2\infty+1} \circ \big(\big(\otimes_{i}\,t_{(\alpha_{i})}\big) \otimes \big(\otimes_{j}\,t_{(\beta_{j})}\big) \otimes t_{(\gamma)}\big) \circ \Delta^{2 \infty +1}$$ and the proof is complete. ◻
:::

Hence, any point $\omega$ of the Thoma simplex $\Omega$ corresponds to:

1.  an irreducible character $\chi^{\omega}$ of $\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}$ and a coherent system of probability measures $(\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega})_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ on the levels $\mathfrak{Y}_{n}$ of the Young graph;

2.  a system of measures $(\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega})_{n\in \mathbb{N}}$ on the symmetric groups $\mathfrak{S}_{n}$ such that $\Lambda_{*}\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}=\mathbb{P}_{n,\omega}$;

3.  and a random algorithm of shuffling that produces permutations $\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_{n}$ with distribution $\mathbb{Q}_{n,\omega}$.
