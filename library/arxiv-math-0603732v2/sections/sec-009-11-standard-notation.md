Let $k$ be a commutative base field, arbitrary for the moment. When we are working with quantum groups it will sometimes be necessary to assume that $k$ contains ${\mathbb C}(q)$. Unless stated otherwise all vector spaces are over $k$, and an unadorned $\otimes$ will mean $\otimes_k$. An algebra or a ring always means a $k$-algebra with associative multiplication $m_A$ and with unit 1. Every algebra homomorphism is a unitary $k$-algebra homomorphism. All $A$-modules will be by default left modules. Let $A^{\sf op}$ denote the opposite algebra of $A$ and let $A^e$ denote the enveloping algebra $A\otimes A^{\sf op}$. The category of left \[resp. right\]

$A$-modules is denoted by $A$-$\mathop{\mathrm{{\sf Mod}}}$ \[resp. $A^{\sf op}$-$\mathop{\mathrm{{\sf Mod}}}$\]. So an $A^e$-module - that is, an object of $A^e$-$\mathop{\mathrm{{\sf Mod}}}$ - is the same as an $A$-bimodule central over $k$.

When $A$ is a Hopf algebra we shall use the symbols $\Delta$, $\epsilon$ and $S$ respectively for its coproduct, counit and antipode. The coproduct of $a \in A$ will be denoted by $\Delta(a)=\sum a_1 \otimes a_2$. For details concerning the above terminology, see for example [@Mo Chapter 1]. In many places we assume that $$\begin{aligned}
\qquad \textit{the antipode $S$ of the Hopf algebra $A$ is
bijective.}\label{antipode}
\end{aligned}$$

Some of the results in this paper are valid more generally, but the bijectivity of $S$ is satisfied by all the examples which will concern us. Recently Skryabin proved the following.

::: proposition
[@Sk] Let $A$ be a noetherian Hopf algebra. If one of the following hold, then $S$ is bijective.

1.  $A$ is semiprime.

2.  $A$ is affine PI.
:::

Thus ([\[antipode\]](#antipode){reference-type="ref" reference="antipode"}) is a reasonable condition and may be satisfied by all noetherian Hopf algebras.
