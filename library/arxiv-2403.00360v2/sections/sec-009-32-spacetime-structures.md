A causal fermion system describes a spacetime together with all structures therein. The structures are the causal and metric structures, spinors and interacting fields (for details see [@cfs Chapter 1]). All these spacetime structures are *inherent* in the sense that they use information already encoded in the causal fermion system. We now give a brief outline of the spacetime structures, with a focus on those structures which will be needed later on.

Let $\rho$ be a *minimizing* measure. *Spacetime* is defined as the support of this measure, $$M := \mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\rho \;\subset\; {\mathscr{F}}\:,$$ where on $M$ we consider the *topology* induced by ${\mathscr{F}}$ (generated by the operator norm on $\text{\rm{L}}(\mathscr{H})$). Thus the spacetime points are symmetric linear operators on $\mathscr{H}$. The restriction of the measure $\rho|_M$ gives a volume measure on spacetime.

We begin with the following notion of causality:

::: Def
(causal structure) []{#def2 label="def2"} * For any $x, y \in {\mathscr{F}}$, we again denote the non-trivial eigenvalues of the operator product $xy$ (again counting algebraic multiplicities) by $\lambda^{xy}_1, \ldots, \lambda^{xy}_{2n}$. The points $x$ and $y$ are called *spacelike* separated if all the $\lambda^{xy}_j$ have the same absolute value. They are said to be *timelike* separated if the $\lambda^{xy}_j$ are all real and do not all have the same absolute value. In all other cases (i.e. if the $\lambda^{xy}_j$ are not all real and do not all have the same absolute value), the points $x$ and $y$ are said to be *lightlike* separated. *
:::

Restricting the causal structure of ${\mathscr{F}}$ to $M$, we get causal relations in spacetime.

The Lagrangian [\[Lagrange\]](#Lagrange){reference-type="eqref" reference="Lagrange"} is compatible with the above notion of causality in the following sense. Suppose that two points $x, y \in M$ are spacelike separated. Then the eigenvalues $\lambda^{xy}_i$ all have the same absolute value. As a consequence, the Lagrangian [\[Lagrange\]](#Lagrange){reference-type="eqref" reference="Lagrange"} vanishes. Thus pairs of points with spacelike separation do not enter the action. This can be seen in analogy to the usual notion of causality where points with spacelike separation cannot influence each other. This is the reason for the notion "causal" in *causal* fermion system and *causal* action principle.

Next, a causal fermion system encodes *spinorial wave functions*. To this end, for every spacetime point $x \in M$ we define the *spin space* $S_x$ by $S_x = x(\mathscr{H})$; it is a subspace of $\mathscr{H}$ of dimension at most $2n$. It is endowed with the *spin inner product* $\mathopen{\prec}.|. \mathclose{\succ}_x$ defined by $$\label{ssp}
\mathopen{\prec}u | v \mathclose{\succ}_x = -\langle u | x v \rangle_\mathscr{H}\qquad \text{(for all~$u,v \in S_x$)}\:.$$ A *wave function* $\psi$ is defined as a function which to every $x \in M$ associates a vector of the corresponding spin space, $$\psi \::\: M \rightarrow \mathscr{H}\qquad \text{with} \qquad \psi(x) \in S_x \quad \text{for all~$x \in M$}\:.$$ It is an important observation that every vector $u \in \mathscr{H}$ of the Hilbert space gives rise to a distinguished wave function. In order to obtain this wave function, denoted by $\psi^u$, we simply project the vector $u$ to the corresponding spin spaces, $$\psi^u \::\: M \rightarrow \mathscr{H}\:,\qquad \psi^u(x) = \pi_x u \in S_x \:.$$ We refer to $\psi^u$ as the *physical wave function* of $u \in \mathscr{H}$. Another object which will be important in what follows is the *kernel of the fermionic projector* defined by $$\label{Pxyabs}
P(x,y) = \pi_x \,y|_{S_y} \::\: S_y \rightarrow S_x \:,$$ where $\pi_x : \mathscr{H}\rightarrow S_x$ is the orthogonal projection to the spin space $S_x$. The kernel of the fermionic projector can be expressed in terms of the physical wave functions. Indeed, choosing an orthonormal basis $(b_i)$ of $\mathscr{H}$ (for details see [@cfs Chapter 1] or [@intro Section 5.6]) $$\label{Pxyphysical}
P(x,y) \, \phi = -\sum_i \psi^{b_i}(x) \: \mathopen{\prec}\psi^{b_i}(y) |\; \phi \mathclose{\succ}_y \:.$$

The kernel of the fermionic projector plays a central role in the theory of causal fermion systems for two reasons:

-   The causal action principle can be formulated in terms of $P(x,y)$. In order to see this, we note that the trace in the trace constraint [\[trconstraint\]](#trconstraint){reference-type="eqref" reference="trconstraint"} can be written as $\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(x) = \mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}_{S_x}(P(x,x))$. Moreover, the eigenvalues $\lambda^{xy}_j$ of the operator $xy$ (which appear in [\[Lagrange\]](#Lagrange){reference-type="eqref" reference="Lagrange"} and [\[Sdef\]](#Sdef){reference-type="eqref" reference="Sdef"}) coincide with the eigenvalues of the so-called closed chain $A_{xy} := P(x,y)\, P(y,x) : S_x \rightarrow S_x$. Details can be found in [@cfs Section 1.1] and [@intro Section 5.6].

-   According to [\[Pxyabs\]](#Pxyabs){reference-type="eqref" reference="Pxyabs"}, the kernel of the fermionic projector $P(x,y)$ is a mapping from one space $S_y$ to another spin space $S_x$. In this way, it gives relations between the spacetime points. As will be explained in more detail in Section [\[secqg\]](#secqg){reference-type="ref" reference="secqg"}, these relations give rise to *geometric structures* like connection and curvature.

Finally, additional structures like the *metric* and *bosonic fields* arise when describing the dynamics of the causal fermion system, as will be outlined in the next sections.
