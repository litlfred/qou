          ⚠ Non-vacuity. mvGenWronskian_eq_zero_of_dependent is used through its contrapositive, which certifies nothing if every generalized Wronskian vanishes identically. It does not: against the operators (id, ∂/∂x₀) the family (1, x₀) gives the identity matrix.

          Towards the converse: the coefficient minor #

          The converse asks for an operator family witnessing independence. The linear algebra under it does not mention derivatives at all: a family of polynomials is independent over the base field exactly when some m × m minor of its coefficient matrix is non-singular. That is proved here in full; what remains to reach the Wronskian converse is the bridge from coefficient functionals to differential operators, which in characteristic zero is a diagonal rescaling by factorials.

              theorem MvPolynomial.exists_mvGenWronskian_ne_zero {σ : Type u_1} {K : Type u_3} [Field K] [CharZero K] [DecidableEq σ] {m : ℕ} (f : Fin m → MvPolynomial σ K) (hf : LinearIndependent K f) :
              ∃ (D : Fin m → List σ), mvGenWronskian D f ≠ 0

              An independent family has a non-zero generalized Wronskian.

              The Wronskian is non-zero because it is non-zero at the origin. Independence gives a non-singular coefficient minor at multi-indices α; realise each α i as a list of variables to get the operator family; then constantCoeff is a ring hom, so it commutes with the determinant, and the resulting scalar matrix is diagonal c * coeffMatrix α f.

              ⚠ Where characteristic zero enters, and why it cannot be avoided here. The c i are positive integers — products of exponents thrown off by coeff_pderiv. Their product must be non-zero in K, which is exactly CharZero. Over 𝔽ₚ the same construction can produce a genuinely zero determinant from an independent family, which is the multivariate face of the failure that makes the univariate converse false in characteristic p.

              ⚠ The bounded-order gap, witnessed #

              Roth's lemma needs the operator family to have bounded order — Δᵢ of order below i. The family produced by exists_mvGenWronskian_ne_zero carries no such bound, and the two computations below show that this is a limitation of the proof technique, not a slack statement: bounded-order families do suffice, but the origin argument cannot find them.

              For x⁵, x⁶ the order-one family (id, ∂/∂x₀) gives a non-zero Wronskian — yet that Wronskian vanishes at the origin. Since the proof above concludes "non-zero" only from "non-zero at the origin", it is structurally incapable of returning this family; it must reach for operators of order at least 5, enough to see the x⁵ coefficient.

              So closing the gap requires a different argument, not a sharpening of this one.

              Next #

              Both directions of the generalized-Wronskian criterion are proved, so the independence certificate is complete except for the order bound.

              The bounded-order criterion — f independent over K implies some generalized Wronskian with ord (Δᵢ) < i is non-zero — is what Roth's lemma actually consumes, and it is open here. mvGenWronskian_pow_five_six above shows the bound is achievable; the companion computation shows the origin argument cannot achieve it.

              The classical route does not go through coefficients at all. It restricts to a generic line: for a generic base point and direction, the restrictions t ↦ fⱼ(a + tc) stay independent over K, the univariate criterion (QOU.Mathlib.GenWronskian's genWronskian_eq_zero_iff) applies to them, and dⁿ/dtⁿ pulls back to Dᶜ = ∑ᵢ cᵢ ∂ᵢ — an operator of order exactly n. Expanding det (Dᶜ^{i-1} fⱼ) by multilinearity then exhibits one bounded-order generalized Wronskian that is non-zero.

              So the univariate criterion already proved is the engine; what is missing is the genericity argument (a non-zero polynomial over an infinite field has a point where it does not vanish) and the multilinear expansion.

              Then Roth's lemma itself — the induction on the number of variables — and Roth's theorem.
