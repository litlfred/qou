    QOU.Mathlib.SchenstedReverseCascade — undoing the insertion cascade (RSK inverse, brick 2) #

    Brick 1 (SchenstedReverseRow) inverted Schensted insertion on a single row. This file lifts that to the cascade: insert T x drops x into row 0, bumps down the rows, and finally appends a cell in exactly one row; uninsert T i removes the last cell of row i and pushes the displaced value back up through rows i-1, …, 0, returning what falls out of the top.

    Two definitions and one theorem:

    • newRowIndex T x — which row insert T x appends its new cell to. Defined by the same recursion as insert, so it is not a search: it just records where the recursion stopped.
    • uninsert T i — the reverse cascade at row i.
    • uninsert_insert — the inversion law: uninsert (insert T x) (newRowIndex T x) = (T, some x) for a row-sorted tableau with no empty rows.

    Why the two side conditions #

    RowsSorted T is what brick 1's rowUninsert_rowInsert needs on each row it undoes. The no-empty-rows condition is what lets uninsert decide, at the target row, between shortening a row and deleting it: a row that had one cell and loses it must go, or the tableau grows a spurious empty row and the shapes stop matching. Both are preserved by insert (insert_rowsSorted upstream, insert_noEmptyRows here), so the cascade composes.

    Scope #

    One insertion step, undone. Iterating this to invert insertWordRec — and thence the RSK bijection and RSKBurnsideHolds — is brick 3 and beyond (epic qou-nrex). Nothing here claims them.

    Pure combinatorics on ℕ; nothing archimedean.

          The cascade inversion law. Undoing insert T x at the row where it put its new cell recovers T and reports x.

          The recursion follows insert's own: at the appending row, brick 1's no-bump case says the row is old ++ [x], so dropLast / getLast? recover it; at a bumping row, brick 1's inversion law (rowUninsert_rowInsert) undoes the bump and the recursion supplies the value to push up.
