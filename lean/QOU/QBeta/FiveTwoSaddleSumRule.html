    Algebraic skeleton (generic commutative ring, §7c) #

    source
    theorem QOU.QBeta.FiveTwo.cubic_iff_sq_eq_cube {R : Type u_1} [CommRing R] (y : R) :
    y ^ 3 - 2 * y ^ 2 + 3 * y - 1 = 0 ↔ y ^ 2 = (1 - y) ^ 3

    The 5₂ saddle cubic is the branch equation y² = (1−y)³.

    Both forms appear in the sources — GGM eq (233) at x = 1 writes y² = (1−y)³, GGMW eq (184) writes (1−ξ)³ = ξ², and the corpus carries y³ − 2y² + 3y − 1. They are the same equation, over any commutative ring: no positivity, ordering, or archimedean structure is used.

    The branch data is a THEOREM, not an assumption #

    sum_reflection_of_branch below takes the branch relation 2·log yⱼ − 3·log(1−yⱼ) = 2πi·εⱼ as a hypothesis. It need not be assumed: it follows from the cubic alone, with no dilogarithm involved.

    source
    theorem QOU.QBeta.FiveTwo.exp_branch_eq_one {y : ℂ} (hy : y ≠ 0) (hy1 : 1 - y ≠ 0) (h : y ^ 2 = (1 - y) ^ 3) :
    Complex.exp (2 * Complex.log y - 3 * Complex.log (1 - y)) = 1

    exp (2·log y − 3·log(1−y)) = 1 whenever y² = (1−y)³.

    The exponential collapses the logarithms back to y²/(1−y)³, which the cubic makes 1. No branch choice is made here — that is the point.

    source
    theorem QOU.QBeta.FiveTwo.branch_data_of_cubic {y : ℂ} (hy : y ≠ 0) (hy1 : 1 - y ≠ 0) (h : y ^ 2 = (1 - y) ^ 3) :
    ∃ (k : ℤ), 2 * Complex.log y - 3 * Complex.log (1 - y) = ↑k * (2 * ↑Real.pi * Complex.I)

    The branch data of the 5₂ cubic, derived.

    Every root of y² = (1−y)³ satisfies 2·log y − 3·log(1−y) = k·(2πi) for some integer k. Numerically k = (+1, −1, 0) at the three 5₂ roots, and this is the sole source of the π² in the sum rule.

    This discharges the hbranch hypothesis of sum_reflection_of_branch from the cubic alone — no dilogarithm, no analytic input.

    The reflection reduction #

    The analytic content of the partial derivation. Li is abstract: the only thing assumed about it is the reflection identity, so this theorem is exactly as strong as that identity and no stronger.

    source
    theorem QOU.QBeta.FiveTwo.sum_reflection_of_branch (Li : ℂ → ℂ) (y ε : Fin 3 → ℂ) (hrefl : ∀ (z : ℂ), Li z + Li (1 - z) = ↑Real.pi ^ 2 / 6 - Complex.log z * Complex.log (1 - z)) (hbranch : ∀ (j : Fin 3), 2 * Complex.log (y j) - 3 * Complex.log (1 - y j) = 2 * ↑Real.pi * Complex.I * ε j) :
    ∑ j : Fin 3, Li (y j) + ∑ j : Fin 3, Li (1 - y j) = ↑Real.pi ^ 2 / 2 - 2 / 3 * ∑ j : Fin 3, Complex.log (y j) ^ 2 + 2 * ↑Real.pi * Complex.I / 3 * ∑ j : Fin 3, ε j * Complex.log (y j)

    Summing the dilogarithm reflection identity over the three roots.

    Given the reflection identity Li z + Li (1−z) = π²/6 − log z · log(1−z) and the branch data of the cubic — 2·log yⱼ − 3·log(1−yⱼ) = 2πi·εⱼ, which is what y² = (1−y)³ gives after taking logarithms — the Li values at 1−yⱼ are eliminated in favour of log-squares:

    Σ Li(yⱼ) + Σ Li(1−yⱼ)
  = π²/2 − (2/3)·Σ(log yⱼ)² + (2πi/3)·Σ εⱼ·log yⱼ

    This is one equation in the two unknowns Σ Li(yⱼ) and Σ Li(1−yⱼ). It does not close the sum rule; see FiveTwoDilogLadder.

    Bookkeeping consequences of the sum rule #

    Easy algebra — recorded because they are the two claims the prose makes, and a claim worth asserting is worth checking.

        The branch data b = (−1, +1, +1) is not free data #

        The sum rule is stated with Vⱼ = 3·Li₂(yⱼ) + (log yⱼ + bⱼπi)² and a hand-supplied b = (−1, +1, +1), which reads like three fitted signs. It is not: probe qbeta_5two_route5_branch_data_probe (gate G6, error 0.0 at 60 dps) found

        log yⱼ + bⱼπi = Log(−yⱼ)      (principal branch)

        and the three lemmas below are the proof. arg(−z) = arg z − π above the real axis, arg z + π below it, and = π on the positive real axis — so for a cubic with one positive real root and one conjugate pair, b is forced to be (−1, +1, +1) with no freedom whatsoever.

        Consequence: Vⱼ = 3·Li₂(yⱼ) + Log(−yⱼ)², and Log(−z)² is precisely the term in the dilogarithm inversion formula Li₂ z + Li₂ (1/z) = −π²/6 − ½·Log(−z)². That is what makes the inversion route to the sum rule natural rather than opportunistic. See bean qou-0yqu.

        The Log² half of the sum rule, in closed form #

        With b identified as Log(−yⱼ) the sum rule reads Σⱼ Re(3·Li₂(yⱼ) + Log(−yⱼ)²) = −3π²/2, which splits into a dilogarithm part and a pure Log² part. The Log² part is elementary — it is what this block evaluates — so the whole content of the sum rule sits in the dilogarithm part.

        For a positive real root y₃ and a conjugate pair y₁, y₂ = conj y₁ with ‖y₁‖² · y₃ = 1 (Vieta e₃ = 1, see real_root_mul_normSq):

        Σⱼ Re Log(−yⱼ)² = (3/2)·log²y₃ − 2(θ − π)² − π²,     θ = arg y₁.

        Subtracting from −3π²/2 gives the Route 5 closed form for the integral,

        −∫₀¹ log(1 − 2z + 3z² − z³)/z dz = −½·log²y₃ + (2/3)(θ − π)² − π²/6

        exactly as recorded in bean qou-0yqu. So Route 5's restatement is a consequence of the sum rule and this algebra — the two are equivalent given Σⱼ Re Li₂(yⱼ) = −∫₀¹ log P(z)/z dz, which is the one link still open.

        The real root's contribution is a ROGERS dilogarithm #

        Zagier's The Dilogarithm Function §II.1 A defines the Rogers dilogarithm on (0,1) as

        L(x) = Li₂(x) + ½·log(x)·log(1 − x)

        which takes its values most naturally in ℝ/(π²/2)ℤ. The 5₂ saddle's real root contributes to the sum rule exactly

        Re V₃ = 3·L(y₃) − π²

        — verified to 40 dps with difference 0.0, and proved below. The whole content is the branch relation 2·log y₃ = 3·log(1 − y₃) (the real-root case k = 0 of y² = (1−y)³), which turns Re Log(−y₃)² = log²y₃ − π² into (3/2)·log y₃·log(1−y₃) − π², i.e. into Rogers' correction term.

        Why record it. It identifies one of the three summands as a standard object with a known functional-equation theory, rather than an ad-hoc combination. It does not evaluate anything: L(y₃)/(π²/6) = 0.441033… is not rational, as expected for a cubic-field argument, so no ladder value drops out. The sum rule remains a statement about the combination, which is exactly what one expects if only the Bloch-group class is well defined.

        §7d — the distribution (Kubert) route: the algebraic skeleton #

        The header records the identified route to the missing second equation as the Kubert/distribution relation applied to Li₂(y²) = Li₂((1−y)³), and says it is "not formalized here". The algebraic half is formalized below, sorry-free; the analytic half — a complex Li₂ carrying the distribution identity — remains open, and nothing here closes the sum rule.

        The route runs through the cube root. If u³ + u² = 1 then y := u³ solves the saddle cubic and 1 − y = u², so the single parameter u presents y and its reflection simultaneously; the branch equation y² = (1−y)³ becomes u⁶ = u⁶ (branch_trivial_in_cubeRoot). That is the reason to prefer u, and it is why the distribution relation is the natural next input.

        A correction, recorded because the wrong version was drafted first. An earlier draft of this section claimed the distribution family is vacuous on the Galois sum — that summing the relation over the orbit degenerates to A = A, making the whole family a no-go. That claim is false. The reason it is false is exactly saddle_comp_cube_factor and reflected_comp_sq_factor below: X⁹ − 2X⁶ + 3X³ − 1 and X⁶ − X⁴ + 2X² − 1 are both reducible, so the Li-sum over the nine (resp. six) numbers splits into sums over smaller orbits instead of being one irreducibly new unknown. Both cubic factors X³ + X² − 1 and X³ − X² + 1 have discriminant −23 — the same field as y. So the distribution relation is genuinely informative; what it is not is sufficient, which is a different and much weaker statement.

        Numerics for the split, to 60 dps, are in computations/probes/qbeta_5two_route40_distribution_factors_probe.py. Bean qou-0yqu.

          §7h — the five-term (pentagon) identity: its derivative vanishes #

          The analytic input this whole lane is missing is the five-term identity for a complex Li₂. Its standard proof is two steps: differentiate in one variable and show the derivative vanishes identically, then pin the constant. Step one is algebra, and it is proved here.

          Writing L(z) = Li₂(z) + ½·log z·log(1−z) for the Rogers dilogarithm, L′(z) = −½·( log(1−z)/z + log z/(1−z) ). Differentiating the pentagon sum in x and expanding every log of a composite argument into the five basic ones —

          log a₃ = log(1−x) − log(1−xy),      log(1−a₃) = log x + log(1−y) − log(1−xy)
log a₄ = log(1−xy),                 log(1−a₄) = log x + log y
log a₅ = log(1−y) − log(1−xy),      log(1−a₅) = log y + log(1−x) − log(1−xy)

          — the coefficient of each of log x, log(1−x), log y, log(1−y), log(1−xy) vanishes separately as a rational function. That is the content below, with the five logs left as free variables so no branch choice is made.

          ⚠ This is not the five-term identity. Those expansions are exactly where flattening enters, so the branch bookkeeping of Routes 45–46 and Neumann's ker ν are the same difficulty showing up again. Nothing here discharges them.
