Bonds in hand, we assemble atoms into extended molecular structures. A **molecular Hopf chain** of length $N$ composes $N - 1$ covalent bonds along a backbone, giving a tridiagonal linking matrix with free endpoint sites (def: [molecular Hopf chain (open)](#def:molecular-hopf-chain)). The crucial step in passing to closed rings is to replace the naïve picture — $N$ pairwise Hopf links — with a single non-local invariant: the **threading link** $T(2,N)$ whose braid word $(\sigma_1 \cdots \sigma_{N-1})^2$ winds twice through every atom and whose Alexander polynomial $\Delta_{T(2,2m)}(q) = (q^m - q^{-m})/(q - q^{-1})$ records half the pairwise Alexander weight (def: [threading link $T(2,N)$](#def:threading-link)). A **molecular Hopf ring** is then $[\mathcal{A}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{A}_N] \oplus \mathcal{T}_N$ — atoms bound globally by the threading link rather than locally by Hopf links (def: [molecular Hopf ring (closed)](#def:molecular-hopf-ring)).

The remaining blocks read off the dynamical consequences. Open chains behave as **topological springs**: dangling endpoint trefoils support Reidemeister-I curl modes, backbone Reidemeister-II shifts propagate as phonons, and bending stiffness rises with bond order (rem: [spring-like modes of open Hopf chains](#rem:chain-spring-modes)). Ring closure, by contrast, is a **topological phase transition**: the $N - 1$ pairwise bonds must dissolve before the threading link can form, and the activation barrier sits at the transition state where neither encoding stabilises the system (prop: [ring-closure energy barrier from braid rearrangement](#prop:ring-closure-barrier)). The same proposition explains ring super-stability — opening a ring costs $(N-1) - \Delta_{T(2,N)}(q)$ in extra Alexander weight plus the full activation climb.
