The predicted stable neutron number $n_{\mathrm{pred}}(p)$ from
the beta-stability formula
([integer stability](#prop:integer-stability)) is an integer-valued
function of the substrate parameter $q$.  For each element $p$,
there is a range $[q_{\min}, q_{\max}]$ over which
$n_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is constant — the prediction is robust
against local variations in $q$.

**Observations.**

1. **Helium** ($p = 2$): $n_{\mathrm{pred}} = 2$ is stable over
   $q \in [1.001, 1.828]$ — a width of $0.83$.  The $\alpha$
   particle is robust against nearly any value of $q > 1$.

2. **Light elements** ($p \leq 6$): the $q$-stability window
   extends from $q = 1$ to well above $q = q_0$.  These nuclei
   would exist in *any* Descartes universe with $q > 1$.

3. **Medium elements** ($p = 10$--$26$): the window narrows to
   $\Delta q \approx 0.02$--$0.10$.  The prediction becomes
   sensitive to the precise value of $q$.

4. **Heavy elements** ($p > 50$): the window is
   $\Delta q < 0.01$.  A $1\%$ shift in $q$ changes the
   predicted stable isotope.

**Physical interpretation.**
The narrowing of the $q$-stability window with increasing $p$
reflects the growing Coulomb penalty: for heavy nuclei, the
balance between $a_C \cdot p^2/A^{1/3}$ (repulsion) and
$a_A \cdot (n - p)^2/A$ (asymmetry) is delicate, and small
changes in $q$ tip the balance.  Light nuclei, where the
Coulomb term is negligible, are insensitive to $q$.

This means the **periodic table is mostly determined by
$q > 1$** (that is, by the existence of crossing asymmetry),
not by the specific value $q_0$.  The fine structure —
which particular isotope is stable for each heavy element —
is the part that depends on the precise value of $q$.
