The sensitivity $\partial \ln \Gamma_\beta / \partial \ln q \approx 48$
([half-life regimes](#rem:hadron-half-life-regimes)) makes beta-decay
half-lives one of the most sensitive probes of the local substrate
parameter $q$.  Since $q$ is a field coupled to gauge-field curvature
via $q(p) = 1 + |\langle L_\varTheta, F_A^+\rangle|(p)$
([Levi-curvature coupling](#rem:c-substrate-via-levi)), any
environment with significant curvature or field strength shifts $q$
from its background value $q_0$
([local substrate $q$](#prop:local-substrate-q)) and produces a
measurable change in half-lives.

**Laboratory conditions.**
All six half-lives in the verification table
([verification](#ex:hadron-half-life-verification)) were measured in
standard laboratory environments: room temperature, $\sim 1$ atm,
Earth-surface gravity, negligible electromagnetic fields.  In these
conditions the gauge-field curvature $F_A^+$ is effectively zero
and $q = q_0$ to high precision.  The measurements are therefore
valid comparisons against the background-$q_0$ predictions.

**Quantitative prediction.**
A shift $\Delta q$ in the local substrate parameter changes the
beta-decay rate by

$$
  \frac{\Delta \Gamma_\beta}{\Gamma_\beta}
   \approx 
  48 \cdot \frac{\Delta q}{q_0}.
$$

Equivalently, a $1\%$ shift in $q$ changes the half-life by
$\sim 50\%$.  This amplification factor of $\sim 48$ makes
beta-decay timing competitive with atomic-clock precision for
detecting local $q$-gradients.

**Environments where $q$ is shifted.**

1. **Neutron star crusts** ($B \sim 10^{8}$--$10^{12}$ T,
   $\rho \sim 10^{14}$ kg/m${}^3$).  The extreme magnetic field
   and matter density produce large $|F_A^+|$ and hence
   $q \gg q_0$.  Beta-decay rates in the crust should be
   dramatically faster than terrestrial values.  This directly
   affects the r-process nucleosynthesis path: the waiting-point
   half-lives that determine abundance peaks (at $A \approx 80, 130, 195$) are set by the *local* $q$, not the laboratory
   $q_0$.  A shift of $\Delta q / q_0 \sim 1\%$ at the
   $A = 130$ waiting point changes the r-process yield by an
   order of magnitude.

2. **Heavy-ion collision fireballs** ($\sqrt{s_{NN}} \sim$
   GeV, lifetime $\sim 10^{-23}$ s).  The quark-gluon plasma
   creates a transient region of concentrated gauge-field curvature
   where $q$ is locally elevated.  While the fireball is too
   short-lived for direct half-life measurement, the modified $q$
   affects the relative yields of unstable nuclei produced in the
   hadronisation phase.  Anomalous isotope ratios at RHIC or
   ALICE could signal a shifted $q$ in the collision zone.

3. **Strong laboratory magnetic fields** ($B \sim 10$--$45$ T,
   pulsed to $\sim 10^3$ T).  The gauge-field curvature $F_A^+$
   couples to the external $B$-field through the instanton
   connection.  At $B \sim 45$ T (available in resistive magnets),
   the predicted shift is $\Delta q / q_0 \sim 10^{-8}$, giving
   $\Delta \Gamma / \Gamma \sim 5 \times 10^{-7}$.  This is below
   current half-life measurement precision ($\sim 10^{-4}$) but
   within reach of next-generation ion-trap experiments that
   monitor single-ion decays over months.

4. **Gravitational field gradients.**  The Levi form $L_\varTheta$
   couples $q$ to spacetime curvature.  Near the Earth's surface,
   the Schwarzschild curvature produces $\Delta q / q_0 \sim 10^{-9}$ per metre of altitude — far too small for direct
   detection, but relevant for precision comparisons of half-lives
   measured at different altitudes or on orbiting platforms.
   A space-based ${}^{60}\mathrm{Co}$ source compared with a
   terrestrial one would test this at the $10^{-7}$ level in
   $\Gamma$.

**Null result as confirmation.**
The observed constancy of laboratory half-lives across different
terrestrial locations, temperatures, and pressures is itself a
confirmation that $q$ is spatially uniform in the low-curvature
regime.  The Levi-curvature coupling predicts that $q$ should be
constant to $\sim 10^{-9}$ across the Earth's surface — consistent
with the experimental upper bound on half-life variation
($\lesssim 10^{-4}$, from ${}^{32}\mathrm{Si}$ and
${}^{226}\mathrm{Ra}$ monitoring over decades).

**Comparison with other $q$-probes.**
The muon-to-electron mass ratio that fixes $q_0$ is measured to
$10^{-8}$ precision but requires particle accelerators.  Beta-decay
half-lives, by contrast, can be measured with simple counting
experiments on gram-scale samples, making them practical probes for
field environments (inside magnets, at altitude, in space).  The
$\times 48$ amplification compensates for the lower intrinsic
precision of half-life timing versus mass spectrometry.
