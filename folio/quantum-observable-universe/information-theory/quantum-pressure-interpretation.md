Classical kinetic pressure arises from billiard-ball momentum transfer at a boundary. This classical derivation assumes point particles, elastic reflection, and a passive wall. All three assumptions fail in the QOU.

First, particles are knots rather than points. The configuration $K$ forms a braid closure strictly within the Reeb bundle $S^3 \to \mathcal{S}_p$. 

Second, the boundary formally participates in the Hecke algebra. Momentum transfer at $\partial U$ operates via the Frobenius pairing. A morphism $K$ approaching $\partial U$ pairs directly with the boundary datum via $\langle K|_{\partial U}\,,\, \partial U \rangle_A$, yielding a scalar in $\mathrm{End}_{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{1})$.

Third, the $q$-anomaly from $d_q^2 \neq 0$ renders the momentum transfer structurally inelastic. Each interaction with the boundary shifts the action by $\delta \mathfrak{S}_q \propto \hbar_q^2$. This shift contributes a direct, finite-size quantum correction to the pressure. 

Consequently, the boundary pressure $P_q^{(\mathrm{boundary})}$ has no classical analogue. It vanishes identically as $q \to 1$ (where $\hbar_q \to 0$) and drives the quantum corrections to the ideal gas law. 

Operationally, quantum pressure measures the rate at which the partition function scales under dilation of $U$. Expanding $U$ by $\delta V_q$ admits new knot configurations into $\mathrm{Conf}(U)$. As the Reeb bundle gains volume, geometrically longer braids become available. The pressure quantifies the structural information cost, measured in units of $T_q$, per unit volume of these newly available configurations.
