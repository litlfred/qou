**Mass splitting.**
The up and down quarks possess the same knot type and environment. The mass difference $\mu_{\mathfrak{d}} - \mu_{\mathfrak{u}}$ arises from crossing asymmetry. The Skein relation weights positive crossings by $q$ and negative crossings by $q^{-1}$. The reversed-orientation component therefore carries a distinct Skein resolution energy.

**Isolation.**
Like the up quark, the down quark cannot be isolated. It labels a Borromean component rather than a free particle.

**Orientation reversal.** The [down quark](#def:down-quark) differs from the up quark only by crossing orientation. At each crossing point within $\mathcal{B}_3$, the down quark strand passes under where the up quark passes over. This reversal contributes $q^{-1}$ instead of $q$ to the Skein polynomial.

**Note (charge).** The reversal is an exact mirror, formalized in the Lean library: the two orientations differ by a sign. The [Coulomb charge](#def:coulomb-charge) is odd under that mirror, so the mechanism gives $Q(\mathfrak{d}) = -Q(\mathfrak{u})$ — equal magnitude, opposite sign — for every crossing count. It does not give $-\tfrac{1}{3}$ against $+\tfrac{2}{3}$, whose ratio is $-2$; nor can it give thirds at all, since $Q \in \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}$. The sharper consequence is that a mirror-odd charge makes the neutron the proton's exact opposite, $Q(\mathfrak{n}) = -Q(\mathfrak{p})$, so demanding a neutral neutron forces every quark charge to vanish. The Standard Model assignment escapes this only because it takes $|Q_{\mathfrak{u}}| = 2|Q_{\mathfrak{d}}|$, an asymmetry between the two orientations that a pure mirror cannot supply.
