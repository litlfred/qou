Part 1 is the statement that a composite system carries no interaction of its own. Crossing $X \otimes Y$ past $Z$ is crossing $Y$ past $Z$, then crossing $X$ past $Z$. Iterating, every interaction among $n$ strands is a word in the elementary crossings, and each letter of that word touches exactly two strands.

The decomposition would be worthless if it depended on how the diagram was drawn. Part 2 removes that worry. A diagram in which three strands meet at a single point is degenerate; to read it one perturbs it, sliding the third strand either above or below the crossing of the other two. The two perturbations give different sequences of pairwise crossings. Part 2 says the sequences are equal. This is the Yang--Baxter equation, drawn as the Reidemeister move of type III.

Two familiar systems behave the same way. In an integrable scattering theory the three-body $S$-matrix factors into a product of two-body $S$-matrices, and the consistency condition on the two factorizations is again Yang--Baxter \cite{kauffman1991}. In a fluid, three vortex tubes driven onto a single point do not reconnect at once; the collision resolves into successive pairwise reconnections \cite{kleckner2013}.

None of this makes the many-body problem cheap. Part 3 is the price: bringing all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs of $n$ strands into contact costs $\binom{n}{2}$ elementary crossings. For three strands that is three, and two will not do — after two elementary crossings one of the three pairs has never been adjacent. The count is the categorical form of the observation that a three-particle bound state is not a pair of two-particle bound states. It also fixes the growth rate: the cost of a fully interacting configuration is quadratic in the number of strands rather than linear.

What the proposition buys is a change of bookkeeping. The many-body content of an interaction sits in the sequence of pairwise crossings rather than in an irreducible many-body vertex, so a dictionary assembled from two-particle crossings loses nothing. The typed crossing operator introduced later in this chapter is exactly such a two-strand datum, and the Borromean baryon is the three-strand word it generates.
