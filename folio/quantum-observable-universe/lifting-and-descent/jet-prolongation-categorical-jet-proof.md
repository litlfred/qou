**Proof.** Since $S$ is commutative, $m$ is an $R$-algebra map, so $I$ is an ideal and $I^2 \subseteq I$. Hence $m$ factors through the quotient and induces $r$. The section $\ell$ is the inclusion $a \mapsto a \otimes 1$ followed by the quotient map, so $r(\ell(a)) = m(a \otimes 1) = a$. For the kernel, write $j = [y]$ with $y \in S \otimes S$. Then $r(j) = m(y)$, so $j \in \ker r$ exactly when $y \in I$, that is, when $j$ lies in the image $I/I^2$. Every $j$ splits as $j = \ell(r(j)) + (j - \ell(r(j)))$ with the second term in $\ker r$, and the two pieces meet only in $0$ because $r \circ \ell = \mathrm{id}$. So $J^1(S) = \ell(S) \oplus I/I^2$, and $I/I^2 = \Omega^1_{S/R}$ is the module of Kähler differentials.

The earlier choice $J^1(A) := A \otimes \Omega^1(A)$ with $\Omega^1(A) = \ker m$ does not have $A$ as a summand: at $A = R$ the multiplication $R \otimes R \to R$ is an isomorphism, so $\Omega^1(R) = 0$ and no injective map $R \to R \otimes \Omega^1(R)$ exists.

No Frobenius or Hopf structure on $S$ is used. A Frobenius–Hopf structure on $S$ does not pass to $\Omega^1$ by restriction; see the [remark](#rem:jet-comul-does-not-restrict). $\square$
