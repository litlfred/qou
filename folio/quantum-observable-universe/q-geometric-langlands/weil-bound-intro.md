This section is where the book says what it can and cannot say about the Riemann hypothesis. The claims are of four kinds, and they are kept apart here because the surrounding exposition does not always keep them apart.

Throughout the section $q = |\mathbb{F}_q|$ is the size of a finite field, a power of a prime. It is not the aether parameter $q$ of the rest of the book, and nothing here takes a limit $q \to 1$.

**What is proved.** For a smooth projective curve of genus $g$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, Weil (1948) proved that the eigenvalues $\alpha_i$ of Frobenius on $H^1$ satisfy $|\alpha_i| = q^{1/2}$. The book does not reprove Weil's theorem. It carries his two inputs as named hypotheses, the functional-equation pairing $\alpha \mapsto q/\alpha$ and the Castelnuovo--Severi inequality $|\alpha|^2 \le q$, and derives from them in Lean that the inequality is an equality ([Hasse--Weil bound](#thm:hasse-weil-bound)), together with its rewriting $\mathrm{Re}(\rho_i) = 1/2$ under $\alpha_i = q^{\rho_i}$ ([spectral half-line](#cor:spectral-half-line)). The functional equation alone already gives the weaker $\sum_i \mathrm{Re}(\rho_i) = g$. Each of these Lean results has its prose statement in the block that carries it, against the same definitions.

**What can be checked prime by prime.** For Bring's curve $\mathcal{B}$ ($g = 4$) the eight eigenvalues are not arbitrary. Its Jacobian is isogenous to $E^4$ for one elliptic curve $E$ (Serre; [the Bring--Prym Jacobian](#rem:x5-bring-prym-jacobian)), and point counts identify $E$ with the curve $y^2 + xy + y = x^3 - x - 2$ (Cremona 50a1) of the [colour cubic](#prop:color-elliptic): for every prime $7 \le p \le 41$, $|\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{F}_p)| = p + 1 - 4a_p$ with $a_p = p + 1 - |E(\mathbb{F}_p)|$, and $|\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{F}_{49})| = 90$ as $(1 - 2T + 7T^2)^4$ predicts. So at a prime of good reduction the [local zeta function](#def:local-q-zeta-function) has numerator $(1 - a_p T + pT^2)^4$, and the finite-field Riemann hypothesis for $\mathcal{B}$ at $p$ is the single integer inequality $a_p^2 \le 4p$, which is Hasse's bound for $E$ (1933). At any given prime that is an exact finite check. In Lean, the data $(X^2 - a_pX + p)^4$ are shown to satisfy both Weil inputs, so $|\alpha_i| = p^{1/2}$, and $a_p^2 \le 4p$ is checked for $7 \le p \le 41$ (the integer checks in `QOU.BringsSurface.BringFrobeniusTraces`, their translation to absolute values in `QOU.Transcendental.BringsSurface.BringHasseLocalZeta`). That these data are the local zeta of Bring's curve rests on Weil's rationality theorem, the isogeny and the point count, which are cited, not formalised.

**What is open, and is a different problem.** Multiplying the local factors over all primes gives the zeta function of $\mathcal{B}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, which, up to finitely many Euler factors, is

$$
\zeta_{\mathcal{B}}(s) = \frac{\zeta(s)\,\zeta(s-1)}{L(E, s)^4}.
$$

The Frobenius eigenvalues build $L(E, s)$. The factor $\zeta(s)$ comes from $H^0$, which carries no eigenvalue but $1$. So the critical-line property at every prime is information about $L(E, s)$, whose own Riemann hypothesis ($\mathrm{Re}(s) = 1$ in this normalisation) is open, and it is no information about the zeros of $\zeta(s)$. It does not imply either statement: Hasse's bound holds at every prime and both remain open. The classical [Riemann hypothesis](#conj:classical-riemann-hypothesis) is stated in Lean over Mathlib's `riemannZeta`, faithfully and with no instance. The book assumes no proof of it.

**What is interpretation, not result.** Three readings in the nearby blocks are pictures, not theorems. That the zeros of $\zeta$ are eigenvalues of a Reeb operator $\xi_q$: no such operator is constructed. That the local alignment persists to $\zeta(s)$: the first form of the [adelic persistence conjecture](#conj:adelic-critical-line-persistence) said this, but by the factorisation above it names the wrong global object. The conjecture is now stated for $L(E,s)$, with the $\zeta$ form kept as a backup. And the [surreal energy bound](#rem:rh-surreal-bound): its inequality uses no property of $\zeta$, so it cannot tell the Riemann hypothesis from its negation.
