**Rigidity.** Rigidity of $\mathbf{C}$ is not required of a quantum universe. It is imposed on a [quantum observable universe](#def:quantum-observable-universe), where duals carry the antiparticles.

**On the ground ring.** $R$ is a commutative ring, not a field, and "invertible in $R$" in axiom 4 is strictly stronger than "nonzero" once $R$ has non-units. The two clauses coincide only over a field.

**On the substrate parameter.** The axioms ask of $R$ only that it be a commutative ring. The substrate parameter is emergent rather than carried: no $q$ appears among the data, the balancing twist determines it on the defining representation, and it lands in $\mathrm{End}(\mathbf{1}) \cong R$. The construction runs $R$, then the $R$-linear braided category, then the twist supplied by the tensor and braiding, and $q$ is read from that twist. The invertibility in axiom 4 is quantified over *this* universe's own blocks, so it constrains the pair $(R, \mathbf{C})$ and not $R$ alone. A ring therefore limits which decompositions it can carry rather than being admissible or not.

That limit is real. In $\mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}]$ the units are exactly $\pm q^{k}$, while each quantum integer is a monomial times a cyclotomic product of positive degree — $[2]_q = q^{-1}\Phi_4(q)$, $[3]_q = q^{-2}\Phi_3\Phi_6$, $[4]_q = q^{-3}\Phi_4\Phi_8$. No $[m]_q$ with $m \geq 2$ is a unit there, so over that ring the block index collapses to the trivial block: the axioms are satisfiable, but only degenerately, and none of the framework's Hecke decompositions fit. Inverting the cyclotomics is what makes a non-degenerate universe available:

$$
  R \;=\; \mathbb{Z}[q, q^{-1}]\bigl[\, \Phi_d(q)^{-1} \;:\; d \geq 2 \,\bigr],
$$

whose spectrum is the $q$-line with the roots of unity of order $\geq 2$ removed. Starting at $d = 1$ would additionally delete $q = 1$, which the classical limit needs, and nothing requires it: $q^{n} - 1 = (q-1)\,[n]_q$, so $q^n - 1$ is a unit exactly when $q - 1$ is, and the constructs inverting $q - 1$ live past the archimedean wall.

**This is a choice of instance, not a strengthening of the definition.** The property that $q$ and every $\Phi_d(q)$, $d \geq 2$, are invertible belongs to the *construction* of a Hecke-type universe, where it is what discharges the axiom-4 fields. It is deliberately not a hypothesis of the definition of a quantum universe, which asks of $R$ only that it be a commutative ring. The ring above is the initial object satisfying the property, so any other base with it — $\mathbb{Q}(q)$, a real algebra at the substrate anchor, a neighbouring convention's Laurent ring — receives a unique map *from* it and inherits the construction. The map runs outward; no universe is asked to embed in this ring, and injectivity is never required — the evaluation at $q = 1$ is not injective, and must not be.

The identification $R \cong \mathrm{End}_{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{1})$ in item 1 is a genuine hypothesis and not a consequence of $R$-linearity. Linearity supplies only the ring map $r \mapsto r \cdot \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf{1}}$; its bijectivity is the statement that $\mathbf{1}$ is simple, and it is what licenses reading the block data $\int_A p_\lambda$ and $\dim_q(\lambda)$ as elements of $R$ rather than as endomorphisms of the unit. It is stated here because the rest of the paper uses it silently — for instance in taking the substrate parameter $q \in \mathrm{End}(\mathbf{1})$.
