Why do the axioms of a physical theory land on a rigid braided monoidal category? Two lines of thought converge here. Composing physical processes in sequence and in parallel forces a monoidal structure. Superselection-sector analysis of local observables forces the same. Any compositional theory of physical systems settles into this categorical scaffold, which is fixed below before stating the quantum-universe axioms.

This section defines the two structures separately, because the paper uses them at two different levels. A quantum universe is braided: it has interactions. A quantum observable universe is braided *and* rigid: it also has duals, hence antiparticles and closed knots.

Two guiding examples remain in view throughout: the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[z]$ and the coordinate ring $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{SU}(2))$. Objects have tensor products and a unit over a base ring, making the category $R$-linear. In the rigid case every object $V$ has a dual $V^{\vee}$, and the zig-zag (snake) equations make $V^{\vee}$ a right dual. A braiding exchanges tensor factors. The symmetric case $c_{Y,X} \circ c_{X,Y} = \mathrm{id}$ marks the classical limit.
